AI platform helps scholars recover a lost hymn to Babylon

The AI-supported Electronic Babylonian Library platform at LMU Munich matched tablet fragments, which let two scholars edit a lost Hymn to Babylon from 21 manuscripts in 31 fragments.

  • 1 an organization

    The teams at LMU Munich and the University of Baghdad published a peer-reviewed edition. The sources describe no change to either institution.

  • 1 society

    The edition recovers one lost literary text, which the LMU press release calls a fascinating hymn describing Babylon and its inhabitants. In 2026 a scholar who was not an author used the hymn as evidence for Babylonian scribal education.

The system’s part
The platform's matching identified additional manuscripts of the hymn (30 per the LMU release; the edition uses 21 manuscripts in 31 fragments per the article). The edition, translation and interpretation were done by the two authors. The AI attribution comes from the LMU release, in which Jiménez speaks of “our AI-supported platform”. The article credits the eBL platform and team without mentioning artificial intelligence. The LMU release is vendor-authored, since LMU is the platform's developer, and ScienceAlert mainly relays that release and the paper. The peer-reviewed edition appeared in Iraq. Its first author Anmar A. Fadhil is at the University of Baghdad, with one developer co-author (Jiménez).
Limits
The sources give different counts: The edition uses 21 manuscripts in 31 fragments, LMU's press release reports 30 further manuscripts, and Archaeology Magazine reports 30 fragments from 20 tablets. The edition credits the platform without isolating its AI component. The statement that the work would formerly have taken decades comes from the developer.

What happened

Assyriologists Anmar A. Fadhil (University of Baghdad) and Enrique Jiménez (LMU Munich) edited a previously unknown Babylonian hymn that praises Babylon and its people. Their edition appeared online in the journal Iraq (volume 86) on 17 March 2025. The composition may have been about 250 lines long. It survives in no fewer than 20 manuscripts from the Sippar Library tablets, which date from the 7th to the 2nd/1st centuries BCE. The edition draws on the Electronic Babylonian Library (eBL), a fragment-matching platform that Jiménez's project at LMU Munich built. The article states that without the eBL platform the edition could not have used 21 manuscripts in 31 fragments. It credits the platform and its team and does not mention artificial intelligence. On 1 July 2025 LMU's press release said that the team's AI-supported platform had identified 30 further manuscripts belonging to the hymn. Jiménez said in the release that this process would formerly have taken decades. In December 2025 Archaeology Magazine, published by the Archaeological Institute of America, named the hymn one of its Top 10 Discoveries of 2025. Its writer reports that the team used AI models to search for overlaps and found 30 fragments from 20 tablets. On 24 September 2026 Shana Zaia of Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam used the edited hymn in an Iraq article on religious education in Babylonian scribal schools.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

The eBL platform matched related cuneiform passages across digitized fragments and helped locate additional manuscripts of the hymn.

Workflow

Digitized transliterations of cuneiform fragments in the eBL Fragmentarium. Algorithmic matching of sign sequences across fragments and collections. Philological edition and translation by Fadhil and Jiménez.

Human oversight

Fadhil and Jiménez evaluated the manuscripts and produced the edition, translation, and interpretation.

AI contribution

The Electronic Babylonian Library platform matched fragments and identified additional manuscripts of the hymn. The LMU press release and Archaeology Magazine credit artificial intelligence, and the edition in Iraq credits the platform and its team without mentioning artificial intelligence.
