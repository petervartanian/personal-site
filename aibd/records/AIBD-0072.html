What happened

Assyriologists Anmar A. Fadhil (University of Baghdad) and Enrique Jiménez (LMU Munich) published an edition of a previously unknown Babylonian hymn in praise of Babylon and its people in the journal Iraq (volume 86, online 17 March 2025). The composition, which may have been about 250 lines long, survives in no fewer than 20 manuscripts dating from the 7th to the 2nd/1st centuries BCE, found among the Sippar Library tablets. According to LMU's press release of 1 July 2025, the team's AI-supported Electronic Babylonian Library platform identified 30 further manuscripts belonging to the hymn, work that would formerly have taken decades; the article itself states that without the eBL platform the edition could not have used 21 manuscripts in 31 fragments.

What it was built to do. Match and join cuneiform fragments across collections to reconstruct Babylonian literary texts

The system's part

The platform's matching identified additional manuscripts of the hymn (30 per the LMU release; the edition uses 21 manuscripts in 31 fragments per the article); the edition, translation and interpretation were done by the two authors. The AI attribution comes from the LMU release, in which Jiménez speaks of “our AI-supported platform”; the article credits the eBL platform and team without mentioning artificial intelligence. The LMU release is vendor-authored, since LMU is the platform's developer, and ScienceAlert mainly relays that release and the paper; admission rests on the peer-reviewed edition in Iraq, whose first author Anmar A. Fadhil is at the University of Baghdad and which has one developer co-author (Jiménez), so it is not counted as vendor-authored.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. Digitized transliterations of cuneiform fragments in the eBL Fragmentarium. Algorithmic matching of sign sequences across fragments and collections. Philological edition and translation by Fadhil and Jiménez.

What must hold again. Fragment transliterations exist in the database. Overlapping text between manuscripts allows joins. Each proposed join is verified by Assyriologists.
