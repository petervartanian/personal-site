Brain implant lets a paralyzed man type 22 words a minute

A BrainGate2 implant with a recurrent neural network decoder turned two paralyzed men's attempted finger movements into typing, at up to 22 words per minute with a 1.6% word error rate.

  • 2 a person

    Two identifiable participants typed sentences of their own choosing at up to 22 words per minute. One of them has anarthria and only eye movement left. The typing happened only in research sessions. The paper lists independent use as future work, so the gain is a measurable improvement in a capability.

  • Not rated society

    The outcome is reported for two trial participants in research sessions. A typing rate faster than that of earlier decoders is a research result, and no source reports a realized change for society.

Depth runs from 1, a convenience or time saved, to 5, a life or a livelihood preserved.

The system’s part
The decoder is the component that makes typing possible. It is a recurrent neural network of five gated recurrent unit layers with 512 units each. It was trained with CTC loss on 20 ms bins of threshold crossings and spike-band power. It maps attempted finger movements to characters, and a 5-gram language model corrects them. Without it the arrays only record. The error rate figures are word error rates after the language model. Performance figures come from the developers' own peer-reviewed paper. The paper declares that the MGH Translational Research Center has research support agreements with several BCI companies, for which L.R.H. provides consultative input.
Limits
The headline speed and error rate refer to the faster participant and include language-model correction. Research sessions at home do not establish unsupervised daily use, sustained quality-of-life improvement, or equivalent performance in both participants.
Costs and harms
Communication was faster for T18 than for T17, and neural drift could reduce accuracy between calibrations. The benefit also required implanted electrodes and continued technical support.
Later
Nothing later is known. No development after the 16 March 2026 publication was found in the cited sources.

What happened

T17 and T18 took part in the BrainGate2 pilot trial (NCT00912041). T17 is a 33-year-old man with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, tetraplegia and anarthria. He depends on a ventilator. T18 is a 48-year-old man who had a cervical spinal cord injury, graded C4 ASIA A, about 18 months before enrollment. Each received six 64-electrode Utah arrays in precentral gyrus. A decoder of five gated recurrent unit layers, with a 5-gram language model, turned attempted finger movements into QWERTY keystrokes. It needed as few as 30 calibration sentences. Research sessions took place at each participant's place of residence, on trial days 44 to 144. T18 reached 110 characters (22 words) per minute with a 1.6% word error rate. T17 reached 47 characters per minute. Both typed sentences of their own choosing. The paper describes independent use as future work. The work was published in Nature Neuroscience on 16 March 2026. On 30 March 2026 the same team reported in Cell Reports that decoding of attempted speech in T17 was not accurate enough for effective communication. The authors write that they provided communication to T17 through the QWERTY typing interface.

Details

AI system

Model

The decoder used a five-layer gated recurrent unit network with 512 units per layer, trained with connectionist temporal classification loss.

Inputs and outputs

Implanted electrodes recorded neural activity during attempted finger movements. A recurrent neural network decoded characters, and a 5-gram language model helped select the resulting words.

Workflow

Six 64-electrode Utah arrays per participant placed in precentral gyrus after fMRI-guided parcellation, giving hand-motor electrodes for both hands. A recurrent neural network decoder trained with CTC loss on attempted finger movements, recalibrated from as few as 30 sentences, plus a 5-gram language model. A keyboard setup the participants already knew. Neither was a touch typist, and both learned the layout over sessions. A pilot-trial infrastructure that brings research staff and equipment to the participant's residence.

Human oversight

Participants chose what to type during research sessions. The investigators calibrated and evaluated the decoder; independent daily use was described as future work.

Relation to AI

The decoder is the component that makes typing possible. It is a recurrent neural network of five gated recurrent unit layers with 512 units each. Benefits

Beneficiaries

The demonstrated benefit was communication by T17 and T18. The results support feasibility for these participants, not an established success rate for everyone with paralysis.

Reach

Two participants, T17 and T18, used the system in research sessions. The sources report no wider use. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9]

Duration

Research sessions ran on trial days 44 to 144, and the paper lists independent use as future work. On 30 March 2026 the team reported that decoding of T17's attempted speech was not accurate enough for effective communication. The team said that it provided communication to T17 through the typing interface. The sources report no later follow-up of typing by either participant. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Use was confined to research sessions. The paper describes independent use through online decoder training as future work. Performance differed by participant. T18 reached 22 words per minute with word error below 7% across blocks. T17 reached 47 characters per minute with word error mostly below 15%. Invasive surgery is required. The device is investigational under an FDA investigational device exemption. The paper reports no clinical-trial safety outcomes.

Availability

This was an investigational BrainGate2 system used by two participants in research sessions, with independent use still under development.
