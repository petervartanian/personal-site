A brain–spine interface restored control of walking after paralysis

Gert-Jan Oskam used the STIMO-BSI interface to stand, walk, and climb stairs after a spinal cord injury.

  • 3 a person

    Life-changing benefit to one identifiable person: standing, walking, stair climbing and independent home use restored after ten years of paralysis. Some recovery persists when the device is switched off.

What happened

Gert-Jan Oskam received the STIMO-BSI brain–spine interface after an incomplete cervical spinal cord injury. The decoder translated signals from his brain into spinal stimulation. He regained control of standing and walking, including stairs and uneven ground. After 40 rehabilitation sessions, he could also walk more than 10 meters with crutches while the stimulation was off. The researchers reported stable control for over a year, including seven months of home use. An infection required removal of one cortical implant 167 days after implantation. Rehabilitation continued after treatment, and the implant was replaced in March 2023. The report concerns one participant. On 22 October 2026, Jiang and colleagues reported in Med that a brain-spine interface restored brain-controlled leg movement on the first day after surgery in three people with motor-complete spinal cord injury in China. Their system used depth electrodes in the motor cortex, and none of the authors belong to the Lausanne team.

Details

AI system

Model

REW-MSLM, a decoder that combines a hidden Markov model with multilinear regressions. [1]

Role

The decoder estimated intended leg movements and adjusted spinal stimulation. [1]

Configuration

WIMAGINE implants recorded brain activity. A portable computer controlled an ACTIVA RC pulse generator and a Specify 5-6-5 spinal lead. [1]

Inputs and outputs

Cortical signals entered the decoder. Its predictions set the timing and strength of spinal stimulation. [1]

Autonomy

Stimulation followed movement intentions within preset amplitude limits. The participant could start activities and adjust the permitted range. [1]

Workflow

Researchers calibrated the decoder, followed by 40 rehabilitation sessions and continued use at home. [1]

Human oversight

The participant and experimenter judged calibration before use. Physiotherapists supervised rehabilitation. [1]

Benefits

Beneficiaries

Gert-Jan Oskam, a participant with an incomplete cervical spinal cord injury. [1] [3]

Reach

The Lausanne study concerns one participant. On 22 January 2026 ONWARD reported that seven participants had received the later ARC-BCI system. Implant counts do not establish how many recipients recovered movement. On 22 October 2026 Jiang and colleagues reported that a separate trial in China restored brain-controlled leg movement in three people. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6]

Distribution and access

Access required surgery, personalized calibration, and rehabilitation within a clinical study. [1]

Duration

Control remained stable for over a year, including seven months of home use. [1]

Measurement

Measurement

Researchers recorded movements and muscle activity, performed clinical walking tests, and collected the participant’s reports. [1]

Setting and sample

One 38-year-old man at CHUV in Lausanne, ten years after an incomplete C5/C6 spinal cord injury. [1]

Measures

Walking distance and speed, balance, assistance required, muscle activity, and decoder accuracy. [1]

Results

After rehabilitation, the participant walked more than 10 meters with crutches and no stimulation. Brain-controlled stimulation also improved walking across uneven terrain. [1]

Additional value from AI

Brain-controlled stimulation produced more natural steps than stimulation triggered by motion sensors. The study did not compare different decoders. [1]

Limitations

The study involved one participant. Surgery, stimulation, and rehabilitation accompanied the decoder, so their individual contributions to recovery were not isolated. [1]

Denominators

One participant completed 40 rehabilitation sessions over 15 weeks, followed by seven months of home use. [1]

Materials for replication

The authors deposited source data and supporting MATLAB scripts in Zenodo. [1] [6]

People and places

Dependencies

The interface required cortical implants, a portable processor, wireless links, and an implanted spinal stimulator. [1]

Cross-border connections

The Swiss clinical team worked with CEA-Clinatec in France. Brain mapping took place in Grenoble, while surgery and rehabilitation took place in Lausanne. [1]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The approach requires viable spinal circuits below the injury, suitable brain signals, implanted devices, and personalized rehabilitation. [1]

Replication attempts

ONWARD reported further ARC-BCI implantations in 2024 and 2026. Those announcements did not establish recovery in each participant. [4] [5]

Availability

ARC-BCI remained investigational and unavailable for commercial use in ONWARD’s January 2026 announcement. [5]
