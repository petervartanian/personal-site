A brain–spine interface restored control of walking after paralysis

Gert-Jan Oskam used the STIMO-BSI interface to stand, walk, and climb stairs after a spinal cord injury.

ⓘ

AI system

System

STIMO-BSI brain–spine interface [1]

Model

REW-MSLM, a decoder that combines a hidden Markov model with multilinear regressions. [1]

Release

The decoder was calibrated for the participant. The paper names no software release. [1]

Application developer

NeuroRestore (EPFL/CHUV/UNIL) with CEA-Clinatec and ONWARD Medical

Role

The decoder estimated intended leg movements and adjusted spinal stimulation. [1]

Configuration

WIMAGINE implants recorded brain activity. A portable computer controlled an ACTIVA RC pulse generator and a Specify 5-6-5 spinal lead. [1]

Inputs and outputs

Cortical signals entered the decoder. Its predictions set the timing and strength of spinal stimulation. [1]

Autonomy

Stimulation followed movement intentions within preset amplitude limits. The participant could start activities and adjust the permitted range. [1]

Workflow

Researchers calibrated the decoder, followed by 40 rehabilitation sessions and continued use at home. [1]

Human oversight

The participant and experimenter judged calibration before use. Physiotherapists supervised rehabilitation. [1]

Relation to AI

The REW-MSLM decoder estimated intended leg movements from cortical signals and adjusted spinal stimulation. Researchers calibrated it for the participant and set the permitted stimulation ranges.

AI contribution

The REW-MSLM decoder estimated intended leg movements from cortical signals and adjusted spinal stimulation. Researchers calibrated it for the participant and set the permitted stimulation ranges. [1]

Costs and harms

An infection required removal of one cortical implant after 167 days. Rehabilitation resumed after treatment, and the implant was replaced in March 2023. [1]

Measurement

Studies

STIMO-BSI, NCT04632290. [1]

Study design

Researchers followed one participant and compared walking under different stimulation conditions. Six independent physiotherapists assessed gait videos without knowing the condition. [1]

Measurement

Researchers recorded movements and muscle activity, performed clinical walking tests, and collected the participant’s reports. [1]

Setting and sample

One 38-year-old man at CHUV in Lausanne, ten years after an incomplete C5/C6 spinal cord injury. [1]

Measures

Walking distance and speed, balance, assistance required, muscle activity, and decoder accuracy. [1]

Comparison

Walking with brain-controlled stimulation was compared with stimulation triggered by motion sensors and with no stimulation. [1]

Results

After rehabilitation, the participant walked more than 10 meters with crutches and no stimulation. Brain-controlled stimulation also improved walking across uneven terrain. [1]

Additional value from AI

Brain-controlled stimulation produced more natural steps than stimulation triggered by motion sensors. The study did not compare different decoders. [1]

Limitations

The study involved one participant. Surgery, stimulation, and rehabilitation accompanied the decoder, so their individual contributions to recovery were not isolated. [1]

Denominators

One participant completed 40 rehabilitation sessions over 15 weeks, followed by seven months of home use. [1]

Materials for replication

The authors deposited source data and supporting MATLAB scripts in Zenodo. [1] [6]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The approach requires viable spinal circuits below the injury, suitable brain signals, implanted devices, and personalized rehabilitation. [1]

Replication attempts

ONWARD reported further ARC-BCI implantations in 2024 and 2026. Those announcements did not establish recovery in each participant. [4] [5]

Later findings

By January 2026, ONWARD reported seven ARC-BCI participants. The later system paired WIMAGINE with ARC-IM. [4] [5]

Availability

ARC-BCI remained investigational and unavailable for commercial use in ONWARD’s January 2026 announcement. [5]
