What happened

ARMMAN's mMitra program sends automated voice messages to low-income pregnant women and new mothers in India, many of whom stop listening. With Google Research India and Harvard, ARMMAN tested a restless multi-armed bandit (RMAB) that selects which beneficiaries get limited staff service calls each week. In a seven-week field study starting 26 April 2021, 23,003 women were split into RMAB, round-robin and current-standard-of-care groups (7,668/7,668/7,667). Each week 225 RMAB-selected women were called. The RMAB group had 32.0% fewer cumulative engagement drops than standard care (p = 0.044). It had 28.3% fewer than round robin (p = 0.098, significant only at the 10% level). Round robin was not significantly better than standard care. ARMMAN reports the AI reduced drop-off risk by up to 32%. Google Research reports the system was deployed for weekly call scheduling.

What it was built to do. Allocate limited health-worker service calls to the beneficiaries whose program engagement they are most likely to preserve.

The system's part

The RMAB output chose which 225 women per week received ARMMAN-initiated service calls. ARMMAN staff blind to group made the calls. The peer-reviewed AAAI paper is by the developers with ARMMAN, so is marked vendor-authored. The non-vendor documentation is ARMMAN's own program page as the implementing non-governmental organization.

Replication

What produced it. RMAB Whittle-index policy over per-beneficiary engagement states. Historical engagement data for parameter inference via clustering. ARMMAN call-center capacity (fixed weekly call budget).

What must hold again. Engagement defined as listening at least 30 seconds to weekly messages. Call budget small relative to cohort. Beneficiary behavior patterns stable enough for transition estimates.
