Clinatec's ECoG decoder drives a four-limb exoskeleton

64.0% success using the avatar at home and 70.9% with the exoskeleton in the laboratory

A 28-year-old with C4-C5 tetraplegia cortically controlled simulated walking and 8-DoF arm movements over 24 months (Lancet Neurology 2019).

What happened

A 28-year-old man with tetraplegia after a C4-C5 spinal cord injury was implanted at the Clinatec research centre of the French Alternative Energies and Atomic Energy Commission (CEA), associated with Grenoble University Hospital, with two wireless epidural recorders of 64 electrodes each over the sensorimotor cortex. Epidural electrocorticographic signals were processed online by an adaptive decoding algorithm that sent commands to a virtual avatar or a four-limb exoskeleton. Between 12 June 2017 and 21 July 2019 he cortically controlled a programme that simulated walking and made bimanual upper-limb movements with eight degrees of freedom, with 64.0% success using the avatar at home and 70.9% with the exoskeleton in the laboratory; decoding models were reusable for about seven weeks without recalibration (Lancet Neurology, 2019).

What it was built to do. Restore mobility to people with tetraplegia by decoding brain signals to drive an exoskeleton

The system's part

The adaptive decoding algorithm (later described by the same group as a recursive exponentially weighted Markov-switching multilinear mixture-of-experts model) is the component that converts epidural ECoG into effector commands; without it the implants record only. The exoskeleton itself was ceiling-suspended in the laboratory.

Replication

What produced it

  • Chronic bilateral epidural ECoG recorders with wireless transmission
  • Adaptive, incrementally trained decoder with idle-state support
  • Progressive training on mental tasks over 24 months

What must hold again

  • Stable epidural signal quality over years
  • Decoder recalibration roughly every seven weeks
  • Laboratory suspension system for safe exoskeleton walking
