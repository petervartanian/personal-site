Decoder reads a paralyzed man's brain signals to move an exoskeleton

Clinatec's adaptive decoder turned brain signals from a man with tetraplegia into avatar and exoskeleton movements, with 64.0% success at home and 70.9% in the laboratory.

  • 2 a person

    One man with tetraplegia used his brain signals to control a walking simulation and arm movements with eight degrees of freedom, with the avatar at home and the exoskeleton in the laboratory. The control did not give him everyday mobility.

  • Not rated society

    The study involved one participant, and no source reports an effect across a population.

The system’s part
The adaptive decoding algorithm converts epidural ECoG into effector commands. The same group later described it as a recursive exponentially weighted Markov-switching multilinear mixture-of-experts model. Without it the implants record only. The exoskeleton itself was ceiling-suspended in the laboratory.
Limits
The exoskeleton was ceiling-supported in a laboratory, so controlled movements do not establish independent walking or daily mobility. Avatar and exoskeleton success rates concern different tasks and settings, and longer-term safety and home use remain open.

What happened

A 28-year-old man with tetraplegia after a C4-C5 spinal cord injury received two wireless epidural recorders of 64 electrodes each over the sensorimotor cortex. He was implanted at the Clinatec research center of the French Alternative Energies and Atomic Energy Commission (CEA), associated with Grenoble University Hospital. An adaptive decoding algorithm processed his epidural electrocorticographic signals online. It sent commands to a virtual avatar or a four-limb exoskeleton. Between 12 June 2017 and 21 July 2019 he used his brain signals to control a walking simulation and bimanual upper-limb movements with eight degrees of freedom. He achieved 64.0% success using the avatar at home and 70.9% with the exoskeleton in the laboratory. Decoding models were reusable for about seven weeks without recalibration, according to the Lancet Neurology paper of 3 October 2019. BBC News reported on the same day that the patient, Thibault, said that taking his first steps in the suit felt like being the first man on the Moon after two years without walking. The BBC noted that the suit was used only in the lab. Tom Shakespeare of the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine said in the story that the proof of concept was a long way from clinical use. In 2022 the same team reported in the Journal of Neural Engineering that the man was still controlling the exoskeleton bimanually more than three years after implantation. The decoder stayed stable for six months without recalibration. In 2023 a team at Lausanne University Hospital used two Clinatec WIMAGINE implants, the same kind of implant, in a brain-spine interface. It let a second man with tetraplegia stand and walk in community settings (Nature, 2023).

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

Wireless epidural electrodes recorded cortical activity, and an adaptive decoder translated it into commands for an avatar or an eight-degree-of-freedom exoskeleton.

Autonomy

The participant supplied intended movements, while the decoder controlled the avatar or powered exoskeleton. Laboratory walking required a ceiling suspension rather than independent balance.

Workflow

Chronic bilateral epidural ECoG recorders with wireless transmission. Adaptive, incrementally trained decoder with idle-state support. Progressive training on mental tasks over 24 months.

Human oversight

The exoskeleton was used only in the laboratory, suspended from the ceiling for safe walking. The decoder needed recalibration about every seven weeks in the 2019 study. [1] [2] [3]

Benefits

Reach

One man with tetraplegia used the system. He achieved 64.0% success using the avatar at home and 70.9% with the exoskeleton in the laboratory. [1] [2] [3]

Duration

The same team reported in 2022 that the man was still controlling the exoskeleton bimanually more than three years after implantation. The decoder stayed stable for six months without recalibration. [1] [2] [3]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Stable epidural signal quality over years. Decoder recalibration roughly every seven weeks. Laboratory suspension system for safe exoskeleton walking.
