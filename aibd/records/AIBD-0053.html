What happened

A 28-year-old man with tetraplegia after a C4-C5 spinal cord injury received two wireless epidural recorders of 64 electrodes each over the sensorimotor cortex. He was implanted at the Clinatec research center of the French Alternative Energies and Atomic Energy Commission (CEA), associated with Grenoble University Hospital. Epidural electrocorticographic signals were processed online by an adaptive decoding algorithm. It sent commands to a virtual avatar or a four-limb exoskeleton. Between 12 June 2017 and 21 July 2019 he cortically controlled a walking simulation and bimanual upper-limb movements with eight degrees of freedom. Success was 64.0% using the avatar at home and 70.9% with the exoskeleton in the laboratory. Decoding models were reusable for about seven weeks without recalibration (Lancet Neurology, 2019).

What it was built to do. Restore mobility to people with tetraplegia by decoding brain signals to drive an exoskeleton

The system's part

The adaptive decoding algorithm converts epidural ECoG into effector commands. The same group later described it as a recursive exponentially weighted Markov-switching multilinear mixture-of-experts model. Without it the implants record only. The exoskeleton itself was ceiling-suspended in the laboratory.

Replication

What produced it. Chronic bilateral epidural ECoG recorders with wireless transmission. Adaptive, incrementally trained decoder with idle-state support. Progressive training on mental tasks over 24 months.

What must hold again. Stable epidural signal quality over years. Decoder recalibration roughly every seven weeks. Laboratory suspension system for safe exoskeleton walking.
