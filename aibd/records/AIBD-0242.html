Self-driving cars have lower crash odds in most settings, higher at dusk

In a Nature Communications study, Level 4 automated driving systems from unnamed makers had lower crash odds than human drivers in most matched scenarios and higher odds at dawn, dusk and in turns.

What happened

Mohamed Abdel-Aty and Shengxuan Ding of the University of Central Florida's Smart and Safe Transportation Lab assembled 2,100 automated-vehicle crash records that run up to June 2023. The records came from the California Department of Motor Vehicles and from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's Standing General Order databases (1,099 SAE Level 4 automated driving systems, 1,001 Level 2 driver-assistance systems). The authors added 35,113 crashes of human-driven vehicles from California's Statewide Integrated Traffic Records System (SWITRS) and fitted a matched case-control logistic regression. The matched model uses 548 California Level 4 crashes. In rain, a Level 4 automated vehicle had 0.335 times the crash odds of a human-driven vehicle. Most pre-crash movements also reduced crash odds. At dawn or dusk the odds of a crash for a Level 4 automated vehicle were 5.250 times those of a human-driven vehicle, and in turning maneuvers they were 1.988 times. The authors attribute the advantages to object detection, precision control and decision-making. They attribute the disadvantages to sensor adaptation to changing light and to limited situational awareness. They name as a limit that they could not analyze which automation level or system was active in each crash. The analysis does not attribute vehicles to specific developers, so no individual maker is credited. The paper was received on 30 September 2023, accepted on 2 May 2024 and published in Nature Communications on 18 June 2024. In September 2025 a University of Wisconsin team reported in Accident Analysis and Prevention that automated-vehicle crashes were less severe and that intersections posed greater challenges for automated vehicles.

Details

AI system

Autonomy

The sample combined Level 4 automated driving and Level 2 assistance, which have different responsibilities for the human driver.

Workflow

Mandatory crash reporting for automated vehicles to the California DMV and under NHTSA's Standing General Order. A state police-reported crash database (SWITRS) with matching variables for road, environment and pre-crash movement. Matched case-control logistic regression across road, environment and crash type.

Benefits

Beneficiaries

Road users are the potential beneficiaries of better automated driving, but this study observed crash records rather than identified people whose crashes were prevented.

Reach

The study reports no count of people who benefited. Its figures count crash records: 2,100 automated-vehicle crashes, 35,113 human-driven crashes and 548 California Level 4 crashes in the matched model. [1] [2]

Measurement

Measurement

Matched case-control conditional logistic regression on police and regulator crash reports. Automated-vehicle crashes are the cases and human-driven crashes the controls, matched at the same location and road type, with road, environment, pre-crash movement, crash type and outcome as explanatory variables. [2]

Setting and sample

2,100 automated-vehicle crash records (California DMV and NHTSA Standing General Order; 1,099 SAE Level 4 and 1,001 Level 2) and 35,113 human-driven crashes from California's SWITRS, with automated-vehicle records up to June 2023. The matched model uses only California Level 4 crashes: 548 strata at a sampling ratio of 1 to 5. [2]

Measures

Odds ratio of a crash involving an automated driving system against a human-driven vehicle under a given condition. [2]

Results

Lower odds for automated driving systems in most matched scenarios, such as 0.335 in rain; higher odds at dawn or dusk (5.250) and when turning (1.988). [2]

Limitations

The data hold crashes only, so the odds compare crash conditions and do not give crash rates. The authors write that direct comparison of crash numbers is not viable because exposure and fleet size differ so much. They also could not analyze which automation level or system was active in a crash. [2]

Denominators

No vehicle miles traveled for automated vehicles; the authors add annual average daily traffic by road type for a descriptive comparison. [2]

Materials for replication

Public SWITRS human-driven crash data; supplementary information and source data with the article; code available from the authors on request. [2]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Continued regulator collection of ADS and ADAS crash reports. Comparable variable definitions between ADS reports and human crash records. Enough ADS crashes in each condition to estimate odds ratios.
