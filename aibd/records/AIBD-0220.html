Envision Glasses read signs and menus for a blind man in Calgary

Wes Ramage of Calgary, who is blind, uses Envision Glasses, a Google Glass headset with Envision's AI software, to read street signs and menus, find chairs and objects and describe scenes.

  • 3 a person

    Ramage, who is blind, now reads street signs, menus and scenes on his own and relies on the glasses each morning, which restores a capability that he had lost. He says that buses once drove past him at a stop and that the glasses bring a level of comfort that he had not had in years.

What happened

In a Globe and Mail report of 26 December 2023, Joe Castaldo described how Wes Ramage of Calgary, who is blind, uses Envision Glasses. They are a Google Glass headset with Envision's AI software. The Globe and Mail reports that Ramage first came across Envision in 2022 and that he serves as an unpaid beta tester for the company. He uses the glasses to describe scenes, identify clothing colors, find empty chairs and misplaced objects, read street signs when navigating transit and read menus. The article does not date the individual uses. Ramage said that the glasses bring a level of comfort he had not had in many years. He described heading out each morning with his glasses, keys and wallet. The article gives a price of US$1,899 to US$3,499 and reports that a provincial program covered part of his cost. It also profiles Nayla Farah of Toronto using the Be My AI app, covered by an existing record. Envision's product page says that the glasses are built on Google Glass Enterprise Edition 2. They offer Instant Text, Scan Text, Describe Scene and an Ask Envision feature on an OpenAI model. In a crossover study published on 8 January 2025, researchers at Lighthouse Guild and Amsterdam UMC found that Envision Glasses raised the odds that 25 people with vision loss could complete all six text tasks and that they read a street sign faster than without a device. Ramage was not part of that study.

Details

AI system

Model

GPT-4 for the Ask Envision feature.

Role

The glasses recognized text and described scenes aloud.

Configuration

Ramage used Envision software on a Google Glass Enterprise Edition 2 headset. The product combined text recognition and scene-description features.

Inputs and outputs

The glasses converted camera views into spoken readings of text and descriptions of scenes, colors and objects.

Autonomy

Ramage chose how to use the descriptions and readings while traveling and completing daily tasks. The device supplied information rather than controlling his movement.

Workflow

A head-worn camera with on-device OCR and cloud scene description. Funding through a provincial assistance program for a device priced at US$1,899 to US$3,499. A user who learned the environment and routines with the device.

Human oversight

The sources describe no human review of the glasses' readings. Ramage uses the descriptions himself, for example to make sure that he is in the right place for his bus. [1] [2] [3]

Benefits

Reach

One named beneficiary is reported: Wes Ramage of Calgary. The 2025 study tested the glasses with 25 people with vision loss outside this case. [1] [2] [3]

Duration

Ramage described using the glasses as part of his daily routine by December 2023, without a measured period of benefit.

Measurement

Measurement

Each participant did 14 timed and scored daily living tasks once without a device and once with each of four devices, in counterbalanced order over five clinic sessions; usability by the System Usability Scale. [3]

Setting and sample

25 adults with vision loss (acuity 20/100 to no light perception, mean age 64) from the Lighthouse Guild low-vision clinic, New York; no prior proficiency with the devices. [3]

Measures

Odds of completing each task relative to baseline; task time; System Usability Scale score. [3]

Results

With Envision Glasses, completion odds rose significantly for all six text tasks and for three of six searching and identifying tasks; street sign reading was significantly faster than baseline. [3]

Additional value from AI

The baseline had no AI device, so the measured gain is the uplift of the device itself. [3]

Limitations

Small sample in one clinic; single sessions after brief practice; participants were compensated; Envision's mean usability score was 62.5 and its median satisfaction 3 of 5; the glasses misread some text that participants corrected from context. [3]

Denominators

One session of about 90 minutes with each device. [3]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Affordability or subsidy for the hardware. Legible text and adequate lighting for OCR. Continued software support (the maker sells annual feature updates).

Replication attempts

1 follow-up entry. Benefit recurred: 1.
