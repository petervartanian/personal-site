What happened

In a Globe and Mail report of 26 December 2023, Joe Castaldo described how Wes Ramage of Calgary, who is blind, uses Envision Glasses, a Google Glass headset running Envision's AI software, to describe scenes, identify clothing colors, find empty chairs and misplaced objects, read street signs when navigating transit and read menus; Ramage said the glasses bring a level of comfort he had not had in many years and described heading out each morning with his glasses, keys and wallet. The article gives a price of US$1,899 to US$3,499, accessed in his case through a provincial assistance program, and also profiles Nayla Farah of Toronto using the Be My AI app, which is covered by an existing record. Envision's product page states that the glasses are built on Google Glass Enterprise Edition 2 and offer Instant Text, Scan Text, Describe Scene and an Ask Envision feature on an OpenAI model.

The date observed is the article's publication date; the article does not date the individual uses.

What it was built to do. Envision Glasses are built to speak out text and surroundings for blind and low-vision users; reading signs and menus is that purpose.

The system's part

The glasses' text recognition and scene description produced the readings Ramage relies on; the Globe article is the only outcome source and attributes the comfort to the glasses in the user's own words. No clinical or task measurement exists in the sources.

Replication

What produced it. A head-worn camera with on-device OCR and cloud scene description. Funding through a provincial assistance program for a device priced at US$1,899 to US$3,499. A user who learned the environment and routines with the device.

What must hold again. Affordability or subsidy for the hardware. Legible text and adequate lighting for OCR. Continued software support (the maker sells annual feature updates).
