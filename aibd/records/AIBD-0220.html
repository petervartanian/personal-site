Envision Glasses read street signs and menus for Wes Ramage

Globe and Mail, 26 December 2023: Wes Ramage of Calgary uses Envision Glasses (Google Glass plus Envision's AI) to read signs for transit, read menus, find chairs and objects and describe scenes.

ⓘ

What happened

In a Globe and Mail report of 26 December 2023, Joe Castaldo described how Wes Ramage of Calgary, who is blind, uses Envision Glasses. They are a Google Glass headset with Envision's AI software. He uses them to describe scenes, identify clothing colors, find empty chairs and misplaced objects, read street signs when navigating transit and read menus. Ramage said the glasses bring a level of comfort he had not had in many years. He described heading out each morning with his glasses, keys and wallet. The article gives a price of US$1,899 to US$3,499, accessed in his case through a provincial assistance program. It also profiles Nayla Farah of Toronto using the Be My AI app, covered by an existing record. Envision's product page says the glasses are built on Google Glass Enterprise Edition 2.

They offer Instant Text, Scan Text, Describe Scene and an Ask Envision feature on an OpenAI model. It does not date the individual uses.

Purpose. Envision Glasses are built to speak out text and surroundings for blind and low-vision users. Reading signs and menus is that purpose.

AI’s contribution

The glasses' text recognition and scene description produced the readings Ramage relies on. The Globe article is the only outcome source. It attributes the comfort to the glasses in the user's own words. No clinical or task measurement exists in the sources.

Further evidence

Conditions. Affordability or subsidy for the hardware. Legible text and adequate lighting for OCR. Continued software support (the maker sells annual feature updates).
