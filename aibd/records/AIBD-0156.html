What happened

On 14 January 2026 at about 3:10 p.m., an 85-year-old patient's heart rate climbed to 192 beats per minute. The patient was on a general ward of Gwangju Hanguk Hospital (광주한국병원) in Gwangju, South Korea. thynC had monitored the patient since 9 January because of cardiovascular risk factors at admission. thynC's remote central monitor flagged ventricular tachycardia, a precursor sign of cardiac arrest, and alarmed. Staff who ran to the room found seizure-like movements, reduced consciousness and no spontaneous breathing. They performed cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and artificial respiration. The heartbeat and circulation returned, and the patient was moved to intensive care. Daewoong Pharmaceutical, which supplies thynC, announced the case on 11 February 2026 with statements from the hospital's directors and nursing manager.

What it was built to do. The hospital and Daewoong Pharmaceutical describe thynC as an AI-based smart bed monitoring system. It measures electrocardiogram (ECG), heart rate and respiratory rate through wearable devices. It sends an alarm to a central monitor when an abnormal sign appears. So patients on a general ward, where staff cannot stay at every bedside, are watched in real time as in an intensive care unit. Staff can respond early to deterioration. Flagging a pre-arrest arrhythmia and summoning staff is exactly that design. Daewoong's hospital-division head describes thynC as built to recognize changes in a patient's condition early and help medical staff respond quickly.

The system's part

The system's contribution was detection and summons. The wearable ECG stream showed the heart rate climbing to 192 beats per minute. The remote central monitor identified ventricular tachycardia and raised an alarm. The attending director says that is how staff learned of it at once and reached the bedside to begin CPR within the golden time. The resuscitation itself was done by people. The counterfactual rests on the hospital's framing that general-ward patients are not under continuous observation and that deterioration can be noticed late. How long this patient would otherwise have gone unobserved is not recorded. No medical record or independent assessment has been published. The case reached the press through a Daewoong Pharmaceutical announcement of 11 February 2026.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. thynC wearable monitoring of ECG, heart rate and respiratory rate, linked to a remote central monitor on a general ward. It started on 9 January 2026 because the patient had cardiovascular risk factors at admission. An alarm raised the moment the heart rate climbed to 192 beats per minute and the monitor identified ventricular tachycardia. Ward staff who went to the bedside on the alarm and performed CPR and artificial respiration within the golden time. The reports put that time at four minutes for acute cardiac arrest, citing National Fire Agency data.

What must hold again. The case reached the press through a Daewoong Pharmaceutical announcement of 11 February 2026. The hospital's attending director, chief director and nursing manager are quoted. But no medical record, case report or independent review has been published. Only the return of heartbeat and circulation and the transfer to intensive care are reported. Survival to discharge and neurological outcome are not. The alarm shortens the time to detection, not the treatment. The benefit depends on staff who can reach the bedside and resuscitate within minutes. The nursing manager says that is hardest to guarantee at night and when staffing is thin.
