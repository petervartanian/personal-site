AI system

System

thynC (씽크) AI-based smart bed monitoring system: wearable sensors measuring ECG, heart rate and respiratory rate in real time, with alarms sent to a remote central monitor

Application developer

Not stated in the read reports, which describe thynC as the smart bed monitoring system that Daewoong Pharmaceutical supplies (자사 공급)

Workflow

The patient wore thynC monitors from 9 January 2026. The system alerted ward staff when the heart rate reached 192 beats per minute and identified ventricular tachycardia. Staff reached the bedside and performed CPR. The report describes that response as occurring within the time needed for resuscitation.

Human oversight

Hospital staff responded to the alarm and performed CPR.

Relation to AI

The system's contribution was detection and summons. The wearable ECG stream showed the heart rate climbing to 192 beats per minute. The remote central monitor identified ventricular tachycardia and raised an alarm. The attending director says that is how staff learned of it at once and reached the bedside to begin CPR within the golden time. The resuscitation itself was done by people. The counterfactual rests on the hospital's framing that general-ward patients are not under continuous observation and that deterioration can be noticed late. How long this patient would otherwise have gone unobserved is not recorded. No medical record or independent assessment has been published. The case reached the press through a Daewoong Pharmaceutical announcement of 11 February 2026.

AI contribution

The thynC system detected ventricular tachycardia in a wearable ECG feed and raised an alarm. The hospital reported that staff reached the bedside promptly and began CPR.

Entry details

Field

Healthcare (inpatient monitoring / cardiac arrest). Hospital general-ward remote patient monitoring.

Purpose

The hospital and Daewoong Pharmaceutical describe thynC as an AI-based smart bed monitoring system. It measures electrocardiogram (ECG), heart rate and respiratory rate through wearable devices. It sends an alarm to a central monitor when an abnormal sign appears. So patients on a general ward, where staff cannot stay at every bedside, are watched in real time as in an intensive care unit. Staff can respond early to deterioration. Flagging a pre-arrest arrhythmia and summoning staff is exactly that design. Daewoong's hospital-division head describes thynC as built to recognize changes in a patient's condition early and help medical staff respond quickly.
