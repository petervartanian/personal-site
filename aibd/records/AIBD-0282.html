AresGW finds eight new gravitational-wave candidates in LIGO O3

The deep-learning pipeline AresGW found eight new O3 candidates with p_astro > 0.5 (cumulative 5.94); an independent burst search detected one of them, and a later search recovered none.

  • 2 an organization

    The group's pipeline found eight new O3 candidates with p_astro above 0.5 beyond the catalogs that it compares against, a measurable gain in detection capability. An independent burst search detected one of them. An independent deep-learning search recovered none of the eight.

  • Not rated society

    If real, the candidates add to the binary black hole population; no catalog has adopted them in the sources.

What happened

The authors are Alexandra Koloniari, Evdokia Koursoumpa, Paraskevi Nousi, Paraskevas Lampropoulos, Nikolaos Passalis, Anastasios Tefas and Nikolaos Stergioulas. They are at Aristotle University of Thessaloniki and the Swiss Data Science Center (ETH Zurich). They posted “New Gravitational Wave Discoveries Enabled by Machine Learning” on arXiv on 10 July 2024. Their ResNet-based code AresGW, with a new hierarchical classification of triggers by noise and frequency filters, ran on 6.7 months of LIGO Hanford-Livingston O3 data. They write: “we identify eight new GW candidates in the O3 data, with p_astro > 0.5 and a cumulative astrophysical probability of 5.94. This is the first identification of new coincident events in GW searches with a network of detectors by a machine-learning-based algorithm.” In the arXiv table, GW190511_125545 has p_astro 1.00 and false alarm rate 0.27 per year. The others are GW190614_134749 (0.99), GW190607_083827 (0.99), GW190904_104631 (0.72), GW190523_085933 (0.68), GW200208_211609 (0.55), GW190705_164632 (0.51) and GW190426_082124 (0.50). The text and Figure 2 call the first event GW190511_135545. With them, AresGW's total within its effective training range reached 42 events, against 34 and 36 for the two IAS catalogs cited. Mishra and colleagues, who have no tie to AresGW, posted a burst search of O3 on arXiv on 19 October 2024. They published it in Physical Review D on 29 January 2025. Their search found a candidate at the same second as GW190607_083827, with p_astro 0.99, and their paper does not mention AresGW. The AresGW paper appeared open access in Machine Learning: Science and Technology 6(1) 015054 on 27 February 2025 (received 20 September 2024, accepted 13 February 2025). It reports no confirmation by other pipelines. In September 2025 the same group posted a follow-up on the model's sensitivity across month-long datasets. They write that “Our findings reveal notable performance variations”. On 19 December 2025 a Harvard University team with no tie to AresGW posted a convolutional network search of LIGO O1 to O3 data. They report lower false alarm rates than AresGW for most cataloged events, and they do not report the eight AresGW-only candidates. A University of Western Australia team published its own deep-learning search of O3 in Physical Review D on 22 July 2026. Their pipeline recovered none of the eight AresGW candidates, and they attribute most misses to their lower sensitivity at low chirp mass. They note that GW190607_083827 is supported by AresGW and by an independent burst search.

Details

AI system

Model

AresGW.

Role

The pipeline detected and ranked possible gravitational-wave events.

Configuration

AresGW used a hierarchy of classifiers to assess possible signals. The 2025 follow-up calls the model AresGW model 1.

Inputs and outputs

Public LIGO Hanford and Livingston recordings produced ranked candidate gravitational-wave events and estimated astrophysical probabilities.

Workflow

A ResNet trained on LIGO Hanford and Livingston data with hierarchical classification of triggers by noise and frequency filters. 6.7 months of coincident O3 data; significance from a logarithmic ranking statistic and injections into O3 data.

Human oversight

The authors estimated significance from injections into O3 data and call the eight events candidates. An independent burst search detected a candidate at the same second as GW190607_083827. An independent deep-learning search by a University of Western Australia team recovered none of the eight. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6]

Relation to AI

The deep-learning pipeline produced the candidate list and the ranking statistic. The authors computed p astro from injections into O3 data. The developers are the authors, and the only source for the discovery is their own peer-reviewed paper and preprint. The paper itself calls the events candidates and reports no external confirmation.

Benefits

Duration

The search covered 6.7 months of archived O3 data from LIGO Hanford and Livingston. In its search published on 22 July 2026, a University of Western Australia team recovered none of the eight candidates. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Sources within the effective training range (component masses 7 to 50 solar masses, chirp masses 10 to 40 solar masses). Two-detector coincidence; the network was not trained on Virgo data. A follow-up by the same group found sensitivity varies notably between month-long datasets, so detection counts may not transfer.
