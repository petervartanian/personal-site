STAR finds two motile sperm; couple conceives after 19 years

depth 3 for a person, depth 2 for an organization

found 7 sperm (2 motile) in 2.5m images of a 'sperm-free' sample

STAR AI found 7 sperm (2 motile) in 2.5m images of a 'sperm-free' sample; a couple infertile for 19 years conceived and had a girl.

What happened

At Columbia University Fertility Center in New York, a 39-year-old man with non-obstructive azoospermia and his 37-year-old wife, called 'Rosie' in TIME, had tried for 19 years, through 15 failed IVF cycles and two surgical sperm extractions. His 3.5 mL semen sample, read as sperm-free by manual examination, was run through STAR, which captured about 2.5 million images in roughly two hours and detected seven sperm, two of them motile. The two motile sperm were injected into eggs, two embryos were transferred on day 3, and a pregnancy test was positive 13 days later, in March 2025. The Lancet published the case on 31 October 2025; a girl was born in December 2025.

What it was built to do. STAR (Sperm Tracking and Recovery) was built over about five years at the Columbia University Fertility Center to find, and recover undamaged, the few sperm present in semen samples of men with azoospermia or cryptozoospermia so that they can be used for IVF by intracytoplasmic sperm injection; this pregnancy was exactly that intended use and its first clinical result.

Who benefited

    A healthy girl was born in December 2025 to a couple who had tried for 19 years through 15 failed IVF cycles and two surgeries, and whose husband had been told donor sperm or adoption were his only options (BBC, New York Post, DongA Science, NYT in syndication); a life-changing benefit to identifiable individuals, not a life-saving one, so -3 rather than -4.

The system's part

The detector flagged seven sperm, two motile, in roughly 2.5 million frames of a 3.5 mL sample that manual examination had called sperm-free, and the microfluidic recovery step delivered them intact; without a usable sperm the couple's remaining options were donor sperm or adoption. Everything after that was conventional IVF done by people: embryologists injected the two motile sperm into one fresh and one thawed egg, cultured two embryos and transferred them on day 3, and obstetric care followed. Limits on the attribution: the much-quoted comparison (44 sperm in an hour after two days of manual search) concerns a different sample; whether a longer manual search of this sample would have found the two sperm was not tested; and the letter's model version is not stated in any source read here (INCIID reports a YOLOv8 architecture, Columbia's patent filings describe YOLOv4).

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. A man with non-obstructive azoospermia whose 3.5 mL ejaculate still held a handful of sperm (seven, two motile) that manual microscopy and two surgical extractions had missed, rather than no sperm production at all. STAR's full pipeline on that sample: flow through a single-use microfluidic chip, a high-speed camera capturing about 2.5 million frames in roughly two hours, an AI detector flagging sperm among debris, and a gate or robotic step diverting each flagged sperm gently enough to leave it usable. A partner with mature eggs available (one fresh, one thawed) and an IVF laboratory able to inject the two motile sperm, culture two embryos and transfer them on day 3. About five years of in-house development at an academic fertility center staffed with embryologists, microfabrication, machine-learning and robotics specialists, and a couple who kept trying after 15 failed IVF cycles.

What must hold again. Some sperm must exist in the sample: STAR finds sperm, it does not create them, and it cannot help men who produce none (NYT in syndication; Williams in the Washington Post). Rates are low even at Columbia: about 28% of prior-azoospermia patients have sperm found, about 20% of mature eggs fertilize and about 18% of fertilized eggs become good-quality embryos (service page); across 175 cases sperm were recovered in 26% (NYT) to just under 30% (BBC). The evidence is one peer-reviewed case plus clinic-reported series: as of August 2026 no controlled trial and no published success rate (Medical Daily), and the ICSI pioneer Gianpiero Palermo warned the approach may sell false hope (Washington Post). Access is narrow: three systems at one New York clinic (NYT), $3,000 per analysis or $1,500 if nothing is found, not billable to insurance (service page).
