STAR finds two motile sperm; couple conceives after 19 years

STAR AI found 7 sperm (2 motile) in 2.5m images of a “sperm-free” sample; a couple infertile for 19 years conceived and had a girl.

AI system

System

STAR (Sperm Tracking and Recovery): a semen sample flows through a single-use microfluidic chip while a high-speed camera (about 300 frames per second, over 8 million images an hour) images it; an AI object-detection model flags sperm among cellular debris in real time and a microfluidic gate or robotic step diverts each flagged sperm into a droplet for immediate injection or freezing

Model

A detector from the YOLO family.

Editor’s note. INCIID names “YOLOv8 Architecture” (reference 25), whereas Columbia’s patent illustrates a “YOLOv4 object detection model” (reference 31, figures 4–5). Neither source identifies the version used for this pregnancy.

Release

Exact detector release not established.

Application developer

Columbia University, Columbia University Irving Medical Center (team led by Zev Williams and Hemant Suryawanshi)

Role

The detector located sperm in microscope images so that the retrieval system could recover them.

Workflow

STAR examined about 2.5 million images of a sample that manual examiners had considered sperm-free. It recovered seven sperm, two of them motile. Embryologists injected the two motile sperm into eggs, cultured two embryos, and transferred them on day three.

Human oversight

Embryologists injected the two motile sperm into eggs, cultured the embryos, and transferred them. Clinicians provided subsequent obstetric care.

Relation to AI

The detector flagged seven sperm, two motile, in roughly 2.5 million frames of a 3.5 mL sample that manual examination had called sperm-free. The microfluidic recovery step delivered them intact. Without a usable sperm the couple's remaining options were donor sperm or adoption. Everything after that was conventional IVF done by people. Embryologists injected the two motile sperm into one fresh and one thawed egg, cultured two embryos and transferred them on day 3. Obstetric care followed. Limits on the attribution: the much-quoted comparison (44 sperm in an hour after two days of manual search) concerns a different sample. Whether a longer manual search of this sample would have found the two sperm was not tested. The letter's model version is not stated in any source on record (INCIID reports a YOLOv8 architecture, Columbia's patent filings describe YOLOv4).

AI contribution

STAR detected seven sperm, including two motile sperm, in a sample that manual examination had classified as sperm-free. The system recovered them for use in IVF.

Measurement

Setting and sample

One 3.5 mL semen sample from a 39-year-old man with non-obstructive azoospermia.

Measures

Sperm recovered, sperm motility, pregnancy, and live birth.

Results

In this sample, STAR recovered seven sperm after manual examination had found none. News reports give sperm recovery in roughly 26% to 30% of a series of 175 cases. The clinic separately reports that about 20% of mature eggs fertilize and about 18% of fertilized eggs become good-quality embryos. These figures describe different stages, not an overall chance of a live birth.

Limitations

The comparison of 44 sperm found in an hour concerns another sample. This study did not test whether a longer manual search would have found the sperm. The evidence includes one peer-reviewed case and clinic-reported series, without a controlled trial or a published overall pregnancy success rate as of August 2026.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The sample must contain sperm. A fertility laboratory must be able to recover viable sperm and use them for intracytoplasmic sperm injection, then culture and transfer embryos.

Later findings

30 April 2026. By April 2026 the first STAR baby, a girl, had been born at the end of 2025, STAR was in regular use at Columbia with hundreds on the waiting list, sperm had been found in just under 30% of the latest 175 patients, and a second pregnancy (from testicular tissue, a boy due July 2026) was reported; Medical Daily (August 2026) notes that only one pregnancy has been published in peer-reviewed form.

Availability

As described in the sources, three STAR systems operated at one New York clinic. An analysis cost US$3,000, or US$1,500 if no sperm were found, and was not billable to insurance.

Reasons

The couple had a healthy daughter after 19 years of infertility, 15 failed IVF cycles, and two surgical sperm extractions. Columbia published the clinical result and introduced STAR as a paid service. The evidence establishes a major benefit for this couple, without establishing the treatment’s overall success rate.

Entry details

Field

Healthcare - reproductive medicine (IVF for non-obstructive azoospermia). Academic fertility clinic and IVF laboratory.

Purpose

STAR (Sperm Tracking and Recovery) was built over about five years at the Columbia University Fertility Center. It finds, and recovers undamaged, the few sperm in semen samples of men with azoospermia or cryptozoospermia. They can then be used for IVF by intracytoplasmic sperm injection. This pregnancy was exactly that intended use and its first clinical result.
