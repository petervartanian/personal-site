STAR finds two motile sperm, and a couple has a girl after 19 years

Columbia University's STAR system found seven sperm, two of them motile, in 2.5 million images of a “sperm-free” sample; a couple who had tried for 19 years then had a girl.

  • 3 a person

    A healthy girl was born in December 2025 to a couple who had tried for 19 years, through 15 failed in vitro fertilization cycles and two surgeries. The husband had been told that donor sperm or adoption were his only options (BBC, New York Post, DongA Science, The New York Times in syndication). The treatment enabled the couple to have a child after years of infertility.

  • 2 an organization

    The case became the clinic's published proof of STAR. The service launched in late 2025 at $3,000 per analysis. Three systems are in operation, and the waiting list runs to hundreds. TIME named STAR one of the Best Inventions of 2025 (Columbia service page, The New York Times in syndication, BBC, TIME). The gain is material for one institution.

Depth runs from 1, a convenience or time saved, to 5, a life or a livelihood preserved.

The system’s part
The detector flagged seven sperm, two motile, in roughly 2.5 million frames of a 3.5 mL sample that manual examination had called sperm-free. The microfluidic recovery step delivered them intact. Without a usable sperm the couple's remaining options were donor sperm or adoption. Everything after that was conventional IVF done by people. Embryologists injected the two motile sperm into one fresh and one thawed egg, cultured two embryos and transferred them on day 3. Obstetric care followed. Limits on the attribution: the much-quoted comparison (44 sperm in an hour after two days of manual search) concerns a different sample. Whether a longer manual search of this sample would have found the two sperm was not tested. The letter's model version is not stated in any source on record (INCIID reports a YOLOv8 architecture, Columbia's patent filings describe YOLOv4).
Limits
The reported pregnancy and birth followed sperm detection, recovery, and conventional IVF. One successful case cannot establish general effectiveness or that additional manual searching would have failed. The frequently repeated 44-sperm comparison concerns a different sample, and later birth reports must be distinguished from the original pregnancy report.
Costs and harms
STAR can help only when usable sperm remain. Finding sperm still leaves substantial uncertainty about fertilization and embryo development.
Later
Scaled up, 30 April 2026. By April 2026 the first STAR baby, a girl, had been born at the end of 2025, STAR was in regular use at Columbia with hundreds on the waiting list, sperm had been found in just under 30% of the latest 175 patients, and a second pregnancy (from testicular tissue, a boy due July 2026) was reported; Medical Daily (August 2026) notes that only one pregnancy has been published in peer-reviewed form.

Brief from Peter

Columbia University Fertility Center now charges $3,000 for a STAR analysis.1 The first pregnancy that the center reported with STAR came from a couple who had tried for 19 years to have a child. The husband, who had non-obstructive azoospermia, gave a sample that had been judged sperm-free on manual examination. STAR captured about 2.5 million images of it and detected seven sperm, two of them motile. Embryologists injected the two motile sperm into eggs, and the pregnancy test was positive in March 2025. A girl was born in December 2025. The evidence for the method is one peer-reviewed research letter, written by the team that built STAR, together with figures that the clinic reports about itself.2 In September 2026 Chia Lin Chang of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital in Taiwan, who declares no conflict of interest, concluded that the case shows that the method is technically feasible. Chang added that the case cannot count as evidence of clinical efficacy without data from a prospective series of cases.3 Nobody tested whether a longer manual search of the same sample would have found the two sperm, and STAR cannot help men who produce none.4

Details

AI system

Model

A detector from the YOLO family.

Editor’s note. INCIID names “YOLOv8 Architecture” (reference 25), whereas Columbia’s patent illustrates a “YOLOv4 object detection model” (reference 31, figures 4–5). Neither source identifies the version used for this pregnancy.

Role

The detector located sperm in microscope images so that the retrieval system could recover them.

Inputs and outputs

STAR analyzed successive microscopic images, detected candidate sperm, and directed their recovery into a small volume of fluid.

Workflow

A man with non-obstructive azoospermia whose 3.5 mL ejaculate still held a handful of sperm (seven, two motile). Manual microscopy and two surgical extractions had missed them. He did produce some sperm. STAR's full pipeline on that sample: flow through a single-use microfluidic chip and a high-speed camera capturing about 2.5 million frames in roughly two hours. An AI detector flagged sperm among debris. A gate or robotic step diverted each flagged sperm gently enough to leave it usable. A partner with mature eggs available (one fresh, one thawed). An IVF laboratory able to inject the two motile sperm, culture two embryos and transfer them on day 3. About five years of in-house development at an academic fertility center staffed with embryologists, microfabrication, machine-learning and robotics specialists. A couple who kept trying after 15 failed IVF cycles.

Human oversight

Embryologists selected recovered sperm for injection into eggs and performed the embryo culture and transfer.

AI contribution

STAR detected seven sperm, including two motile sperm, in a sample that manual examination had classified as sperm-free. The system recovered them for use in IVF.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Some sperm must exist in the sample. STAR finds sperm, it does not create them, and it cannot help men who produce none (NYT in syndication; Williams in the Washington Post). Rates are low even at Columbia. About 28% of prior-azoospermia patients have sperm found, about 20% of mature eggs fertilize and about 18% of fertilized eggs become good-quality embryos (service page). Across 175 cases sperm were recovered in 26% (NYT) to just under 30% (BBC). The evidence is one peer-reviewed case plus clinic-reported series. As of August 2026: no controlled trial and no published success rate (Medical Daily). ICSI pioneer Gianpiero Palermo warned the approach may sell false hope (Washington Post). Access is narrow. Three systems at one New York clinic (NYT), $3,000 per analysis or $1,500 if nothing is found, not billable to insurance (service page).

Entry details

Sector

Healthcare - reproductive medicine (IVF for non-obstructive azoospermia). Academic fertility clinic and IVF laboratory.

Purpose

STAR (Sperm Tracking and Recovery) was built over about five years at the Columbia University Fertility Center. It finds, and recovers undamaged, the few sperm in semen samples of men with azoospermia or cryptozoospermia. They can then be used for IVF by intracytoplasmic sperm injection. This pregnancy was exactly that intended use and its first clinical result.
