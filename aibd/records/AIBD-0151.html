What happened

At Columbia University Fertility Center in New York, a 39-year-old man with non-obstructive azoospermia and his 37-year-old wife had tried for 19 years. TIME calls her “Rosie”. That included 15 failed IVF cycles and two surgical sperm extractions. His 3.5 mL semen sample, read as sperm-free by manual examination, was run through STAR. It captured about 2.5 million images in roughly two hours and detected seven sperm, two motile. The two motile sperm were injected into eggs and two embryos were transferred on day 3. A pregnancy test was positive 13 days later, in March 2025. The Lancet published the case on 31 October 2025. A girl was born in December 2025.

What it was built to do. STAR (Sperm Tracking and Recovery) was built over about five years at the Columbia University Fertility Center. It finds, and recovers undamaged, the few sperm in semen samples of men with azoospermia or cryptozoospermia. They can then be used for IVF by intracytoplasmic sperm injection. This pregnancy was exactly that intended use and its first clinical result.

The system's part

The detector flagged seven sperm, two motile, in roughly 2.5 million frames of a 3.5 mL sample that manual examination had called sperm-free. The microfluidic recovery step delivered them intact. Without a usable sperm the couple's remaining options were donor sperm or adoption. Everything after that was conventional IVF done by people. Embryologists injected the two motile sperm into one fresh and one thawed egg, cultured two embryos and transferred them on day 3. Obstetric care followed. Limits on the attribution: the much-quoted comparison (44 sperm in an hour after two days of manual search) concerns a different sample. Whether a longer manual search of this sample would have found the two sperm was not tested. The letter's model version is not stated in any source on record (INCIID reports a YOLOv8 architecture, Columbia's patent filings describe YOLOv4).

Replication

No replication cited.

What produced it. A man with non-obstructive azoospermia whose 3.5 mL ejaculate still held a handful of sperm (seven, two motile). Manual microscopy and two surgical extractions had missed them. He did produce some sperm. STAR's full pipeline on that sample: flow through a single-use microfluidic chip and a high-speed camera capturing about 2.5 million frames in roughly two hours. An AI detector flagged sperm among debris. A gate or robotic step diverted each flagged sperm gently enough to leave it usable. A partner with mature eggs available (one fresh, one thawed). An IVF laboratory able to inject the two motile sperm, culture two embryos and transfer them on day 3. About five years of in-house development at an academic fertility center staffed with embryologists, microfabrication, machine-learning and robotics specialists. A couple who kept trying after 15 failed IVF cycles.

What must hold again. Some sperm must exist in the sample. STAR finds sperm, it does not create them, and it cannot help men who produce none (NYT in syndication; Williams in the Washington Post). Rates are low even at Columbia. About 28% of prior-azoospermia patients have sperm found, about 20% of mature eggs fertilize and about 18% of fertilized eggs become good-quality embryos (service page). Across 175 cases sperm were recovered in 26% (NYT) to just under 30% (BBC). The evidence is one peer-reviewed case plus clinic-reported series. As of August 2026: no controlled trial and no published success rate (Medical Daily). ICSI pioneer Gianpiero Palermo warned the approach may sell false hope (Washington Post). Access is narrow. Three systems at one New York clinic (NYT), $3,000 per analysis or $1,500 if nothing is found, not billable to insurance (service page).

    Replication
    Produced by
    • A man with non-obstructive azoospermia whose 3.5 mL ejaculate still held a handful of sperm (seven, two motile). Manual microscopy and two surgical extractions had missed them. He did produce some sperm.
    • STAR's full pipeline on that sample: flow through a single-use microfluidic chip and a high-speed camera capturing about 2.5 million frames in roughly two hours. An AI detector flagged sperm among debris. A gate or robotic step diverted each flagged sperm gently enough to leave it usable.
    • A partner with mature eggs available (one fresh, one thawed). An IVF laboratory able to inject the two motile sperm, culture two embryos and transfer them on day 3.
    • About five years of in-house development at an academic fertility center staffed with embryologists, microfabrication, machine-learning and robotics specialists. A couple who kept trying after 15 failed IVF cycles.
    Must hold
    • Some sperm must exist in the sample. STAR finds sperm, it does not create them, and it cannot help men who produce none (NYT in syndication; Williams in the Washington Post).
    • Rates are low even at Columbia. About 28% of prior-azoospermia patients have sperm found, about 20% of mature eggs fertilize and about 18% of fertilized eggs become good-quality embryos (service page). Across 175 cases sperm were recovered in 26% (NYT) to just under 30% (BBC).
    • The evidence is one peer-reviewed case plus clinic-reported series. As of August 2026: no controlled trial and no published success rate (Medical Daily). ICSI pioneer Gianpiero Palermo warned the approach may sell false hope (Washington Post).
    • Access is narrow. Three systems at one New York clinic (NYT), $3,000 per analysis or $1,500 if nothing is found, not billable to insurance (service page).
