Police phone app identifies man wanted since 2012 in Cardiff

South Wales Police's Operator Initiated Facial Recognition phone app matched a man in Cardiff to a European arrest warrant from Poland dating from 2012, and he was remanded in custody (July 2025).

The system’s part
The app compared a photograph taken on a police phone with a reference database and returned the match that identified the man. Officers then confirmed the warrant and detained him. In 2022 tests for the Metropolitan Police and South Wales Police, the National Physical Laboratory found that the NEC NeoFace V4 algorithm used for OIFR returned the correct match first for every probe image of a person in the database. NPL ran those tests offline, without officers taking the photographs or judging the matches. No source gives the match score in the Cardiff case.
Limits
No source gives the match score in the Cardiff case or confirms the identification independently. The National Physical Laboratory tested the algorithm offline, without officers taking the photographs or judging the matches, so its results do not show how the app performs in street use. No source reports the outcome of the extradition proceedings.
Costs and harms
The app can identify only people whose images are already in the reference database. The linked incident 1305 in the AI Incident Database reports that Home Office testing found higher false positive rates for Black and Asian people in facial recognition that UK police use.
Later
Persisted, 1 August 2026. A year after the Cardiff arrest, the ICO reported that OIFR was still a pilot in a limited number of forces in England and Wales. No source reports the outcome of the extradition proceedings.

What happened

South Wales Police and Gwent Police are the first UK forces with the Operator Initiated Facial Recognition (OIFR) app. It lets an officer photograph a person on a police phone and compare the image with a reference database. It is for when the person refuses or gives false details. In 2022 the National Physical Laboratory tested the NEC NeoFace V4 algorithm that the app uses, under contract to the Metropolitan Police and South Wales Police. The app launched in December 2024. In one use, an officer in Cardiff identified a 38-year-old man wanted on a European arrest warrant since 2012. The warrant was for drug dealing and assaulting a police officer with a weapon in Poland. He was remanded in custody to Westminster magistrates' court (Wales Online, 14 July 2025). The arrest date is not given. The forces report 50 South Wales searches producing 10 arrests, and six Gwent uses with three matches and one arrest. They cite the National Physical Laboratory testing, in which the correct match came top for people in the database (Wales Online). The accuracy claim rests on those 2022 tests. More than 500 officers had been trained by July 2025 (Emergency Services Times, 15 July 2025). In an audit report of August 2025, the Information Commissioner's Office said that it had observed the app at the two forces and called it a pilot in its infancy. In August 2026 the Information Commissioner's Office still described the app as a pilot in a limited number of forces. No source reports the outcome of the extradition proceedings.

Details

AI system

Reported configuration

NEC NeoFace V4 with the HD5 face detector (version tested by NPL in 2022)

Inputs and outputs

An officer took a photograph on a police phone and received candidate matches from a reference database.

Autonomy

An officer initiated the search, checked the warrant and decided on detention.

Workflow

A street encounter in which the person refuses or gives false details. A phone app linked to a reference database that holds the wanted person's image. A warrant record current enough for officers to act on the match.

Human oversight

An officer takes the photograph, and officers confirmed the warrant before they detained the man. Force policy allows use of the app only after an attempted engagement and where necessary and proportionate. The Information Commissioner's Office observed the pilot in 2025 and made no formal recommendations on it. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]

Benefits

Duration

The man was remanded in custody, and no source reports the outcome of the extradition proceedings. In August 2026 the Information Commissioner's Office described the app as still a pilot in a limited number of forces in England and Wales. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]

Measurement

Measurement

Offline batch searches of probe photographs of a volunteer cohort against a reference image database, using a program that NEC supplied to NPL's specification. [7]

Setting and sample

Volunteer cohort photographed on the Samsung XcoverPro phone model that South Wales Police used for OIFR, searched against a reference database of 178,400 images; tests run from September to November 2022. [7]

Measures

True positive identification rate at rank 1 (the share of searches for people in the database that return the right person first). [7]

Results

100 percent of OIFR probe images returned the correct reference at rank 1, with no difference across demographic groups. [7]

Limitations

Processing was retrospective and left out officers taking photographs and judging matches; the evaluator was under contract to the two forces; the test measures accuracy and says nothing about arrests. [7]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The app is used after an attempted engagement and only where necessary and proportionate, per the force's policy. The match is confirmed against the warrant before detention.
