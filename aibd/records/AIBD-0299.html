HEART-SAFE software detects a dying patient's cardiac arrest afterwards

Run afterwards on light-sensor data from a Samsung Watch5 Pro worn by a dying patient at Amsterdam UMC, the HEART-SAFE algorithm marked circulatory arrest in step with the electrocardiogram.

  • Not rated a person

    The patient died as expected under palliative withdrawal. The detection was retrospective and no alert, treatment or resuscitation followed.

  • Not rated society

    The authors present the case as a step toward real-world detection of unwitnessed arrests. No deployment or survival outcome exists.

Depth runs from 1, a convenience or time saved, to 5, a life or a livelihood preserved.

It marked the arrest when residual pulse amplitude fell below its thresholds, shortly before the monitor flatlined, with no false alarms. It was not running on the watch and triggered nothing. What happened

In a 2025 proof-of-principle study in Resuscitation, the HEART-SAFE group reported that its algorithm identified 28 of 31 circulatory arrests. The subjects were volunteers with cuff-restricted arm flow, cardioplegic surgery patients and slaughtered swine. Alerts were delivered to the HartslagNu test environment. Van den Beuken, Tuinman, Nideröst, Goossen, van Schuppen, Loer, Schwarte and Schober then reported a single case. They are of Amsterdam UMC with Utek B.V. and MiBida (the HEART-SAFE project's partners). Resuscitation Plus received the case report on 24 December 2025 and published it on 29 January 2026. Neither the report nor the journal dates the event. The authors call it the first detailed description of automated detection of a spontaneous, non-procedural cardiac arrest from consumer smartwatch data. An 82-year-old man with a right cerebellar hemorrhage and documented do-not-resuscitate directives was moved to palliative care. After about 42 hours life-sustaining treatment was withdrawn, and he died about 1.5 hours later. With the family's consent a Samsung Watch5 Pro was placed on his right wrist about 40 minutes after withdrawal. Electrocardiogram (ECG), invasive arterial pressure and clinical photoplethysmography (PPG) served as reference. The green-light PPG signal was later run through the group's previously described algorithm. It analyzes five-second windows using peak detection with minimum-amplitude safeguards. It flagged circulatory arrest when the ECG showed agonal bradycardia with the monitor reading 0/min and mean arterial pressure had fallen to 9 mmHg. Low-amplitude oscillations at about 30 per minute persisted at that point. It produced no false alarms over the observation period. The authors state that the algorithm was applied retrospectively. They say that they chose not to run it live on the device, because it is still in development and to avoid alarms disturbing the family. They state that the controlled end-of-life setting does not represent out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. Samsung is not an author. The authors describe the method as peak detection; they do not describe it as machine learning. In the accompanying editorial, Veigl and Billig, who have no stake in the project, restate the authors' finding without new data. On 24 July 2026 the Radboud DETECT group reported that its own wristband algorithm detected 23 of 23 arrests in a prospective test cohort in the same end-of-life setting. That study does not test the HEART-SAFE algorithm.

Details

AI system

Model

HEART-SAFE, a rule-based algorithm that compares pulse amplitude with thresholds.

Application developer

HEART-SAFE project (Amsterdam UMC with Utek B.V. And MiBida) for the algorithm; Samsung for the Watch5 Pro

Configuration

Researchers applied HEART-SAFE after the event to data from a Samsung Watch5 Pro. The algorithm did not run live or send an alert.

Inputs and outputs

Stored green-light pulse data were analyzed retrospectively with a peak-detection algorithm.

Autonomy

The algorithm did not run live or intervene in the patient’s palliative care.

Workflow

A consumer smartwatch collecting green-light PPG through the project's own data-collection software. Reference ECG, arterial pressure and clinical PPG to time the arrest. Retrospective application of the HEART-SAFE peak-detection algorithm with minimum-amplitude safeguards.

Human oversight

The algorithm ran only afterwards on recorded data, so no alert reached clinicians or the family. The authors chose not to run it live, partly to avoid alarms disturbing the family. The evaluation had institutional review board approval. [1] [2] [3]

Benefits

Beneficiaries

The work informed detector development and did not change this patient’s care or outcome.

Duration

The algorithm was applied once, afterwards, to one patient's recorded data. No HEART-SAFE paper or registered prospective HEART-SAFE study dated after the case report had appeared by 29 October 2026. [1] [2] [3]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

A live on-device implementation, which the authors deliberately did not use. Specificity high enough not to overburden emergency services, which the 2025 study says is still needed. Transfer from a slow, bradycardic end-of-life arrest to sudden arrhythmic out-of-hospital arrests, which the authors say the case does not represent.

2 follow-up entries. Benefit recurred: 2.
