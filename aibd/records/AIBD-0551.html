Kingston reports shorter social-care documentation with AI

Kingston reports that Magic Notes cuts documentation time by 50–60%, with supervision write-ups falling from 40 minutes to under 10.

    Reported reductions in case-note, assessment, and supervision writing are task-time savings.

  • 1 a person

    Workers report less documentation effort and time. Improvements in service users’ health or care are unmeasured.

The system’s part
Magic Notes transcribes and summarizes care and supervision conversations. Staff review, amend, and supplement outputs. The council attributes documentation savings to this assisted workflow, without establishing improved service-user outcomes.
Limits
The time savings need a defined sample and accounting for correction work. Poor audio and limited verbal communication constrain applicability, and the account does not establish improved care outcomes or equal accuracy across service users.
Costs and harms
The Ada Lovelace Institute reports that the work of reviewing AI outputs keeps some social workers from saving time and that AI errors risk entering care records. A fluent but inaccurate summary could make the worker’s checking task harder.
Later
Scaled up, 3 June 2026. Kingston phased the tool into all its adult social care teams in summer 2024. Achieving for Children later adopted it for the borough's children's services, and the council reported staff uptake of 75% to 80% in June 2026.

What happened

Kingston piloted Beam Magic Notes to transcribe and summarize adult-social-care conversations, assessments and supervision. Diginomica reported on 3 June 2026 that the pilot ran for four months from February 2024. The council reports 50–60% less time completing notes and assessments, and supervision write-ups falling from 40 minutes to under 10 minutes. The council's account reports limitations for people with low verbal skills or mental capacity and with poor audio quality. Diginomica reported that Kingston then rolled the tool out across its adult social care teams that summer. On 28 September 2024 The Guardian reported that Kingston was among seven councils using the tool. On 11 February 2026 the Ada Lovelace Institute published a study based on interviews with 39 social workers in 17 councils. The authors found that social workers report time savings from such tools but that the savings are uneven and that errors can enter care records. In June 2026 Diginomica reported that Achieving for Children had adopted the tool and that staff uptake stood at 75% to 80%.

Details

AI system

Reported configuration

Enterprise version of ChatGPT run inside Beam's environment

Inputs and outputs

Recorded care conversations become draft transcripts and assessments based on council templates. [1]

Workflow

The pilot began with a small social-worker group and expanded, using templates adapted with council feedback. The tool supports mobile recording and produces material for the existing case-management workflow.

Human oversight

Social workers edit the transcripts and summaries before using them in case records. [1]

Benefits

Reach

No source gives the number of staff who use the tool. In June 2026 Diginomica reported staff uptake of 75% to 80%. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Duration

Kingston rolled the tool out across its adult social care teams in summer 2024, and in June 2026 Diginomica reported staff uptake of 75% to 80%. No source gives dated follow-up of the time savings themselves. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Measurement

Measurement

Staff feedback on time saved, gathered by Beam and the council in a weekly digest.

Setting and sample

Kingston adult social care pilot that started with three social workers and grew to more than 10.

Measures

Time to complete case notes, assessments and supervision write-ups.

Results

Average time cut of 50% to 60% for notes and assessments; supervision write-ups fell from 40 minutes to under 10.

Limitations

Self-reported times with no control group and no published sample counts; the council reports poor transcripts for people with low verbal skills or limited capacity and for poor audio.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Practitioners are expected to obtain participants’ consent for recording. The source reports that poor audio and limited verbal detail can reduce transcript quality, and staff retain responsibility for editing outputs.
