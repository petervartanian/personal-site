Tutor CoPilot helps tutors raise students' mastery of math topics

Tutor CoPilot, a Stanford system built on GPT-4 that suggests moves to human tutors, made students 4 percentage points more likely to master topics, with no significant gain on end-of-year tests.

  • 2 a person

    Students of tutors with the tool were 4 percentage points more likely to pass session exit tickets (62% to 66%), and 9 points for lower-rated tutors. The authors found no significant gain on end-of-year tests.

  • 2 an organization

    The provider's tutors with the tool prompted students to explain more often and gave generic praise less often, at about $20 per tutor per year.

Depth runs from 1, a convenience or time saved, to 5, a life or a livelihood preserved.

The system’s part
Tutors were randomized to have access to the tool or not, so the 4-point gain measures the effect of access. Among sessions where tutors used it, the authors estimate a 14-point gain. The model suggested moves to the human tutor, and the tutor chose what to say to the student.
Limits
The gain was measured on session exit tickets, and the authors found no significant gain on end-of-year math tests. Outside researchers quoted by The 74 and MIT Technology Review cautioned that the mathematics was basic. No independent study of the tool is cited.

What happened

Wang, Ribeiro, Robinson, Loeb and Demszky of Stanford built Tutor CoPilot, a language-model system that gives human tutors expert-like suggestions during live sessions. They tested it with FEV Tutor, a virtual tutoring provider, and a large southern U.S. school district serving over 30,000 students. Nine schools took part. The district identified 1,787 students in grades 3–8, 80% Hispanic, who had performed below grade level. The preprint (arXiv, 3 October 2024) says that the study started at the end of March 2024 and lasted two months. Version 3 of the preprint (10 October 2026) dates the trial from 27 March to 17 May 2024. The 900 tutors were evenly split between treatment and control. Students of treated tutors were 4 percentage points more likely to master topics. Students of lower-rated tutors gained 9 points. The authors put the cost at about $20 per tutor per year. The 74 (7 October 2024) and MIT Technology Review (28 October 2024) reported that the system runs on OpenAI's GPT-4. They gave pass rates of 66% versus 62% on exit assessments and quoted outside researchers' caveats about the basic level of the mathematics. Education Week (31 October 2024), quoting co-author Susanna Loeb, reported that tutors using the tool were more likely to prompt students to explain. They were less likely to give away solutions. The 74 reported on 29 January 2025 that FEV Tutor, the provider that ran the trial, had shut down on 25 January 2025 after failing to raise money or find a buyer. In version 3 of the preprint, the authors report that they found no significant gain on end-of-year math tests.

Details

AI system

Model

GPT-4. [1] [2]

Provider

Stanford researchers developed Tutor CoPilot with FEV Tutor. OpenAI supplied GPT-4. [1] [2]

Role

GPT-4 proposed responses that human tutors could use during lessons. [1] [2]

Configuration

Bridge supplied teaching strategies. The system removed names and sent at most the 10 latest chat messages, the lesson topic, and the requested strategy. [1] [2]

Inputs and outputs

The model used recent messages, the lesson topic, and a teaching strategy to suggest a tutor response. [1] [2]

Autonomy

Tutors chose, edited, regenerated, or ignored the suggestions before responding to students. [1] [2]

Workflow

A language model prompted with a taxonomy of expert tutoring moves rather than answers. Integration into a live virtual-tutoring platform where tutors can request suggestions mid-session. Tutor-level randomization (900 tutors) with preregistered analysis.

Human oversight

Tutors could edit, regenerate or ignore suggestions before responding to students.

Benefits

Reach

The trial covered 1,787 students in grades 3 to 8 at nine schools. Their 900 tutors were split evenly between treatment and control, and the gain applies to the students of treated tutors. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]

Distribution and access

The measured gain was larger for students taught by lower-rated tutors: 9 percentage points versus a 4-point average.

Duration

The trial ran from 27 March to 17 May 2024. The authors found no significant gain on end-of-year math tests. FEV Tutor, the provider that ran the trial, shut down on 25 January 2025. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]

Measurement

Measurement

Session exit-ticket results from the tutoring platform; end-of-year NWEA MAP math scores were the initial preregistered outcome; classifiers applied to more than 350,000 chat messages. [7]

Setting and sample

900 tutors at FEV Tutor randomized 450 to each arm, of whom 783 tutored students in the study; 1,013 students in grades 3 to 6 from nine schools of a large southern U.S. District; 27 March to 17 May 2024. [7]

Measures

Share of sessions in which the student passed the exit ticket. [7]

Results

4 percentage points higher (62% to 66%); 9 points for students of lower-rated tutors. [7]

Additional value from AI

Intent-to-treat effect of 4 percentage points; 14 points estimated for sessions in which the tool was used. [7]

Limitations

No significant gain on end-of-year test scores; seven weeks of exposure; tutors were randomized while students were not; the authors built the tool and wrote the study; the work remains a preprint. [7]

Denominators

About seven weeks of access during live chat sessions. [7]

Materials for replication

Preregistration on the Open Science Framework and a demonstration notebook on GitHub. [7]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Human tutors willing to use suggestions in real time. Basic-level mathematics content (outside researchers' caveat). Students below grade level in under-served schools, the population studied.
