Upstart lends to more people with low credit scores, at lower rates

Upstart's machine-learning lending model approved 15 to 30 percent of low-score applicants that traditional models reject, at lower rates; a 2024 monitor found disparities for Black applicants.

  • 2 a person

    Applicants with low credit scores whom a traditional model would reject got loans at lower rates. The authors of a 2026 peer-reviewed study report that their later finances improved. The effects are measured across Upstart's applicants as a group.

  • Not rated society

    Sources describe aggregate access effects and later disparities; no societal outcome is measured.

The system’s part
The CFPB states that the 2019 gains reflect alternative data and machine learning together. The 2026 Management Science study separates the two. Its authors report that alternative data drove most gains for borrowers with thin credit files and low scores, and that the more complex model added gains where credit reports held more information. Upstart built and ran the model and supplied the data for both analyses.
Limits
The bureau did not replicate Upstart's 2019 analysis. The bureau's post gives no approval rate for the traditional model. No cited source measures a societal outcome.
Costs and harms
In 2024 the independent monitor found statistically and practically significant approval disparities for Black applicants. In February 2020 the Student Borrower Protection Center reported that Upstart quoted a hypothetical Howard University graduate nearly $3,499 more over a five-year loan than a similar New York University graduate. Upstart rejected the monitor's proposed less discriminatory alternative model.

Challenged, 27 March 2024. The independent monitor found approval disparities for Black applicants and proposed a less discriminatory alternative model. Upstart and the monitor did not agree on adopting it. What happened

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau granted its first no-action letter to Upstart Network in September 2017. On 6 August 2019 Patrice Alexander Ficklin and Paul Watkins reported in a bureau blog post on results from the compliance plan attached to that letter. Upstart compared its tested model with a hypothetical traditional model using conventional variables. It reported that the tested model approves 27% more applicants and yields 16% lower average annual percentage rates for approved loans. Acceptance rates were up 23–29% and annual percentage rates down 15–17% across racial, ethnic and sex categories. Near-prime applicants (FICO 620–660) were approved approximately twice as frequently. Applicants under 25 were 32% more likely to be approved and those with incomes under $50,000 13% more likely. The post states that the results for minority, female and 62-and-older applicants show no disparities requiring further fair-lending analysis. The post says that the results reflect the net effect of both the alternative data and the machine-learning methodology. Upstart supplied the analysis. The bureau stated that it did not separately replicate it. In February 2020 the Student Borrower Protection Center reported that Upstart quoted a hypothetical Howard University graduate nearly $3,499 more over a five-year refinancing loan than a similar New York University graduate. On 8 June 2022 the bureau terminated the 2020 no-action letter at Upstart's request after Upstart planned to expand the variables in its model. On 27 March 2024 the NAACP Legal Defense Fund and the Student Borrower Protection Center announced the final report of Relman Colfax, the independent monitor. The report identified approval disparities for Black applicants. It also found some disparities in annual percentage rates for Black, Hispanic and female applicants, but none exceeded its thresholds for practical significance. Upstart rejected the monitor's proposed less discriminatory alternative model. On 13 May 2026 Marco Di Maggio and Dimuthu Ratnadiwakara reported in Management Science that Upstart approves 15 to 30 percent of low-score applicants rejected by traditional models, at lower rates. They found that alternative data drove most of the gains for borrowers with thin credit files and low scores, and that wider access improved borrowers' later finances.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

Upstart combined conventional credit information with alternative applicant data to generate approval and pricing decisions in its underwriting comparison.

Workflow

Alternative data combined with machine-learning underwriting, compared against a hypothetical traditional model on Upstart's own applicant pool. A regulatory compliance plan requiring fair-lending testing and reporting.

Human oversight

The sources do not describe human review of individual loan decisions. A compliance plan under the bureau's no-action letter required fair-lending testing. Relman Colfax later served as an independent monitor and found approval disparities for Black applicants. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Benefits

Reach

The record gives no count of borrowers who benefited. The 2026 study reports a share: Upstart approves 15 to 30 percent of low-score applicants that traditional models reject. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Distribution and access

The 2019 simulation reported especially large approval gains for near-prime and younger applicants. Later monitoring identified approval disparities for Black applicants and some pricing disparities that did not exceed the monitor’s practical-significance thresholds.

Duration

The 2019 figures come from a single comparison against a hypothetical model. The authors of the 2026 Management Science study report that wider access improved borrowers' later finances. The record gives no period over which that improvement was measured. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Measurement

Measurement

Anonymized lending data from Upstart, with approvals compared with those of traditional models; later outcomes estimated from exogenous variation in credit access. [4]

Setting and sample

Applicants to Upstart, a U.S. Fintech lender of unsecured personal loans. [4]

Measures

Approval of low-credit-score applicants, offered rates, later financial outcomes. [4]

Results

The platform approves 15 to 30 percent of low-credit-score applicants that traditional models reject and offers them lower rates. [4]

Additional value from AI

Alternative data drives most gains for thin-file borrowers; model sophistication adds gains where credit files are fuller. [4]

Limitations

Data from one lender, supplied by that lender. [4]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

An applicant pool and comparison model similar to the 2019 test. Fair-lending testing that measures approval as well as pricing disparities. Independent monitoring, which in 2024 found approval disparities the 2019 test did not report.
