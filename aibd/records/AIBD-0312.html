What happened

The Arizona Daily Sun (staff report, 26 March 2026) reports that a small fire later named the Diamond Fire began shortly after 2 p.m. on Thursday, 26 March 2026. It started just north of Lake Mary Road, about three miles southeast of the intersection of Lake Mary Road and Beulah Boulevard. That is south of Pine Canyon Golf Club and east of Flagstaff Pulliam Airport. It reached seven acres by 3:16 p.m., according to WatchDuty. Its forward progress was stopped at 3:37 p.m., about 80 minutes after the first report, and it was 100% contained at 6:09 p.m. The Daily Sun does not mention a camera, the Coconino National Forest or who made the first report. The interagency wildfire record (2026-AZCOF-000289) places the Diamond Fire on the Coconino National Forest. It gives discovery at 2:15 p.m. Mountain Standard Time on 26 March 2026 at a tenth of an acre. It also gives a final size of 7 acres, a human cause and containment at 6:08 p.m. It does not say who detected the fire. No official incident record naming the camera was found; InciWeb's “Diamond Fire” is a June 2023 Scottsdale fire. The Associated Press reported on 4 May 2026 that artificial intelligence had detected something resembling smoke on a camera feed from Arizona's Coconino National Forest on a March afternoon. The report is by Dorany Pineda and Brittany Peterson, on record in the KPBS and Courthouse News republications. One of dozens of AI cameras installed for the utility Arizona Public Service had spotted early signs of the fire that came to be known as the Diamond Fire. The Associated Press says that human analysts verified the camera's detection before they alerted the state forest service and Arizona Public Service. Firefighters raced to the scene and contained it before it grew past 7 acres (2.8 hectares). The Daily Sun and Associated Press accounts are matched by the fire's name, month and size. The Associated Press says that Arizona Public Service has nearly 40 active AI smoke-detection cameras and plans 71 by summer's end. Cindy Kobold, a meteorologist at Arizona Public Service, says that the technology notifies the utility about 45 minutes faster on average than the first 911 call. Pano AI, launched in 2020 and charging around $50,000 a year per camera, counts Arizona Public Service among its customers. It says that it detected 725 wildfires in the United States in the previous year. The Associated Press does not state outright which company made the camera that spotted the Diamond Fire.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

The system flagged smoke-like imagery in a camera feed from Coconino National Forest for human verification.

Workflow

An AI smoke-detection camera network installed for a utility covering national-forest land near Flagstaff. Human review of the AI detection, then notification to the utility and fire agencies. AP says human intervention keeps false positives low. Crews able to reach a small fire on a March afternoon within about 80 minutes of the first report.

Human oversight

Human analysts checked that the suspected smoke was not a cloud or dust, then alerted the state forest service and Arizona Public Service. Firefighters handled containment.

Relation to AI

AP reports that the camera's AI flagged something resembling smoke. It reports that human analysts then checked that it was not cloud or dust and alerted the state forest service and APS. The interagency record gives discovery at a tenth of an acre at 2:15 p.m. But does not name the source of the first report. Firefighters did the containment.
