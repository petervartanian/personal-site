Global Fishing Watch helps Kiribati fine a vessel US$1 million

Kiribati used the Google-Oceana-SkyTruth platform to document Marshalls 203 fishing illegally in PIPA; a US$1 million fine plus a US$1 million goodwill grant followed (reported September 2016).

ⓘ

What happened

Global Fishing Watch, developed by Oceana, SkyTruth and Google, launched publicly at the Our Ocean conference on 15 September 2016. It processes AIS tracks of tens of thousands of vessels. In Oceana's description, it uses machine learning and cloud computing to identify when they are fishing. Before launch, the Washington Post reported, the government of Kiribati used it to document how a tuna-fishing vessel had operated illegally. That was inside the Phoenix Islands Protected Area, declared off-limits to commercial fishing in early 2015, resulting in a US$1 million fine. Oceana board member Ted Danson wrote in Grist that the vessel was the Marshall Islands-flagged Marshalls 203. The fine was against its owner, Central Pacific Fishing Company. A goodwill arrangement to pay a further US$1 million as a grant came with it.

Oceana's Jacqueline Savitz wrote in BBC Future in 2018 that the technology helped Kiribati extract a US$2 million fine, about 1% of GDP. The cited sources do not date the fine.

Purpose. Make commercial fishing activity visible in near real time by processing AIS vessel tracks to identify when vessels are fishing.

AI’s contribution

An independent newspaper confirms that Kiribati used the platform to document the illegal operation. Oceana describes the platform's fishing detection as machine learning over AIS data. No cited source says whether the classifier's output, as opposed to the vessel's track, carried the case. Developer (Oceana, SkyTruth, Google) and deployer (Kiribati) are distinct.

Further evidence

Setting. A vessel broadcasting AIS inside a no-take area that Kiribati closed to commercial fishing from 1 January 2015. A government willing to prosecute on third-party satellite-derived evidence.

Conditions. AIS transmitters left on (the BBC piece notes vessels can switch them off). Admissibility of AIS-derived evidence under the coastal state's law.
