Global Fishing Watch helps Kiribati fine a vessel's owner US$1 million

Kiribati used Global Fishing Watch to document a vessel fishing illegally in the Phoenix Islands Protected Area; a US$1 million fine plus a US$1 million goodwill grant followed.

  • 3 an organization

    Kiribati secured a US$1 million fine against Central Pacific Fishing Company plus a US$1 million goodwill grant (Grist). The BBC piece puts the total at US$2 million, about 1% of the country's gross domestic product.

  • Not rated society

What happened

Global Fishing Watch was developed by Oceana, SkyTruth and Google. It processes automatic identification system tracks of tens of thousands of vessels. In Oceana's description, it uses machine learning and cloud computing to identify when they are fishing. Kiribati closed the Phoenix Islands Protected Area to commercial fishing on 1 January 2015. Oceana's Jacqueline Savitz wrote in 2018 that Global Fishing Watch revealed illegal fishing in the area a few months after the closure. The platform launched publicly at the Our Ocean conference on 15 September 2016. The Washington Post reported that before the launch the government of Kiribati had used it to document how a tuna-fishing vessel had operated illegally inside the protected area. That resulted in a US$1 million fine. Oceana board member Ted Danson wrote in Grist that the vessel was the Marshall Islands-flagged Marshalls 203. The fine was against its owner, Central Pacific Fishing Company. A goodwill arrangement to pay a further US$1 million as a grant came with it. The cited sources do not date the fine. In June 2017 Indonesia became the first country to publish its vessel monitoring system data through Global Fishing Watch. Savitz wrote in BBC Future in 2018 that the technology helped Kiribati extract a US$2 million fine, about 1% of gross domestic product. A 2018 study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, by economists funded in part by Google, used Global Fishing Watch data. The economists found that fishing in the protected area had more than doubled in the run-up to the closure and fell to nearly zero after it.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

Global Fishing Watch analyzed vessels' AIS positions to identify probable fishing behavior and show activity inside the protected area.

Autonomy

The platform supplied evidence of vessel activity. Kiribati's authorities pursued the enforcement case and secured the fine.

Workflow

A vessel broadcasting AIS inside a no-take area that Kiribati closed to commercial fishing from 1 January 2015. A government willing to prosecute on third-party satellite-derived evidence.

Human oversight

The government of Kiribati used the platform to document the vessel's illegal operation and secured the fine. The cited sources do not describe how officials reviewed the platform's output. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Benefits

Beneficiaries

Kiribati gained an enforcement payment and greater ability to investigate activity in its protected waters. A resulting improvement in fish stocks or local livelihoods was not measured.

Duration

The fine was a single enforcement outcome, and the cited sources do not date it. The 2018 study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences found that fishing in the protected area fell to nearly zero after the closure, and it does not discuss this case. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]
