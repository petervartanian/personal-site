What happened

Global Fishing Watch, developed by Oceana, SkyTruth and Google and launched publicly at the Our Ocean conference on 15 September 2016, processes AIS tracks of tens of thousands of vessels and, in Oceana's description, uses machine learning and cloud computing to identify when they are fishing. Before launch, the Washington Post reported, the government of Kiribati used it to document how a tuna-fishing vessel had operated illegally inside the Phoenix Islands Protected Area, declared off-limits to commercial fishing in early 2015, resulting in a US$1 million fine.

Oceana board member Ted Danson wrote in Grist that the vessel was the Marshall Islands-flagged Marshalls 203 and that the fine against its owner, Central Pacific Fishing Company, was accompanied by a goodwill arrangement to pay a further US$1 million as a grant; Oceana's Jacqueline Savitz wrote in BBC Future in 2018 that the technology helped Kiribati extract a US$2 million fine, about 1% of GDP. The sources on file do not date the fine, so the date is that of the first report.

What it was built to do. Make commercial fishing activity visible in near real time by processing AIS vessel tracks to identify when vessels are fishing.

The system's part

An independent newspaper confirms that Kiribati used the platform to document the illegal operation. The platform's fishing detection is described by Oceana as machine learning over AIS data, but no source on file says whether the classifier's output, as opposed to the vessel's track, carried the case. Developer (Oceana, SkyTruth, Google) and deployer (Kiribati) are distinct.

Replication

What produced it. A vessel broadcasting AIS inside a no-take area that Kiribati closed to commercial fishing from 1 January 2015. A government willing to prosecute on third-party satellite-derived evidence.

What must hold again. AIS transmitters left on (the BBC piece notes vessels can switch them off). Admissibility of AIS-derived evidence under the coastal state's law.
