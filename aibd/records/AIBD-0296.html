Empatica Embrace seizure alert reaches Morgan McGrath's mother

depth 3 for a person

IEEE Spectrum (6 Feb 2018): an Embrace wristband alerted the mother of Morgan McGrath, 26, of Fulton, Mo., during a seizure about 15 months earlier; she called paramedics and McGrath survived.

What happened

Elie Dolgin reported in IEEE Spectrum on 6 February 2018 (the publication date is used because the article dates the seizure only to 'fifteen months ago', about November 2016) that Morgan McGrath, a 26-year-old teacher from Fulton, Missouri, 'suffered an epileptic seizure that left her unresponsive, frothing at the mouth, with blue lips, and not breathing'; her wrist-worn Embrace device sent her mother an alert, her mother called the paramedics, and 'thanks to a timely response, McGrath is alive and well today'. The article explains that the device records skin conductance and three-axis accelerometer data and 'uses a proprietary algorithm to signal when someone is having a convulsive seizure', with the app sending a text or call to pre-specified caregivers, and that in testing on 135 patients over more than 6,500 hours it 'correctly identified nearly every single seizure'. The FDA cleared Embrace (Empatica S.r.l.) under 510(k) K172935 on 26 January 2018, after the McGrath event; the 510(k) summary describes an on-board algorithm validated against video-EEG that, when a likely generalized tonic-clonic seizure is detected, messages the Alert app to call and text designated caregivers. A later prospective multicentre study by Empatica staff with hospital co-authors (Onorati et al. 2021) reports sensitivity of 0.92 in children and 0.94 in adults for a predefined machine-learning algorithm, and an independent Montreal epilepsy-monitoring-unit study (Jahani et al. 2026) found the Embrace2 detected all 16 convulsive seizures that occurred while worn and working, with alerts reaching caregivers in 15 of 16 cases and 29 false alarms. The McGrath rescue rests on this single news item; the Empatica case-study abstract (Regalia, Onorati, Picard, American Epilepsy Society) named by the earlier verifier could not be located, and no second account of the McGrath event was found.

What it was built to do. Built to detect convulsive seizures from electrodermal activity and motion and to summon designated caregivers; the alert to McGrath's mother was that purpose.

The system's part

Per IEEE Spectrum, the Embrace detected the seizure and its app alerted McGrath's mother, who made the emergency call; the device did not call for help itself. The algorithm is described as proprietary by the article, as an on-board algorithm validated against video-EEG by the FDA summary, and as a predefined machine-learning algorithm by Empatica's own 2021 study. The McGrath event is known only from this single news item; the AES case-study abstract cited by the earlier verifier was not found.

Replication

What produced it. A convulsive (generalized tonic-clonic) seizure, the only type the device is cleared to detect. The wristband worn and paired with a phone running the Alert app, with a caregiver designated. A caregiver able to reach the patient or call emergency services on receiving the alert.

What must hold again. Connectivity between watch, phone and caregiver (one of 16 alerts in Montreal failed on a Wi-Fi disconnection). Caregivers tolerate false alarms (29 in 373 monitoring days in Montreal, mostly from routine movements). Focal seizures without convulsions are not detected (none of 510 in Montreal).
