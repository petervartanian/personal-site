Empatica Embrace seizure alert reaches Morgan McGrath's mother

What happened

Elie Dolgin reported in IEEE Spectrum on 6 February 2018 on Morgan McGrath, a 26-year-old teacher from Fulton, Missouri. She “suffered an epileptic seizure that left her unresponsive, frothing at the mouth, with blue lips, and not breathing”. The publication date is used because the article dates the seizure only to “fifteen months ago”, about November 2016. Her wrist-worn Embrace device alerted her mother, who called the paramedics, and “thanks to a timely response, McGrath is alive and well today”. The article says it records skin conductance and three-axis accelerometer data and “uses a proprietary algorithm to signal when someone is having a convulsive seizure”. The app sends a text or call to pre-specified caregivers. In testing on 135 patients over more than 6,500 hours it “correctly identified nearly every single seizure”.

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) cleared Embrace (Empatica S.r.l.) under 510(k) K172935 on 26 January 2018, after the McGrath event. The 510(k) summary describes an on-board algorithm validated against video electroencephalography (EEG). On detecting a likely generalized tonic-clonic seizure, it messages the Alert app to call and text designated caregivers. A later prospective multicenter study (Onorati et al. 2021) by Empatica staff with hospital co-authors reports sensitivity for a predefined machine-learning algorithm. It was 0.92 in children and 0.94 in adults. An independent Montreal epilepsy-monitoring-unit study (Jahani et al. 2026) found the Embrace2 detected all 16 convulsive seizures while worn and working. Alerts reached caregivers in 15 of 16 cases, with 29 false alarms. The McGrath rescue rests on this single news item.

The Empatica case-study abstract (Regalia, Onorati, Picard, American Epilepsy Society) is not among the sources. No second account of the McGrath event was found.

What it was built to do. Built to detect convulsive seizures from electrodermal activity and motion and to summon designated caregivers. The alert to McGrath's mother was that purpose.

The system's part

Per IEEE Spectrum, the Embrace detected the seizure and its app alerted McGrath's mother, who made the emergency call. The device did not call for help itself. The article calls the algorithm proprietary. The FDA summary describes it as an on-board algorithm validated against video-EEG. Empatica's own 2021 study calls it a predefined machine-learning algorithm. The McGrath event is known only from this single news item. The AES case-study abstract is not among the sources.

Replication

What produced it. A convulsive (generalized tonic-clonic) seizure, the only type the device is cleared to detect. The wristband worn and paired with a phone running the Alert app, with a caregiver designated. A caregiver able to reach the patient or call emergency services on receiving the alert.

What must hold again. Connectivity between watch, phone and caregiver (one of 16 alerts in Montreal failed on a Wi-Fi disconnection). Caregivers tolerate false alarms (29 in 373 monitoring days in Montreal, mostly from routine movements). Focal seizures without convulsions are not detected (none of 510 in Montreal).
