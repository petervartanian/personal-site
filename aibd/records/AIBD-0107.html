SkinVision flags a changing mole, and doctors remove a stage 1A melanoma

SkinVision rated a 31-year-old's changing arm mole high risk; stage 1A melanoma was found and removed, and he is cancer-free.

The system’s part
The app's classifier graded a phone photograph of the mole high risk and told him to see a doctor at once. By his account that prompt, not the color change, is what sent him to his GP. He said he only goes to the doctor when absolutely desperate. The referral, excision, biopsy and staging were done by the GP, the dermatologist and pathology, none of whom the Daily Mail quotes. The article does not report the app's score, its version, or whether the 48-hour dermatologist advice it mentions was used.
Limits
The account depends on the patient's report of an app warning, clinical diagnosis, and recovery. The sources contain no direct clinician confirmation, app output, or dated medical record. Early removal of melanoma does not prove that the app prevented death.
Costs and harms
The Basel and Zurich researchers found that the app rated far more lesions high risk than dermatologists did. The BMJ review authors warned that such apps cannot be relied on to detect all skin cancers.
Later
Nothing later is known. No later report on this case was found in the cited sources; SkinVision's site now says the app is not yet available in the United States pending regulatory clearance.

What happened

Jason Sheridan, a 31-year-old office worker from Liverpool living in Vauxhall, South London, noticed a new 1 cm mole on his left arm in March 2018. He did not see a doctor. Twelve months later the mole turned pink, and he checked it with SkinVision, an app that uses artificial intelligence to grade photographed moles low, medium or high risk. The app returned a high-risk rating and urged him to see a doctor. His general practitioner referred him to a dermatologist, and two weeks later the mole was removed in a 40-minute operation. The biopsy showed stage 1A melanoma. A further biopsy found no spread, and he is cancer-free. The Daily Mail reported the case on 17 January 2020 without dates for the app check or surgery. Researchers report mixed results for the app. The authors of a BMJ systematic review published on 10 February 2020 found a sensitivity of 80% across three small studies. They warned that such apps cannot be relied on to detect all skin cancers. In a study published on 4 February 2022 and funded by a research grant from SkinVision, Erasmus MC researchers found that the app rated 86.9% of premalignant and malignant lesions high risk. In a study published on 7 August 2022, researchers at the university hospitals of Basel and Zurich tested app version 6.8.1. They found that the app rated far more lesions high risk than dermatologists did, with a sensitivity of 41.3% to 83.3% for melanoma.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

SkinVision classified Sheridan's photograph of a changing mole as high risk and advised him to see a doctor.

Workflow

A smartphone photograph of a changing mole submitted to SkinVision's risk classifier. A high-risk rating with an instruction to see a doctor, acted on by the user. A GP referral and an excision with pathology that confirms and stages the lesion.

Human oversight

His GP referred him to a dermatologist, and clinicians removed the mole and used pathology to diagnose and stage the melanoma.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The lesion is one the classifier can score from a phone photograph. The article reports no score or app version. The user follows the app's advice rather than treating the rating as noise. Clinical follow-up is available quickly enough for early-stage removal. Here the operation came two weeks after referral.

Original evidence

The case rests on the man's own account in the Daily Mail article of 17 January 2020, which dostor.org relays. The accuracy figures come from a BMJ systematic review, an Erasmus MC study that SkinVision funded and a study at the university hospitals of Basel and Zurich. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6]
