WhaleSpotter thermal AI starts whale alerts in San Francisco Bay

What happened

UC Santa Barbara announced the San Francisco Bay WhaleSpotter system on May 19, 2026. Its first thermal camera was on Angel Island. AI flags whale heat signatures, a marine-mammal specialist checks them, and confirmed detections appear on Whale Safe for mariners and the Coast Guard’s Vessel Traffic Service. IEEE Spectrum attended the launch and reported that the system had logged 6,600 detections in about a week and a half. These were repeated detections, not 6,600 individual whales. Bay Nature later visited the traffic service and described its dedicated Whale Safe screen, near-daily detections, radio guidance to captains, and a second camera on the ferry Lyra. That reporting establishes operational use. The sources do not measure a reduction in strikes or link a particular alert to a documented avoided collision.

What it was built to do. The network was built to detect whales in the bay around the clock and alert vessels so they can slow or alter course.

The system's part

Not assessed. AI detects candidate whale signatures in thermal imagery. Human specialists verify them before alerts reach mariners. IEEE Spectrum adds an early detection count and the developer’s explanation of why human review remains necessary. Bay Nature independently observed the Coast Guard workflow. Neither report independently estimates the system’s effect on whale survival.

Replication

What produced it. A fixed vantage point over a whale-vessel overlap hotspot. Thermal cameras able to see blows at several nautical miles. Human review of each AI detection before alerting. A Vessel Traffic Service willing to relay alerts by radio.

What must hold again. Whales continue to enter the bay in the detection footprint. Mariners and ferry operators act on posted detections. Funding sustains camera nodes and specialist review.
