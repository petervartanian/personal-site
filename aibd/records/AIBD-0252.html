WhaleSpotter detects gray whales and alerts San Francisco Bay ships

WhaleSpotter software flags gray whales in thermal camera images for a specialist to verify before alerts reach San Francisco Bay mariners, and detections had passed 16,900 by early July.

Not isolated. AI detects candidate whale signatures in thermal imagery. Human specialists verify them before alerts reach mariners. Costs and harms

What happened

UC Santa Barbara announced the San Francisco Bay WhaleSpotter system on 19 May 2026. Its first thermal camera was on Angel Island. The AI flags whale heat signatures, and a marine-mammal specialist checks them. Confirmed detections appear on Whale Safe for mariners and for the Coast Guard’s Vessel Traffic Service. IEEE Spectrum attended the launch and reported on 26 May 2026 that the system had logged 6,600 detections in about a week and a half. The count includes repeated detections of the same whales. The Marine Mammal Center counted a whale found off San Leandro on 27 May 2026 among suspected or probable strike deaths. It told CBS News Bay Area that a whale found off Pier 80 on 4 June 2026 died from a suspected vessel strike. Bay Nature later visited the traffic service and described its dedicated Whale Safe screen and near-daily detections. It also described radio guidance to captains and a second camera on the ferry Lyra. That reporting establishes operational use. Inside Climate News reported on 26 July 2026 that detections had passed 16,900 by early July and that San Francisco Bay Ferry captains were receiving alerts on board. Erin Meyer-Gutbrod of the University of South Carolina told Inside Climate News that detection alone does not reduce strike risk. No source measures a reduction in strikes or links a particular alert to a documented avoided collision.

Details

AI system

Autonomy

The AI detects candidates automatically, but people verify sightings and decide how vessels should respond. It does not control ship steering or speed.

Workflow

A fixed vantage point over a whale-vessel overlap hotspot. Thermal cameras able to see blows at several nautical miles. Human review of each AI detection before alerting. A Vessel Traffic Service willing to relay alerts by radio.

Human oversight

A marine-mammal specialist checks each candidate detection before the location reaches Whale Safe. Vessel Traffic Service operators then guide nearby captains by radio.

AI contribution

The software flags candidate whale heat signatures in thermal images, and a marine-mammal specialist verifies each one before it reaches mariners. No source estimates the system's effect on whale survival. Erin Meyer-Gutbrod told Inside Climate News that detection alone does not reduce strike risk. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]
