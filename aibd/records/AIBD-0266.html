Brain implant lets a man with ALS spell for 90 days without retraining

A click detector built on a long short-term memory network let a man with ALS spell through a brain implant at a median 10.2 characters per minute for 90 days without retraining, in the laboratory.

  • 2 a person

The system’s part
The LSTM click detector is the decoding element. Its stability without retraining is the paper's main result. The source is a peer-reviewed report by the developers, with no outside corroboration and no use outside the laboratory.
Limits
The spelling rate and the 90-day stability concern one participant in laboratory testing, with 626 minutes of total use. No later report on the click speller itself is on record. The result does not establish independence from laboratory support or comparable performance for other patients.
Costs and harms
The system required a chronically implanted electrode array. Each session began with a calibration recorded at rest. A transient reduction in signal power interrupted use of the fixed model, and home reliability was not tested.
Later
Nothing later is known. The team later reported that offline gesture decoding in a man with ALS in the same trial worsened over five months, starting about two years after implant, as high gamma power fell. No later report on the click speller itself is on record.

What happened

A participant with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) in a clinical trial at Johns Hopkins (NCT03567213) had a chronically implanted high-density electrocorticographic brain-computer interface over sensorimotor cortex. High-gamma power vectors from 128 channels fed a long short-term memory layer with 25 hidden units and fully connected layers. These classified rest versus grasp every 100 milliseconds. The click detector was trained on almost 44 minutes of data (480 trials) collected up to 21 days before use. It then ran for 90 days without retraining. The participant controlled a switch-scanning speller for a total of 626 minutes with a median spelling rate of 10.2 characters per minute. The team posted the work as a preprint on 25 September 2023. Candrea et al. published it in Communications Medicine on 22 October 2024. The paper states that all data collection and testing were performed in the laboratory. No home use is reported. In a preprint posted on 3 October 2025, the Johns Hopkins and UMC Utrecht team behind the click detector reported that offline gesture decoding in a man with ALS in this trial worsened over five months, starting about two years after implant. A systematic review by Swanson and colleagues (Journal of Neural Engineering, 23 June 2026) records the study as running 12 weeks without recalibration. The review's first author also lists UMC Utrecht.

Details

AI system

Model

A long short-term memory network with 25 hidden units and two fully connected layers.

Role

The model detected intended clicks in cortical recordings.

Configuration

Researchers implemented the network with Keras and TensorFlow 2.8.0.

Inputs and outputs

High-gamma activity from 128 cortical channels was classified as rest or grasp every 100 milliseconds and converted into clicks for a scanning speller.

Autonomy

The participant chose letters by producing intended clicks while the interface scanned possible selections.

Workflow

High-density ECoG over sensorimotor cortex. LSTM click detector trained on under 44 minutes of data. Switch-scanning speller interface.

Human oversight

The paper states that all data collection and testing were performed in the laboratory, with research staff present. Each session began with a calibration recorded while the participant sat still. A voting window kept sporadic classifications from producing clicks. A one-second lockout followed every detected click. [1] [2] [3] [4]
