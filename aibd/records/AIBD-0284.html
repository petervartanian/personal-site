Apple Watch Series 3 drop from 99 to 65 bpm marks AF reversion

sudden 99-to-65 bpm drop at 19:29 that coincided with reversion from AF to sinus rhythm

Melbourne cardiologists matched 199 Apple Watch heart-rate readings to a Holter: the watch showed a sudden 99-to-65 bpm drop at 19:29 that coincided with reversion from AF to sinus rhythm.

What happened

Toner, Al-Kaisey, Koshy and Lim of the Department of Cardiology, Austin Hospital, Melbourne, reported in BMJ Case Reports (published 4 February 2020; the event date is not given, so the publication date is used) an 80-year-old woman with known paroxysmal atrial fibrillation who wore a 24-hour Holter monitor to assess rate control. On returning the monitor she reported feeling her heart 'doing a flip' at 19:29 and noted that her heart rate had suddenly dropped from 99 bpm to 65 bpm as measured by her Apple Watch Series 3. Review of the Holter showed she had reverted from AF to sinus rhythm at that time. The authors extracted 199 watch readings over 22 hours and matched them to the Holter; the watch's average heart rate was 94 bpm in AF and 66 bpm after reversion. No notification, irregular-rhythm alert or ECG app is described: the patient read the heart-rate display herself. The report documents no change in her treatment. The earlier verifier could not read the full text (BMJ CAPTCHA); the PMC copy, read here, names the device.

What it was built to do. The watch's optical heart-rate estimate is built to show the wearer their pulse; its use here to time a rhythm change was that function, read by the patient rather than flagged by the device.

The system's part

No alert, notification or rhythm classifier was involved: the watch displayed a photoplethysmographic heart-rate estimate, the patient noticed the drop, and the authors compared the readings with the Holter afterwards. The paper does not describe the heart-rate estimation as machine learning, so the AI character of the contribution is not established.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it

  • A patient with known paroxysmal AF wearing a heart-rate-displaying smartwatch while a Holter monitor was also fitted
  • The patient noticing and reporting a sudden, persistent heart-rate drop with its time
  • Clinicians willing to export the watch readings (Apple Health app to Excel) and align them with the Holter

What must hold again

  • The rhythm change produces a heart-rate change large enough to be visible on the watch's optical estimate
  • A reference recording (Holter) exists to confirm what the watch suggested
