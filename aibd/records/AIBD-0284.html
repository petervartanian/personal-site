Apple Watch shows a woman's heart rate falling as her arrhythmia ends

An 80-year-old woman's Apple Watch Series 3 showed a sudden drop in heart rate from 99 to 65 beats per minute at 19:29, when a Holter monitor recorded her reversion to sinus rhythm.

What happened

Toner, Al-Kaisey, Koshy and Lim of the Department of Cardiology, Austin Hospital, Melbourne, published a case in BMJ Case Reports on 4 February 2020 without giving the date of the event. The journal accepted the report on 13 January 2020, so the episode took place before that date. An 80-year-old woman with known paroxysmal atrial fibrillation wore a 24-hour Holter monitor to assess rate control. On returning the monitor she reported feeling her heart “doing a flip” at 19:29. She noted that her heart rate had suddenly dropped from 99 beats per minute (bpm) to 65 bpm on her Apple Watch Series 3. The Holter showed that she had reverted from atrial fibrillation to sinus rhythm at that time. The authors exported 199 watch readings over 22 hours and matched them to the Holter. The watch's average heart rate was 94 bpm in atrial fibrillation and 66 bpm after reversion. The report describes no notification, irregular-rhythm alert or electrocardiogram app. The report documents no change in her treatment. The authors declared no competing interests and no funding. In its 2018 decision on the Apple Watch Irregular Rhythm Notification Feature, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) states that the feature is not intended for people already diagnosed with atrial fibrillation. The patient read the plain heart-rate display herself, so the FDA decision does not cover this use.

    It is also not intended for use in individuals previously diagnosed with AFib.

Details

AI system

Role

The watch supplied a time-stamped clue to spontaneous rhythm conversion, with no documented treatment change or established AI classifier.

Configuration

The participant used an Apple Watch Series 3.

Autonomy

The watch displayed heart-rate estimates. The patient recognized the change, and clinicians interpreted its significance.

Workflow

The patient noticed a sudden fall in the displayed heart rate and reported its time; clinicians confirmed the rhythm change on the simultaneous Holter recording.

Human oversight

The patient read the heart-rate display herself, and no alert or rhythm classifier was involved. The authors compared the watch readings with the concurrent Holter recording afterwards. [1] [2]

Benefits

Reach

One patient benefited, and the benefit was information about her heart rhythm. [1] [2]

Duration

The benefit was knowledge of a single rhythm change at 19:29, confirmed on the Holter. The authors say that outcomes of arrhythmias found by smartwatch heart-rate monitoring are the subject of ongoing research, and no later report on this patient is cited. [1] [2]
