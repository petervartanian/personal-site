What happened

Toner, Al-Kaisey, Koshy and Lim of the Department of Cardiology, Austin Hospital, Melbourne, reported a case in BMJ Case Reports (published 4 February 2020). The event date is not given, so the publication date is used. An 80-year-old woman with known paroxysmal atrial fibrillation wore a 24-hour Holter monitor to assess rate control. On returning the monitor she reported feeling her heart “doing a flip” at 19:29. She noted her heart rate had suddenly dropped from 99 bpm to 65 bpm on her Apple Watch Series 3. The Holter showed she had reverted from AF to sinus rhythm at that time. The authors exported 199 watch readings over 22 hours and matched them to the Holter. The watch's average heart rate was 94 bpm in AF and 66 bpm after reversion. No notification, irregular-rhythm alert or electrocardiogram (ECG) app is described: the patient read the heart-rate display herself.

The report documents no change in her treatment. The full text was not available from BMJ. The PMC copy is among the sources and names the device.

What it was built to do. The watch's optical heart-rate estimate is built to show the wearer their pulse. Its use here to time a rhythm change was that function, read by the patient rather than flagged by the device.

The system's part

No alert, notification or rhythm classifier was involved. The watch displayed a photoplethysmographic heart-rate estimate, the patient noticed the drop, and the authors compared the readings with the Holter afterwards. The paper does not describe the heart-rate estimation as machine learning. So the AI character of the contribution is not established.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. A patient with known paroxysmal AF wearing a heart-rate-displaying smartwatch while a Holter monitor was also fitted. The patient noticing and reporting a sudden, persistent heart-rate drop with its time. Clinicians willing to export the watch readings (Apple Health app to Excel) and align them with the Holter.

What must hold again. The rhythm change produces a heart-rate change large enough to be visible on the watch's optical estimate. A reference recording (Holter) exists to confirm what the watch suggested.
