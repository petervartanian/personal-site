SES Water says AI leak alarms cut leakage run times by up to 40%

reduced leakage run rate times by up to 40%

SES Water installed 1,000 underground flow and pressure sensors with AI prediction that raises leak alarms within minutes; it told the BBC leakage run rate times fell by up to 40% (11 March 2024).

What happened

The BBC (Yvette Austin, 11 March 2024) reported that Sutton and East Surrey (SES) Water, supplying 160 million liters a day to 300,000 homes and businesses in parts of Surrey, Kent and South London, was one of the first UK companies to use artificial intelligence across its whole network to reduce leaks: 1,000 underground sensors monitor flow and pressure, the technology predicts what levels should be, and when a leak happens an alarm is raised within minutes and engineers deployed. Innovation manager Jeremy Heath said pipes are often repaired before customers notice a drop in pressure; head of asset strategy Liam Ahearne said the smart network had reduced leakage run rate times by up to 40%. The BBC noted Ofwat's figure that about a fifth of water put into the network in England and Wales is lost to leakage, SES Water's 2022/23 loss of 76 liters per property per day, and the Angling Trust's criticism that the company's targets were not good enough. The vendor and method are not named, and no regulator report of a specific repaired burst was found.

What it was built to do. The smart network was built to detect leaks early from sensor data against predicted levels and dispatch repairs; the reduced run times are its intended use.

The system's part

not assessed The BBC report, an independent source with named utility staff, describes the AI only in general terms (prediction of expected levels, alarms within minutes); the developer is unnamed and the 40% figure is the deployer's claim, so the AI's share of any leakage reduction is unverified.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it
  • Dense sensor coverage (1,000 flow and pressure sensors) across a compact supply area
  • Predicted-versus-actual comparison raising alarms within minutes
  • Repair crews dispatched on alarms rather than customer reports
What must hold again
  • Sensor density and telemetry sufficient to localize anomalies
  • Crew capacity to act on alarms quickly
  • Independent reporting of leakage volumes to confirm the claimed effect
