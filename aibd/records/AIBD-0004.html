Stanford neuroprosthesis decodes Pat Bennett's speech at 62 wpm

Four 64-electrode microelectrode arrays and an RNN with a 125,000-word language model let Pat Bennett (ALS) produce text at 62 wpm, 23.8% word error rate

ⓘ

What happened

At Stanford University, a participant with bulbar-onset amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (identified in news reports as Pat Bennett, 68) had four 64-electrode arrays implanted. They went into ventral premotor cortex (area 6v) and area 44 under the BrainGate2 trial. Stanford's release dates the surgery to 29 March 2022. A recurrent neural network (RNN) converted her neural activity into phoneme probabilities and a 125,000-word language model assembled sentences. She produced text at 62 words per minute (Willett et al., Nature, 23 August 2023). The word error rate was 9.1% on a 50-word vocabulary and 23.8% on the full vocabulary. The work was described as a proof of concept in research sessions. The paper dates sessions by post-implant day (119, 136 and 141 for the reported data), not calendar date.

The Stanford release says research sessions began about a month after the 29 March 2022 surgery. It says 62 words per minute came after four months. That places the result in about August 2022, so 1 August 2022 is used as the observation date.

Purpose. Built to restore communication by decoding attempted speech from intracortical recordings into text; large-vocabulary text communication was the intended benefit and is what occurred.

AI’s contribution

An RNN decoder plus an n-gram language model is the mechanism translating neural activity into text. The Stanford neurosurgeon describes the language model as “a sophisticated auto-correct”. The benefit is attributable to the decoding system.

Beneficiaries

  • a person

    Material benefit to one identifiable person: large-vocabulary communication at conversational pace in research sessions. The team itself called it a proof of concept rather than an everyday device.

Further evidence

Setting. Preserved articulatory code for phonemes in ventral premotor cortex years after loss of speech. Four intracortical arrays in speech-related cortex. RNN decoder plus large-vocabulary language model trained on the participant's attempted speech. Regulated trial setting (BrainGate2) with research support.

Conditions. Array signal quality remains adequate. Participant can attempt speech and tolerate recording sessions. Decoder retraining available across sessions.
