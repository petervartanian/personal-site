What happened

Soubh, Rasenack, Haarmann, Zabel and Bergau of the Department of Cardiology and Pneumology, University Medical Center Göttingen, Germany, reported in European Heart Journal - Case Reports (published 15 April 2025; the event dates are not given, so the publication date is used) a 34-year-old man with no medical history who consulted his primary care physician after his smartwatch detected “heart arrhythmias” while he was asymptomatic. A 12-lead ECG showed sinus rhythm with frequent premature atrial contractions (PACs); a cardiologist found a left ventricular ejection fraction of 35% with a dilated ventricle. Despite guideline-directed heart-failure therapy he returned months later with palpitations and chest pain; cardiac MRI showed LVEF 15%, coronary disease was excluded by CT angiography, and Holter recordings showed a PAC burden of 30–50%.

High-density 3D mapping located the ectopic focus at the left inferior pulmonary vein and radiofrequency ablation terminated the arrhythmia. At six months he was symptom-free with LVEF 50% and fewer than 1% PACs, and spironolactone was stopped. The authors call it the first reported PAC-related arrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy initially detected by a wearable. The watch's brand, model and the form of its alert are not stated.

What it was built to do. Smartwatch arrhythmia alerts are built to prompt medical review of irregular rhythms; the alert here was that purpose, although the condition it uncovered (PAC-induced cardiomyopathy) is one the authors say wearables are not usually credited with finding.

The system's part

The watch's detection of “heart arrhythmias” is what sent an asymptomatic man to his doctor; everything after (ECG, echo, MRI, mapping, ablation) was clinical. The report does not say what the watch flagged, whether a notification or an ECG reading, nor which algorithm, so the device's contribution is established only as the trigger.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. An asymptomatic wearer whose smartwatch flagged arrhythmias and who took the flag to a physician. A primary care ECG followed by cardiology work-up including echocardiography, MRI and Holter monitoring. An electrophysiology service able to map and ablate a high-burden PAC focus.

What must hold again. The PAC burden is high enough to be caught by the watch and to be the cause of the cardiomyopathy (the authors note case reports typically exceed 30%). The ventricular dysfunction is still reversible when the arrhythmia is eliminated.
