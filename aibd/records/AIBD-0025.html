What happened

In a case report published on 4 July 2024 (no event dates given; publication date used), an asymptomatic 39-year-old man presented to the cardiology outpatient clinic of University Medical Centre Mannheim, Germany, because of QT prolongation recorded with his Withings ScanWatch, whose single-lead ECG recordings he had made out of curiosity; the watch displayed values such as QT 506 ms and QTc 457 ms. Genetic screening found a previously unpublished nonsense variant in KCNH2, establishing congenital long QT syndrome. Nadolol 40 mg daily shortened his QTc by 50–70 ms to 430 ms. His 7-year-old daughter was found to carry the same variant with a borderline QTc of 460 ms and was also prescribed nadolol.

What it was built to do. The ScanWatch records a single-lead ECG and, in the version described, includes a QT monitoring function; flagging a prolonged QT interval was within what it was built to do.

The system's part

The watch's automated ECG analysis displayed the QT and QTc values that prompted the clinic visit; no automatic alert is described and the report does not say who first noticed the values. Diagnosis rested on clinical ECGs and genetic testing.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. The wearer records ScanWatch ECGs and the QT function reports a prolonged interval. The wearer or a clinician takes the displayed values to a cardiology service. Clinical ECG and genetic testing confirm an inherited channelopathy.

What must hold again. The QT function is available in the wearer's region and firmware. Recordings are of adequate quality. Cardiogenetic evaluation and family screening are accessible.
