BrainGate wireless iBCI lets T5 and T10 use a tablet at home

T5 typed 196 correct characters in 14.6 minutes (13.4 per minute)

Two men with tetraplegia used a wireless BrainGate iBCI at home for web browsing, apps and typing, with 24-hour home recording.

What happened

In a BrainGate pilot-trial study published in IEEE Transactions on Biomedical Engineering in 2021 (Simeral et al.; Brown University, Massachusetts General Hospital, Providence VA Medical Center and Stanford University), two men with tetraplegia from C4 spinal cord injury, T5 (63) and T10 (35), used a 192-electrode intracortical brain-computer interface whose cables were replaced by wireless transmitters. Sessions took place in their homes; a Kalman filter decoder and a linear discriminant classifier converted neural activity into cursor velocity and clicks. Both controlled an unmodified commercial tablet to browse the web and run apps; T5 typed 196 correct characters in 14.6 minutes (13.4 per minute) in Notepad, and neural data were recorded continuously from T10 for 24 hours at home. Calendar dates are not given, so the publication date is used.

What it was built to do. Restore computer access and communication for people with paralysis by decoding intended movement from motor cortex

Who benefited

The system's part

Machine-learning decoders (Kalman filter and linear discriminant classifier) are the mechanism that translates intracortical activity into cursor movement and clicks; wireless hardware enabled home use, and the decoded control is what produced the tablet and typing capacity.

Replication

What produced it

  • Chronically implanted microelectrode arrays with usable spiking signals
  • Daily decoder calibration with Kalman filter and LDA classifier
  • Wireless transmitters removing the cable tether

What must hold again

  • Stable array signal quality years after implantation
  • Research staff available to set up sessions
  • Participant retains intention-to-move signals in motor cortex
