Wireless BrainGate implant lets two paralyzed men use a tablet at home

Two men with tetraplegia used a wireless BrainGate brain-computer interface at home; its decoders turned neural activity into cursor movement and clicks for browsing and typing on a tablet.

  • 2 a person

    Two participants gained tablet control, web browsing and typing at home during research sessions. No source reports lasting independent daily use, so the gain is a measurable improvement in capability.

  • Not rated society

    The study covers two participants in a pilot trial, and no source reports that the system reached anyone else, so no realized societal outcome is documented.

What happened

Simeral and colleagues published a study from the BrainGate pilot trial in IEEE Transactions on Biomedical Engineering in 2021. The authors are at Brown University, Massachusetts General Hospital, Providence VA Medical Center and Stanford University. Two men with tetraplegia from C4 spinal cord injury, T5 (63) and T10 (35), used a 192-electrode intracortical brain-computer interface. Wireless transmitters replaced its cables. Sessions were in their homes. A Kalman filter decoder and a linear discriminant classifier converted neural activity into cursor velocity and clicks. Both controlled an unmodified commercial tablet to browse the web and run apps. T5 typed 196 correct characters in 14.6 minutes (13.4 per minute) in Notepad. Neural data were recorded continuously from T10 for 24 hours at home. Calendar dates are not given. Brown University announced the study on 31 March 2021. In the release, Leigh Hochberg said that home visits stopped in March 2020 because of the pandemic. He said that caregivers were then trained to set up the wireless link, so that a trial participant used the interface with no research staff present. T5 went on to type 90 characters per minute by attempted handwriting in a Nature study published on 12 May 2021.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

Implanted electrodes recorded motor-cortex activity. A Kalman decoder generated cursor velocity, and a linear discriminant classifier generated clicks on an ordinary tablet.

Workflow

Chronically implanted microelectrode arrays with usable spiking signals. Daily Kalman filter and linear discriminant decoder calibration. Wireless transmitters removing the cable tether.

Human oversight

Research staff supported the home sessions, and the decoders were calibrated before the tested tasks. Leigh Hochberg said that after home visits stopped in March 2020, caregivers were trained to set up the wireless link so that a participant used the interface with no research staff present. [1] [2]
