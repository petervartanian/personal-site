Neuralink implant lets a paralyzed man control a computer by thought

Neuralink's N1 implant and its decoding software let Noland Arbaugh, paralyzed after a diving accident, control a computer by thought, and he still used it 28 months after surgery.

What happened

Noland Arbaugh, paralyzed after a diving accident, received the Neuralink N1 implant at Barrow Neurological Institute, Phoenix, in January 2024 (ABC15, 2 May 2024). He demonstrated online chess and cursor movement by thought. Tech Times later reported that roughly 85% of the implant's threads retracted from his brain about a month after surgery. ABC News reported on 17 May 2024 that threads had retracted and that removal was considered. It quoted him: “I can control a computer just like anyone else can”. Neuralink said that it modified the recording algorithm and the translation of signals into cursor movements. Nature news reported on 6 August 2024 that the device “has been adjusted in a bid to prevent glitches”. The Debrief counted 7 users on 7 July 2025, 6 of them implanted at Barrow. Sky News reported on 29 January 2026 that its science editor watched a UK trial participant, who received the implant at University College London Hospital, move a cursor by thought. The same report states that results from the trials had not yet been published in peer-reviewed journals or submitted to regulators. Tech Times reports that by early 2026 the trial had enrolled more than 20 participants across sites in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and the United Arab Emirates. Barrow reported on 29 April 2026 that the second participant, Alex Conley, controls a robotic arm to operate light switches and doors. Conley has a C4-C5 spinal cord injury and takes part in the CONVOY study. Tech Times reported on 28 May 2026, 28 months after the surgery, that software refinement had restored Arbaugh's use after the retraction. Citing an earlier report, Tech Times gave about 10 hours of daily use for chess, coursework, scheduling and communication.

Details

AI system

Role

The decoder translated neural signals into cursor and robotic-arm movements.

Configuration

The deployment used the N1 implant.

Inputs and outputs

Neural signals from the N1 implant were decoded into cursor movement and computer commands.

Autonomy

Arbaugh intentionally controlled the cursor through attempted movement, while the decoder translated his signals into commands.

Workflow

Implanted electrode threads in motor cortex. Company decoding software translating neural signals into cursor or robotic-arm commands. Software recalibration after thread retraction.

Benefits

Reach

The record documents benefits for Arbaugh, for Conley and for a UK participant observed by Sky News. The Debrief counted 7 users on 7 July 2025, and Tech Times reports that the trial had enrolled more than 20 participants by early 2026. These enrollment counts do not show how many participants benefited. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]

Duration

A May 2026 press account describes use 28 months after implantation. Its roughly ten-hour daily-use figure comes from an August 2025 interview, not a measured 28-month assessment.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Electrode threads remain sufficiently in place or software compensates. Daily use continues to be reported by the user. Peer-reviewed outcome reporting (absent so far).

Replication attempts

3 follow-up entries. Benefit recurred: 2. Outcome unresolved: 1.

Availability

The N1 was available to enrolled research participants through the PRIME study, with Barrow as a surgical site.
