An arthritis drug helped patients with severe COVID-19

Researchers used the Benevolent Platform to identify baricitinib as a candidate treatment for COVID-19.

ⓘ

AI system

System

Benevolent Platform

Editor’s note. The name appears in later publications. The original paper describes a knowledge graph and tools.

Model

SVO relationship extraction, graph queries, and Louvain community detection.

Editor’s note. Documented components, without a single named model.

Release

January 2020 configuration.

Editor’s note. The reviewed sources give no exact release or graph snapshot.

Provider

BenevolentAI

Role

The system extracted biomedical relationships, searched the graph, and analyzed protein networks.

Configuration

Coronavirus-augmented knowledge graph, SVO extraction, graph queries, and interactive protein-network analysis.

Inputs and outputs

Biomedical literature and databases became relationships, protein networks, and candidate drugs for expert review.

Editor’s note. These were scientific resources. Trial-patient records were not described as inputs to the discovery system.

Autonomy

Scientists directed the analysis and selected the drug. Clinicians controlled treatment.

Workflow

Scientists searched the graph, examined protein networks, and assessed drugs. Clinical investigators then tested baricitinib.

Human oversight

Researchers selected candidates. Clinical investigators, ethics committees, and trial monitors oversaw treatment.

Relation to AI

AI-assisted drug repurposing, followed by clinical trials.

AI contribution

Biomedical relationships and candidate drugs identified for researchers’ review.

Editor’s note. Researchers selected the drug. Trials measured its treatment effect without isolating the additional contribution from AI.

Benefits

Outcome identifiers

AIBD-0042-outcome-personal, AIBD-0042-outcome-organizational, AIBD-0042-outcome-societal

Benefit

Fewer deaths and faster recovery among patients hospitalized with COVID-19.

Category

Improved Physical Health

Status

Patient benefit observed. Hospital savings modeled. Wider access supported by WHO guidance.

Editor’s note. Neither realized hospital savings nor population-wide effects were measured in these studies.

Beneficiary groups

People, organizations, and society.

Beneficiaries

Hospitalized COVID-19 patients, hospitals, and health systems with access to baricitinib.

Reach

Trial participants: ACTT-2, 1,033. COV-BARRIER, 1,525. RECOVERY, 8,156.

Editor’s note. Enrollment counts exposure to the trials, not people whose lives were saved. No verified worldwide beneficiary count is available.

Distribution and access

ACTT-2: faster recovery among patients receiving high-flow oxygen or noninvasive ventilation.

Editor’s note. Subgroup estimates are less precise. RECOVERY detected no subgroup differences, without establishing equal benefit. The U.S. cost model excluded uninsured patients.

Duration

Trial outcomes: 28 days. COV-BARRIER also followed mortality to 60 days. Economic projections: admission and lifetime.

Editor’s note. Lifetime benefits are modeled, rather than observed follow-up. The 60-day analysis was exploratory.

Expected?

Expected: researchers deliberately sought a treatment, and mortality was a prespecified outcome.

Costs and harms

Serious adverse events in COV-BARRIER: 15% with baricitinib, 18% with placebo. RECOVERY: 12 suspected serious reactions.

Editor’s note. Short follow-up cannot exclude rare or long-term harms. The economic model’s mortality-only scenario increased hospital costs.

Measurement

Studies

ACTT-2, COV-BARRIER, RECOVERY, and Ohsfeldt et al.’s 2021 economic model.

Study design

Three randomized trials: two blinded, RECOVERY open-label. A separate economic simulation.

Measurement

Clinical assessments, follow-up forms, health records, and a simulation of treatment costs.

Setting and sample

Hospitalized patients, 2020–2021: ACTT-2, 1,033. COV-BARRIER, 1,525. RECOVERY, 8,156. Economic model: insured U.S. adults.

Measures

Recovery time, 28-day disease progression and mortality, hospital costs, and quality-adjusted life-years.

Editor’s note. Primary outcomes differed: recovery in ACTT-2, disease progression in COV-BARRIER, and mortality in RECOVERY.

Comparison

Baricitinib added to remdesivir or usual care, compared with the same care without baricitinib.

Results

ACTT-2: median recovery 7 vs. 8 days. COV-BARRIER: deaths 8% vs. 13%. RECOVERY: deaths 12% vs. 14%.

Editor’s note. Mortality was secondary in COV-BARRIER. RECOVERY’s adjusted mortality rate ratio was 0.87 (95% CI, 0.77–0.99).

Additional value from AI

Likely less manual work to find and examine relevant biomedical relationships.

Editor’s note. This is an inference from the workflow. No comparison measures time saved or AI’s share of the treatment benefit.

Limitations

COV-BARRIER missed its primary endpoint. RECOVERY was open-label. Lilly funded COV-BARRIER and the economic model.

Editor’s note. COV-BARRIER’s secondary mortality result was not conclusive under its planned procedure for multiple comparisons.

Denominators

Treatment/control: ACTT-2, 515/518. COV-BARRIER, 764/761. RECOVERY, 4,148/4,008.

Materials for replication

Trial registrations, protocols, statistical plans, appendices, and published AI methods.

Editor’s note. The complete January 2020 AI configuration and executable economic model are not public. Some economic inputs remain proprietary.

Money

Kind of value

Estimated hospital savings and lifetime treatment costs per quality-adjusted life-year.

Editor’s note. The analysis values baricitinib treatment. It does not isolate the value of AI.

Amount

US$1,932 less in net hospital costs per patient. Lifetime payer costs rise US$17,276, with 0.6703 additional quality-adjusted life-years.

Editor’s note. These are modeled results. In the mortality-only scenario, hospital costs increase US$1,978 per patient instead.

Currency and prices

U.S. dollars, using cost and payment inputs from 2020–2021. No currency conversion.

Editor’s note. Some costs were adjusted to 2020 dollars. The study does not establish one price year for every input.

Period and unit

Per patient: the hospital admission for hospital costs, a lifetime for payer costs.

Realization

Modeled estimates, without observed cash savings.

Recipients and payers

Patients gain health. Hospitals pay treatment costs. Medicare, Medicaid, and private insurers reimburse care.

Gross or net

Hospital costs net of reimbursement. Lifetime payer costs include medical care and missed work.

Editor’s note. AI costs, long COVID, COVID-related readmissions, and the value of freed hospital beds are excluded.

Included costs

Drug acquisition, inpatient and postacute care, lifetime healthcare, and missed work.

Editor’s note. AI development and operating costs were not reported. A full 14-day drug course costs US$2,114 at the model’s reference price.

Comparison

Baricitinib plus standard care versus placebo plus the same standard care.

Method

Clinical trial estimates combined with U.S. costs, a 3% annual discount rate, and sensitivity analyses.

Editor’s note. Hospital savings depend on clinical differences that were not all statistically conclusive. Some inputs remain proprietary.

Return and payback

AIBD calculation: US$16,239 in net health value per patient over a lifetime.

Editor’s note. At US$50,000 per quality-adjusted life-year: 50,000 × 0.6703 − 17,276. This is a valuation, not cash, AI return, or a payback date.

People and places

Participants

BenevolentAI: discovery. NIAID, Lilly, and Oxford: trials. FDA: authorization. WHO: guidance. Patients: beneficiaries.

Editor’s note. Clinical investigators evaluated the medicine. They were not operators of BenevolentAI’s discovery system.

Jurisdictions

AI developer and operator: United Kingdom. Trials: Argentina, Brazil, Denmark, Germany, India, Italy, Japan, Mexico, Russia, Singapore, South Korea, Spain, United Kingdom, United States.

Editor’s note. Trial results combine locations. These countries do not identify the AI system’s hosting location, which is unreported.

Ownership and control

Kenneth Mulvany controlled BenevolentAI in 2019. Calculated share of general votes: 43.18%.

Editor’s note. Calculated from the October 2019 share register. This percentage excludes nonvoting equity and does not establish majority ownership.

Dependencies

BenevolentAI graph and tools. KEGG, Reactome, STRING, SIGNOR, BioGRID, ChEMBL, CTD, GWAS Catalog, Gene Ontology, OMIM, Mondo, and SciPy.

Editor’s note. The sources do not identify the hardware, hosting, or complete graph snapshot.

Cross-border connections

Singaporean investment in U.K. research. Japanese and Italian resources informed discovery before multinational trials.

Editor’s note. Resource origins indicate knowledge reuse, not server locations or patient-data transfers. The investment funded the company, not this study alone.

Geopolitical implications

Potential improvement in U.K. pandemic preparedness through drug repurposing and international research.

Editor’s note. Editorial inference from the research sequence. National advantage, time saved, and the share attributable to AI were not measured.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Comparable hospitalized patients and background care; reproducing discovery also requires the original graph, configuration, and expert decisions.

Replication attempts

COV-BARRIER and the independent RECOVERY trial both found lower mortality.

Editor’s note. These trials tested the drug, not the discovery process. Mortality was secondary in COV-BARRIER and primary in RECOVERY.

Later findings

Mortality benefit confirmed independently in RECOVERY, July 2022.

Availability

Internal research platform; methods and clinical publications are public. Baricitinib is a separate prescription medicine.

Editor’s note. Public access to the complete historical system and its licensing price are not established.

Institutional decision

FDA: emergency treatment authorization. WHO: clinical recommendation for severe or critical COVID-19.

Grounds

FDA considered ACTT-2 and emergency-use criteria. WHO assessed clinical trials, benefits, risks, affordability, and access.

Action and result

FDA authorized baricitinib with remdesivir on 19 November 2020. WHO recommended baricitinib with corticosteroids on 14 January 2022.

Success criterion

Better survival or recovery with acceptable harms, plus access for eligible patients.

Editor’s note. The agency statements do not quantify the additional uptake or health improvement that their decisions caused.

Evidence

Source numbers

AIBD-0042-source-0001 through 0010, plus eight supplementary references.

Citations

References 1–10 below cover discovery, trials, and costs. References 11–18 support ownership, funding, and geography.

Language

English. No translation used.

Source relationships

Four developer sources, four independent sources, and two evaluations with drug-sponsor interests.

Editor’s note. Lilly funded COV-BARRIER and the economic model. ACTT-2 received medicines and protocol input from manufacturers, but no manufacturer funding.

Original evidence

FDA and WHO conclusions derive from clinical trials. Developer announcements repeat the discovery and trial findings.

Access and versions

Discovery letter corrected in June 2020; COV-BARRIER figure in September 2021; RECOVERY appendix in September 2022.

Editor’s note. Sorrell’s supplementary table and the January 2022 WHO guideline PDF were inaccessible. The original article and dated WHO announcement were available.

Supported findings

Researchers used AI to select baricitinib for testing. Clinical trials subsequently found faster recovery and fewer deaths.

Evidence links

Discovery: 3–4. Recovery: 5. Mortality: 1–2. Institutional decisions: 6–7. Treatment costs: 10.

Evidence strength

Randomized evidence for treatment effects; developer documentation for the AI-assisted discovery.

Editor’s note. No trial compared discovery with and without AI. The drug’s measured benefit cannot be assigned wholly to AI.

Missing evidence

Exact software release, AI-specific effect, realized savings, and worldwide reach are not reported.

Grades

Assessed benefits

Hospitalized patients, hospitals, and the wider population that could receive treatment.

Depth

Person: 5. Organization: 2. Society: 4.

Reasons

Fewer deaths among patients, potential hospital capacity gains, and intended prevention of severe harm across society.

Observed or intended

Patient benefit observed; hospital benefits projected; wider health benefit intended.

Rubric

AIBD’s five-level depth scale and three beneficiary groups, 25 October 2026.

Assessor and date

AIBD, with Codex assistance. 25 October 2026.

Alternative assessments

Organizational depth 2 and societal depth 4 depend on admitting potential or intended effects.

Fill

Solid fill: independently studied treatment benefit.

Editor’s note. The shared fill does not establish that projected hospital or societal benefits occurred.
