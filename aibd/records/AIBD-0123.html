NEO BCI lets a man quadriplegic for 14 years drink unaided

depth 3 for a person

drink water unaided, with grasp-decoding accuracy above 90%

54-year-old man, quadriplegic for 14 years, drank unaided by driving a pneumatic glove through the NEO epidural BCI; announced 29 Jan 2024.

What happened

On 24 October 2023, teams led by Zhao Guoguang at Xuanwu Hospital and Hong Bo at Tsinghua University implanted the NEO wireless epidural brain-computer interface in a 54-year-old man with complete C4 spinal cord injury (ASIA A) from a car accident, quadriplegic for 14 years. Discharged ten days later, he trained at home for three months. At a trial-phase summary meeting on 29 January 2024 the joint team announced that he could drive a pneumatic glove with his brain activity to drink water unaided, with grasp-decoding accuracy above 90% and improved ASIA scores and somatosensory evoked potentials. A September 2024 preprint reported nine months of home use: a 0.91 grasp-detection F1-score, eating and drinking, and a 27-point ARAT gain.

What it was built to do. NEO was built to let people with tetraplegia control external equipment, including a pneumatic exoskeleton glove, from epidural brain signals, for assistance and hand-function rehabilitation at home; restoring this patient's grasp so that he could drink was exactly that purpose.

The system's part

The AI contribution is the decoder: a Riemannian-geometry classifier, calibrated in about ten minutes on beta, low-gamma and high-gamma features of eight-channel epidural ECoG, with a hidden Markov model for temporal smoothing, that detects grasp intention and triggers the pneumatic glove. The preprint reports 94% calibration accuracy, a grasp-detection F1-score rising above 0.9 after three months (average 0.91 over nine months), and 100% object-transfer success with the BCI against 35% without it. The electrodes, implant, surgery and glove are not AI; the authors attribute the later neurological recovery to repeated BCI training, not to the decoder alone.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. Eight chronic epidural electrodes over the sensorimotor cortex of a patient whose cortex still generated grasp-related signals after 14 years of complete C4 injury. A grasp-intention decoder calibrated in about ten minutes and robust to the signal drift and chewing artifacts of home use. A battery-free implant powered and read through the scalp, so the patient could train at home daily after discharge ten days post-surgery. A wearable pneumatic glove able to execute the decoded grasp on the patient's right hand, which retained limited forearm movement.

What must hold again. The patient's motor cortex must still produce decodable grasp intention despite years of paralysis. The decoder must stay accurate without frequent recalibration across months of home use. The patient or a caregiver must be able to attach the external coil and the glove for daily sessions. Neurological recovery beyond assisted grasping is a single-patient finding and must be reproduced in further patients.
