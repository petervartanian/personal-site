NEO brain implant lets a man quadriplegic for 14 years drink unaided

The NEO wireless epidural brain-computer interface let a 54-year-old man, quadriplegic for 14 years, drink unaided by driving a pneumatic glove; the research team announced this on 29 January 2024.

  • 3 a person

    After 14 years of complete quadriplegia the patient regained the ability to drink and eat by himself through the glove that the interface drove. The authors of the preprint report neurological recovery: His upper-limb motor score rose by 5 points, and his score on the Action Research Arm Test rose by 27 points. He could also hold objects without the interface.

Depth runs from 1, a convenience or time saved, to 5, a life or a livelihood preserved.

The system’s part
The AI contribution is the decoder, a Riemannian-geometry classifier with a hidden Markov model for temporal smoothing. Calibrated in about ten minutes on beta, low-gamma and high-gamma features of eight-channel epidural ECoG, it detects grasp intention and triggers the pneumatic glove. The preprint reports 94% calibration accuracy and a grasp-detection F1-score rising above 0.9 after three months (average 0.91 over nine months). It gives 100% object-transfer success with the BCI against 35% without. The electrodes, implant, surgery and glove are not AI. The authors attribute the later neurological recovery to repeated BCI training, not the decoder alone.
Limits
One participant without a control group and repeated training do not establish that the decoder alone caused his neurological recovery. The F1-score and the success in object transfer measure different endpoints from daily independence or lasting quality of life. Scientific American reports that the commercial approval covers only people who still have some upper arm function.
Costs and harms
The system restored a useful grasp through a limited, repeatable command, and the participant's retained forearm movement helped him position objects. He or a caregiver had to attach the external coil and the glove for each session.
Later
Scaled up, 13 March 2026. Scientific American reported on 13 March 2026 that China approved Neuracle's brain implant for commercial use in people with spinal cord injuries. It reported that the approval covers only people who keep some upper arm function. Nature reported on 16 March 2026 that the device was the first brain-computer interface available outside clinical trials.

What happened

On 24 October 2023, teams led by Zhao Guoguang (Xuanwu Hospital) and Hong Bo (Tsinghua University) implanted the NEO wireless epidural brain-computer interface. The patient was a 54-year-old man with complete C4 spinal cord injury (ASIA A) from a car accident, quadriplegic for 14 years. He was discharged ten days later and trained at home for three months. At a trial-phase summary meeting on 29 January 2024, the team reported that he could drive a pneumatic glove with his brain activity to drink water unaided, with grasp-decoding accuracy above 90%. The team also reported improved ASIA scores and somatosensory evoked potentials. In a preprint of September 2024, the authors reported nine months of home use. They reported a grasp-detection F1-score of 0.91, eating and drinking, and a gain of 27 points on the Action Research Arm Test. On 13 March 2026 Scientific American reported that China approved Neuracle's implant for commercial use in people with spinal cord injuries. It reported that the approval covers only people who still have some upper arm function.

Details

AI system

Model

A classifier based on Riemannian geometry, with a hidden Markov model for temporal smoothing.

Role

The decoder detected intended grasping movements and triggered the pneumatic glove.

Configuration

The deployment used the NEO brain-computer interface.

Inputs and outputs

Eight epidural electrodes recorded grasp-related signals, and a classifier with temporal smoothing converted detected grasp intention into pneumatic-glove activation.

Workflow

Eight chronic epidural electrodes over the sensorimotor cortex of a patient whose cortex still generated grasp-related signals after 14 years of complete C4 injury. A grasp-intention decoder calibrated in about ten minutes and robust to the signal drift and chewing artifacts of home use. A battery-free implant powered and read through the scalp, so the patient could train at home daily after discharge ten days post-surgery. A wearable pneumatic glove able to execute the decoded grasp on the patient's right hand, which retained limited forearm movement.

Human oversight

Clinical teams implanted and calibrated the interface. At home, the participant or a caregiver had to attach the external coil and glove for training.

Benefits

Reach

One person benefited: the 54-year-old man implanted on 24 October 2023. A second patient received the implant at Tiantan Hospital on 19 December 2023, and no outcome is reported for that patient. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]

Duration

The later preprint reported nine months of home use with an average grasp-detection F1 score of 0.91.

Measurement

Measurement

Grasp-detection F1-score, object transfer tests, upper limb motor score, Action Research Arm Test and somatosensory evoked potentials over nine months of home use. [1]

Setting and sample

One man with complete cervical spinal cord injury, implanted at Xuanwu Hospital and trained at home. [1]

Measures

Grasp-detection F1-score; ARAT score. [1]

Results

Average F1-score of 0.91, 100% success in object transfer tests, a 5-point gain in upper limb motor score and a 27-point gain in ARAT. [1]

Additional value from AI

The decoder detects grasp intention and triggers the glove; the study does not separate the decoder's share of the neurological recovery from that of repeated training. [1]

Limitations

A single patient; the authors are the system's developers and clinicians; the preprint has not been peer reviewed. [1]

Denominators

Nine months of home use. [1]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The patient's motor cortex must still produce decodable grasp intention despite years of paralysis. The decoder must stay accurate without frequent recalibration across months of home use. The patient or a caregiver must be able to attach the external coil and the glove for daily sessions. Neurological recovery beyond assisted grasping is a single-patient finding and must be reproduced in further patients.

Sector

Purpose

NEO was built to let people with tetraplegia control external equipment, including a pneumatic exoskeleton glove, from epidural brain signals. The aim is assistance and hand-function rehabilitation at home. Restoring this patient's grasp so that he could drink was that purpose.
