What happened

Jonathan Stokes, Regina Barzilay, James Collins and colleagues at MIT, the Broad Institute, Harvard and McMaster University published in Cell on 20 February 2020. They reported halicin, a molecule identified by a directed-message-passing neural network trained on 2,335 molecules to predict inhibition of E. coli growth. Halicin, originally investigated as a kinase inhibitor for diabetes, is structurally divergent from conventional antibiotics. It reduced Acinetobacter baumannii in a mouse wound model and cleared Clostridioides difficile in a mouse infection model. Independent laboratories at KACST in Riyadh (December 2021) and Central South University in Changsha (September 2022) confirmed antibacterial activity in vitro. The latter also in a mouse abscess model. No clinical use or human trial result appears in any source on file.

What it was built to do. Built to predict molecules that inhibit bacterial growth and surface antibiotic candidates. Halicin was that purpose.

The system's part

The neural network's prediction selected halicin for testing from compounds not previously recognized as antibiotics. Wet-lab experiments by the authors and later by independent groups confirmed the activity. The outcome so far is a laboratory and animal-model result.

Replication

What produced it. A growth-inhibition training set of a few thousand molecules. A message-passing network ranking large chemical libraries. Wet-lab confirmation of predicted actives and animal-model testing.

What must hold again. Predicted compounds must be available for synthesis or purchase. Activity must be confirmed experimentally. A verified real-world outcome would require clinical use.
