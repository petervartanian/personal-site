MIT neural network finds halicin, active in vitro and in mice

A deep-learning screen identified halicin, active in vitro and in mice and confirmed by other labs; no human outcome.

ⓘ

What happened

Jonathan Stokes, Regina Barzilay, James Collins and colleagues at MIT, the Broad Institute, Harvard and McMaster University published in Cell on 20 February 2020. They reported halicin, a molecule identified by a directed-message-passing neural network trained on 2,335 molecules to predict inhibition of E. coli growth. Halicin, originally investigated as a kinase inhibitor for diabetes, is structurally divergent from conventional antibiotics. It reduced Acinetobacter baumannii in a mouse wound model and cleared Clostridioides difficile in a mouse infection model. Independent laboratories at KACST in Riyadh (December 2021) and Central South University in Changsha (September 2022) confirmed antibacterial activity in vitro. The latter also in a mouse abscess model. No clinical use or human trial result appears in any cited source.

Purpose. Built to predict molecules that inhibit bacterial growth and surface antibiotic candidates. Halicin was that purpose.

AI’s contribution

The neural network's prediction selected halicin for testing from compounds not previously recognized as antibiotics. Wet-lab experiments by the authors and later by independent groups confirmed the activity. The outcome so far is a laboratory and animal-model result.

Basis
Measured.Excitingly, we observed that halicin resulted in C. difficile clearance at a greater rate than vehicle or the antibiotic metronidazole (Figure 5F), which is not only a first-line treatment for C. difficile infection, but also the antibiotic most similar to halicin based on Tanimoto score (Figure 2H, Table S2H). Indeed, halicin resulted in sterilization of 3 out of 4 mice after 72 hr of treatment, and 4 out of 4 mice after 96 hr of treatment. source
Since then
An independent laboratory at KACST in Riyadh confirmed halicin's in vitro activity against gram-positive and gram-negative strains, including a multidrug-resistant A. baumannii isolate; the compound remains preclinical.The new antibacterial agent was able to inhibit all tested gram-positive and gram-negative bacterial strains, but in different concentrations—including the A. baumannii multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolate. source

Further evidence

Setting. A growth-inhibition training set of a few thousand molecules. A message-passing network ranking large chemical libraries. Wet-lab confirmation of predicted actives and animal-model testing.

Conditions. Predicted compounds must be available for synthesis or purchase. Activity must be confirmed experimentally. A verified real-world outcome would require clinical use.
