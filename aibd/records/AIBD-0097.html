Facebook's settlement maps shrink the area that volunteers map in Malawi

Facebook's machine-learning settlement maps cut the area that Red Cross volunteers had to map for Malawi's June 2017 measles vaccination campaign from 94,080 km² to 2,745 km².

What happened

Malawi's June 2017 measles vaccination campaign (Measles & Rubella Initiative) targeted more than 7.5 million children. The American Red Cross's Missing Maps team needed base maps for campaign monitoring. Facebook's population maps come from a machine-learning computer-vision method that detects buildings in satellite imagery. The team used them to identify where people lived and which areas were already mapped in OpenStreetMap. Concentrating volunteers on settlements filtered out 97.1% of the land area, reducing the area to map from 94,080 km² to 2,745 km². Volunteers completed the base map in a fraction of the normal time. The Red Cross article of 7 June 2017 predates the campaign and says that the base map was to feed a dashboard for government and Red Cross monitors. On 10 August 2017 the Missing Maps blog reported that the traced base map underpinned a real-time monitoring dashboard covering 13,743 children. The blog says that 3,000 local Malawi Red Cross volunteers made house-to-house visits in five districts, and it credits the Facebook data with faster remote tracing. No source reports an effect of the maps on vaccination coverage. In their own validation, the authors from Facebook and the Center for International Earth Science Information Network (CIESIN) report a recall of 0.84 for the layer in a rural test area near Blantyre that Missing Maps volunteers had mapped by hand. In April 2019 Facebook restated the case and credited its maps with letting the Red Cross deploy 3,000 volunteers to specific areas.

Details

AI system

Application developer

Facebook Inc. With CIESIN, Columbia University, and the World Bank Development Data Group

Inputs and outputs

Building detection in satellite images produced settlement maps. The Missing Maps team compared those maps with OpenStreetMap coverage and directed volunteers to inhabited areas that still needed mapping.

Workflow

Facebook settlement detection from satellite imagery. Missing Maps volunteer tracing into OpenStreetMap. Comparison with existing OpenStreetMap coverage. Mobile data-collection dashboard for campaign monitors.

Human oversight

Missing Maps volunteers traced the settlements into OpenStreetMap, and the maps directed where they worked. The authors from Facebook and the Center for International Earth Science Information Network checked the layer against a rural test area near Blantyre that volunteers had mapped by hand. They report a recall of 0.84. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Relation to AI

The Facebook settlement data determined where volunteer mapping effort went and which areas were skipped. The campaign itself was a Measles & Rubella Initiative effort supported by the American Red Cross and other partners, with monitors from the national government. The Red Cross attributes efficiency, not coverage, to the maps. The Red Cross account is the implementing organization's and names only “Facebook's population datasets”, without mentioning machine learning. The identification with the High Resolution Settlement Layer and the machine-learning description are taken from the vendor-authored Tiecke et al. Preprint. It does not mention the Malawi campaign.

Benefits

Beneficiaries

Remote mappers and campaign planners directly gained a smaller, more useful mapping task. Children were the campaign's intended beneficiaries, but the maps' contribution to additional vaccinations was not measured.

Reach

The campaign targeted more than 7.5 million children, and the monitoring dashboard covered 13,743 children. No source counts children who benefited from the maps. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Duration

The cut in mapping area was a one-time result for the June 2017 campaign. On 10 August 2017 the Missing Maps team reported that the base map underpinned a real-time monitoring dashboard covering 13,743 children. In April 2019 Facebook restated the case. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Imagery recent enough to reflect current settlements. Building-detection accuracy in rural Malawi. Volunteer and local mapping capacity.
