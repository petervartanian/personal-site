What happened

A case report in Case Reports in Ophthalmological Medicine (author at Vanderbilt University Medical Center; published 1 September 2025) describes comprehensive low-vision rehabilitation. The patient was a 62-year-old woman with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa, best-corrected acuity 20/40–2 and severely constricted visual fields in both eyes. The rehabilitation included optical aids, white cane training and counseling. After the evaluation visit she was referred to Tennessee's blind and independent living skills training program. At the insistence of her independent living skills trainer she met a Technology Access Program specialist. She downloaded free iPhone apps, among them volunteer-based Be My Eyes and Seeing AI. Seeing AI uses the phone camera to turn printed material into auditory output. The report does not name Seeing AI's maker.

The Microsoft attribution rests on the app's App Store listing. At a later meeting of the clinic's bimonthly support group, she reported using both applications in the kitchen. Seeing AI helped her access printed recipes. The author declares no conflicts of interest. Visit dates are not given, so the publication date is used.

What it was built to do. Read printed text aloud for people with visual impairment using computer vision

The system's part

Seeing AI's computer-vision text recognition is what converts the printed recipe into speech. The report lists it alongside non-AI aids (Be My Eyes volunteers, optical devices). It attributes recipe access specifically to Seeing AI.

Replication

What produced it. Smartphone camera text recognition read aloud. Introduction of the app during structured low-vision rehabilitation. Task-specific use in a familiar environment.

What must hold again. Patient can aim a phone camera at text. Printed material of legible font and contrast. Smartphone ownership and training.
