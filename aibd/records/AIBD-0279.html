FamilySearch and Transkribus make handwritten records searchable

FamilySearch Full-Text Search converts handwritten records into searchable text, and a genealogist used it to help transcribe a 1761 deed; Transkribus claims 200 million pages deciphered.

What happened

FamilySearch introduced Full-Text Search through FamilySearch Labs in 2024. The feature uses handwriting recognition and optical character recognition. Transkribus, developed by the European cooperative READ-COOP SCE, says on its site that it “turns centuries of handwriting into text you can search, study, and share”. It claims over 500,000 users and 200 million pages deciphered. It cites projects such as the University of Georgia transcribing 20,000 pages in two months and Archion's portal of over 200,000 German church books. Historians at Wilfrid Laurier University measured Transkribus on 50 pages of 18th and 19th century English handwriting (arXiv, 2 November 2024; Historical Methods, 26 May 2025). The two Transkribus models read 90 to 92% of characters and 73 to 80% of words correctly, and commercial large language models did better. The newsroom of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints reviewed FamilySearch's year 2024 on 2 January 2025. It says that FamilySearch “can convert an image of text into a machine-readable text format, allowing users to search the full text of the records”. It reports that 2.5 billion searchable records and images were added overall in 2024. It gives no example of a user discovery. FamilySearch's year-end review for 2025, reproduced on 9 January 2026, says that Full-Text Search was fully released from FamilySearch Labs in 2025, alongside 2.2 billion newly searchable names and images. Its plans for 2026, reported on 13 January 2026, are to extend the handwriting-reading technology to additional languages. On 26 October 2026 the genealogist Randy Seaver published a 1761 deed of an ancestor that he had transcribed with help from FamilySearch Full-Text Search, with a few errors corrected. No independent source documents a specific discovery, reunion or entitlement from either system.

The authors are historians at Wilfrid Laurier University, funded by the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada, and they declare no competing interests; they built a competing open-source tool that uses commercial LLMs. They found that the two Transkribus models read 90 to 92% of characters and 73 to 80% of words correctly on 18th and 19th century English handwriting, and that LLMs did better.

    This means that on the task of achieving a perfect transcription, the Transkribus models correctly transcribed between 90 and 92% of the characters and 73 and 80% of the words in the ground truth document.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

Images of handwritten or printed records were converted into searchable text.

Workflow

Handwriting-recognition models applied to digitized record images. Indexing of recognized text for search.

Human oversight

A genealogist reports that he corrected a few errors in the transcription of a 1761 deed that he made with help from Full-Text Search. No source describes other safeguards. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Benefits

Reach

Transkribus claims over 500,000 users and 200 million pages deciphered, and both figures come from the maker. FamilySearch gives no user count for Full-Text Search, and one genealogist reports using it. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Measurement

Measurement

Character and word error rates of machine transcriptions against manually corrected ground truth, each model run ten times on the test set. [4]

Setting and sample

50 pages of 18th and 19th century English-language handwritten documents, 500 pages per model per test. [4]

Measures

Character error rate and word error rate. [4]

Results

Transkribus Titan: CER 8.0%, WER 19.7% (PyLaia: CER 10.3%, WER 27.0%); LLMs: CER 5.7 to 7%. [4]

Limitations

One English-language corpus; the authors built a competing LLM-based tool; results apply to Transkribus base models without fine-tuning; FamilySearch's own engine was not tested. [4]
