Smartwatch ECG captures 165 bpm tachycardia; Emory diagnoses VT

A smartwatch ECG recorded a 165 bpm wide-complex tachycardia during palpitations; VT was confirmed on telemetry, an ICD placed and later shocked VT, and ablation stopped the VT (JACC Case Rep 2025).

ⓘ

What happened

Guynn, Regis, Eckert, D'souza, Turbow, Westerman, Sperling and Lloyd are in Emory University School of Medicine's Division of Cardiology, Atlanta. They reported in JACC: Case Reports (published online 12 March 2025; event dates not reported) on a 64-year-old man. He had a bioprosthetic aortic valve replaced at 55 and a month of intermittent palpitations and near-syncope. During symptoms he used his smartwatch's electrocardiogram (ECG) function. The watch captured a 10-second single-lead ECG and reported a heart rate above 150 beats/min, warning him to speak to a doctor. The tracing showed wide complex tachycardia at 165 beats/min. On admission he was in sinus rhythm at 77 bpm. But telemetry and ECG caught multiple episodes of monomorphic ventricular tachycardia, and he went to intensive cardiac care.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) suggested scar or late myocarditis at the basal septum. An electrophysiology study found easily inducible VT. A secondary-prevention ICD was implanted and he was discharged on metoprolol, spironolactone and dapagliflozin. Three months later the ICD shocked him for VT. Device interrogation showed pace-terminated episodes, some degenerating into ventricular fibrillation. Successful ablation below the aortic prosthesis followed, with no further VT on serial interrogations. The authors write VT would have gone undiagnosed had the clinician not considered the smartwatch data. The watch's brand and model are unstated.

Purpose. Smartwatch ECG apps are built to record a single-lead trace and warn of high heart rates. Capturing the VT during symptoms was that function. The authors note no smartwatch algorithm is validated to detect VT and the diagnosis came from clinicians reading the trace.

AI’s contribution

The device's contribution was recording a user-triggered single-lead ECG and issuing a generic high-heart-rate warning. It did not classify the rhythm as VT, which the authors say no wearable algorithm is validated to do. Clinicians read the QRS morphology on the smartwatch trace and on the 12-lead ECG, and made every treatment decision. The brand is unknown.

Beneficiaries

  • a person

    The captured trace led to a VT diagnosis, an ICD that later delivered shocks for VT degenerating into ventricular fibrillation, and a curative ablation. The report presents the ICD decision as vindicated by those shocks. So the benefit reaches a life-threatening arrhythmia, though the counterfactual is the authors' judgment.

Further evidence

Setting. A wearer who activates the watch's ECG recording during a transient symptomatic episode. A clinician who reviews the single-lead trace rather than dismissing consumer data. Access to telemetry, cardiac MRI, electrophysiology study, ICD implantation and ablation.

Conditions. The arrhythmia occurs while the wearer is able to trigger a recording (the watch has no continuous ECG). The QRS morphology on a single lead is clear enough for a clinician to suspect VT and pursue confirmation.

  1. Benefit reported again

    Three earlier published cases of VT diagnosed from a smartwatch recording, each leading to an electrophysiology study and ICD, ablation or both, as summarized in this report's discussion
