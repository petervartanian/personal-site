Smartwatch ECG captures 165 bpm tachycardia; Emory diagnoses VT

A smartwatch ECG function of an unnamed brand recorded a 165 bpm wide-complex tachycardia during palpitations, and cardiologists diagnosed ventricular tachycardia and implanted a defibrillator.

A successful ablation followed, with no further ventricular tachycardia on serial interrogations. A life-threatening arrhythmia was diagnosed and resolved in time.

Brief from Peter

In a case report published online on 12 March 2025, authors in the Division of Cardiology at Emory University School of Medicine in Atlanta describe a 64-year-old man with a replaced aortic valve. During palpitations he recorded a single-lead electrocardiogram on his smartwatch, which flagged a heart rate above 150 beats per minute with a warning to speak to a doctor. Clinicians read a wide-complex tachycardia at 165 beats per minute on the trace, and hospital telemetry then recorded episodes of ventricular tachycardia. Doctors implanted a defibrillator, and three months after his discharge it shocked him for ventricular tachycardia. After an ablation, later checks of the device showed no further episodes. The watch did not classify the rhythm, and the authors note that no smartwatch algorithm is validated to detect ventricular tachycardia. The diagnosis came from clinicians. The report does not name the watch's brand or model. The authors write that his ventricular tachycardia would have gone undiagnosed had his clinician not considered the smartwatch data.1 In a paper published online on 18 November 2025, cardiologists in Haifa with no tie to the Emory team cite the case and pool 20 published patients whose ventricular arrhythmias were captured by smartwatches.2

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

The user-triggered recording captured a short single-lead ECG and returned a high-heart-rate warning; it did not label the rhythm as ventricular tachycardia.

Workflow

A wearer who activates the watch's ECG recording during a transient symptomatic episode. A clinician who reviews the single-lead trace rather than dismissing consumer data. Access to telemetry, cardiac MRI, electrophysiology study, ICD implantation and ablation.

Human oversight

Clinicians read the smartwatch trace and confirmed ventricular tachycardia with hospital monitoring and electrophysiology testing.

Benefits

Reach

One patient benefited in this case. The systematic review of November 2025 pools 20 published patients whose ventricular arrhythmias were captured by smartwatches. [1] [2]

Duration

Three months after discharge the defibrillator shocked the patient for ventricular tachycardia. A successful ablation followed, and serial interrogations showed no further ventricular tachycardia. [1] [2]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The arrhythmia occurs while the wearer is able to trigger a recording (the watch has no continuous ECG). The QRS morphology on a single lead is clear enough for a clinician to suspect VT and pursue confirmation.
