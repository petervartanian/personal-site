Smartwatch ECG captures 165 bpm tachycardia; Emory diagnoses VT

recorded a 165 bpm wide-complex tachycardia during palpitations

A smartwatch ECG recorded a 165 bpm wide-complex tachycardia during palpitations; VT was confirmed on telemetry, an ICD placed and later shocked VT, and ablation stopped the VT (JACC Case Rep 2025).

What happened

Guynn, Regis, Eckert, D'souza, Turbow, Westerman, Sperling and Lloyd of the Division of Cardiology, Emory University School of Medicine, Atlanta, reported in JACC: Case Reports (published online 12 March 2025; the event dates are not given, so the publication date is used) a 64-year-old man with a bioprosthetic aortic valve replaced at 55 who had a month of intermittent palpitations and near-syncope. He activated the ECG function on his smartwatch during symptoms; the watch captured a 10-second single-lead ECG, reported a heart rate above 150 beats/min with a warning to speak to a doctor, and the tracing showed a wide complex tachycardia at 165 beats/min. On admission he was in sinus rhythm at 77 bpm, but telemetry and ECG captured multiple episodes of monomorphic ventricular tachycardia and he was moved to intensive cardiac care. Cardiac MRI suggested scar or late myocarditis at the basal septum; an electrophysiology study found easily inducible VT; a secondary-prevention ICD was implanted and he was discharged on metoprolol, spironolactone and dapagliflozin. Three months later the ICD shocked him for VT, device interrogation showing pace-terminated episodes, some degenerating into ventricular fibrillation; he then underwent successful ablation below the aortic prosthesis, with no further VT on serial interrogations. The authors write that VT would have gone undiagnosed had the clinician not considered the smartwatch data. The watch's brand and model are not stated.

What it was built to do. Smartwatch ECG apps are built to record a single-lead trace and warn of high heart rates; capturing the VT during symptoms was that function, although the authors note no smartwatch algorithm is validated to detect VT and the diagnosis came from clinicians reading the trace.

The system's part

The device's contribution was recording a user-triggered single-lead ECG and issuing a generic high-heart-rate warning; it did not classify the rhythm as VT, which the authors say no wearable algorithm is validated to do. Clinicians read the QRS morphology on the smartwatch trace and on the 12-lead ECG, and made every treatment decision. The brand is unknown.

Replication

What produced it

  • A wearer who activates the watch's ECG recording during a transient symptomatic episode
  • A clinician who reviews the single-lead trace rather than dismissing consumer data
  • Access to telemetry, cardiac MRI, electrophysiology study, ICD implantation and ablation

What must hold again

  • The arrhythmia occurs while the wearer is able to trigger a recording (the watch has no continuous ECG)
  • The QRS morphology on a single lead is clear enough for a clinician to suspect VT and pursue confirmation
