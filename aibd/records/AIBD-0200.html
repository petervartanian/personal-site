AI teams out-earn growers in Wageningen's greenhouse challenges

Teams' AI algorithms out-earned Dutch growers in Wageningen's greenhouse challenges; the 2018 winner raised sellable cucumber yield 6% and net profit 17%, and all five 2020 teams beat the growers.

  • 2 an organization

    In 2018 the winning algorithm's compartment produced 6% more sellable cucumber than the growers' compartment, and in 2020 every algorithm-run compartment reached a higher net profit than the reference. These are measured results in experimental compartments, and no grower's own accounts are reported.

  • Not rated society

    Resource-use differences are reported per compartment; no wider outcome is documented.

The system’s part
Teams' algorithms set climate and crop setpoints remotely and their compartments out-earned a single manually run reference compartment in both editions. The organizers (WUR) authored the peer-reviewed reports and are independent of the competing teams. Results come from a sponsored competition, not a production deployment.
Limits
The profit comparisons use one human reference compartment and the organizers' prices and costs. Experimental growing performance does not establish returns across commercial farms, and the single winning cucumber team should not be generalized to all competitors.

What happened

Wageningen University & Research runs the Autonomous Greenhouse Challenge in six identical 96 m² high-tech compartments at Bleiswijk, and the organizing team publishes the results. In the first challenge in 2018, five international teams grew cucumber remotely for four months. They set climate, irrigation and crop setpoints with their own AI algorithms. One team outperformed the manually operated reference. The organizers' 2020 paper reports that the winning algorithm increased commercially sellable production by 6% and net profit by 17% over the reference growers. In the second challenge, cherry tomato cv. Axiany was transplanted on 16 December 2019. Five teams (Automatoes, AiCU, DIGILOG, IUA.CAAS, The Automators) took remote control on 20 December 2019. Harvest ran from 13 February to 29 May 2020. Net profit ranged from 3.10 to 6.86 €/m², and all AI-operated compartments exceeded the Dutch growers' reference compartment. Compartment 306 (Automatoes) had the highest income (37.2 €/m²) and the highest net profit, while the reference had high energy and labor costs. The challenge was funded by Tencent. Net profit is computed from the organizers' price and cost model in an experimental facility. No commercial farm took part. Kenneth Tran, who led the 2018 winning team, wrote in March 2021 that he had founded Koidra in 2020 to build on the result. A third edition grew lettuce in 2023. The fourth challenge began on 3 September 2024 with dwarf tomato. Its best algorithm earned 0.34 euro per square meter per day, more than twice the 0.13 earned by a reference team made up of the organizers. Three of the five teams earned the same as the reference or less.

Details

AI system

Autonomy

Teams' algorithms remotely set greenhouse climate, irrigation, and crop-management targets, while greenhouse equipment implemented those setpoints.

Workflow

Six identical 96 m² compartments with heating, ventilation, screening, lighting, fogging, CO2, water and nutrient actuators. Teams combining horticultural and AI expertise setting setpoints via a digital interface. A reference compartment run manually by Dutch growers.

Benefits

Reach

No beneficiary count applies. Each edition compares the AI-run compartments of five teams with one reference compartment in the same experimental facility. [1] [2] [3]

Duration

Each result covers one growing season: four months of cucumber in 2018, and a cherry tomato harvest from 13 February to 29 May 2020. In the fourth challenge, which began on 3 September 2024, the best algorithm again out-earned the reference, while three of the five teams earned the same as the reference or less. [1] [2] [3]

People and places

Dependencies

The challenge required instrumented greenhouse compartments, controllable cultivation equipment, a digital interface, and continuous sensor exchange with the competing teams.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The organizers' simulated price and cost model for net profit. Sensor data exchanged with teams throughout the season.
