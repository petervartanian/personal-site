PAWS-directed patrols remove over 1,000 snares in Cambodia

PAWS-directed ranger patrols in Srepok Wildlife Sanctuary removed over 1,000 snares in December 2018, versus 200–300 in a typical month.

ⓘ

What happened

PAWS (Protection Assistant for Wildlife Security) is a machine-learning pipeline that predicts poaching risk and plans patrols. It was field-tested with WWF Cambodia and Ministry of Environment rangers in Srepok Wildlife Sanctuary, Cambodia, from December 2018. Only the month is given, so the first day is used. Rangers received center coordinates of fifteen 3 x 3 km blocks chosen from PAWS risk predictions, without being told each block's risk class. In the first month, 72 rangers in teams of eight detected poaching in 32 cells and removed over one thousand snares. They had reported removing two or three hundred in an average month. Low-risk blocks yielded no poaching. A second trial ran February to March 2019.

Purpose. PAWS was built to predict where poaching will occur and to prescribe patrol routes that maximize detection and removal of snares. The snare removals are exactly this intended benefit.

AI’s contribution

The AI's output (risk-ranked 3 x 3 km blocks) determined where patrols went. Rangers were blinded to risk class. High-risk blocks yielded detections while low-risk blocks yielded none. The paper itself cautions that the first QENP test's increased findings “may be partially explained by factors beyond the predictive model”. The 1,000-versus-200–300 comparison relies on rangers' reported averages, not a controlled baseline. The peer-reviewed paper is written by the system's academic developers together with staff of the operating organizations (WWF, WCS, Uganda Wildlife Authority). There is no commercial vendor.

Beneficiaries

  • an organization

    The ranger force's monthly snare removals rose from a reported two or three hundred to over one thousand. A material gain in patrol effectiveness for the operating team. A chi-squared test confirmed predictions discriminated risk (p = 0.023).

  • society

    Over one thousand snares were removed from a sanctuary identified as a tiger-reintroduction site. The paper states each snare “represents the life of one animal potentially saved”. A material landscape-scale conservation benefit rather than a documented population-level change.

Since then
By November 2020 PAWS had been incorporated into the SMART conservation software used in parks worldwide, and Srepok rangers reported finding 5 to 20 times more snares since adopting it.PAWS has been incorporated into the latest update to SMART, which is already used widely by rangers and other park employees to gather data and track observations of illegal activity in parks around the world. source

Further evidence

Setting. Historical SMART patrol data (2015–2018) sufficient to train a risk model despite 3.6% positive labels. Rangers willing to patrol the suggested blocks and record findings in SMART. Dry-season deployment when rivers are passable and poaching concentrates in the north of the sanctuary.

Conditions. Patrol capacity exists to act on predictions (SWS ranger numbers had more than doubled since 2016). Poacher behavior remains spatially predictable from past detections. Findings are measured against a comparable baseline. The paper notes increases may partly reflect factors beyond the model.
