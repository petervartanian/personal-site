PAWS-directed ranger patrols in Srepok Wildlife Sanctuary removed over 1,000 snares in December 2018, versus 200-300 in a typical month.

What happened

PAWS (Protection Assistant for Wildlife Security), a machine-learning pipeline that predicts poaching risk and plans patrols, was field-tested with WWF Cambodia and Ministry of Environment rangers in Srepok Wildlife Sanctuary, Cambodia, from December 2018 (only the month is given, so the first day is used). Rangers received center coordinates of fifteen 3 x 3 km blocks chosen from PAWS risk predictions, without being told each block's risk class. In the first month, 72 rangers in teams of eight detected poaching in 32 cells and removed over one thousand snares, against the two or three hundred they reported removing in an average month. Low-risk blocks yielded no poaching. A second trial ran February to March 2019.

What it was built to do. PAWS was built to predict where poaching will occur and to prescribe patrol routes that maximize detection and removal of snares; the snare removals are exactly this intended benefit.

Who benefited

    The ranger force's monthly snare removals rose from a reported two or three hundred to over one thousand, a material gain in patrol effectiveness for the operating team, with a chi-squared test confirming predictions discriminated risk (p = 0.023).

    Over one thousand snares were removed from a sanctuary identified as a tiger-reintroduction site; the paper states each snare 'represents the life of one animal potentially saved', a material landscape-scale conservation benefit rather than a documented population-level change.

The system's part

The AI's output (risk-ranked 3 x 3 km blocks) determined where patrols went; rangers were blinded to risk class, and high-risk blocks yielded detections while low-risk blocks yielded none. The paper itself cautions that the first QENP test's increased findings 'may be partially explained by factors beyond the predictive model', and the 1,000-versus-200-300 comparison relies on rangers' reported averages, not a controlled baseline. The peer-reviewed paper is written by the system's academic developers together with staff of the operating organizations (WWF, WCS, Uganda Wildlife Authority); there is no commercial vendor.

Replication

What produced it. Historical SMART patrol data (2015-2018) sufficient to train a risk model despite 3.6% positive labels. Rangers willing to patrol the suggested blocks and record findings in SMART. Dry-season deployment when rivers are passable and poaching concentrates in the north of the sanctuary.

What must hold again. Patrol capacity exists to act on predictions (SWS ranger numbers had more than doubled since 2016). Poacher behavior remains spatially predictable from past detections. Findings are measured against a comparable baseline; the paper notes increases may partly reflect factors beyond the model.
