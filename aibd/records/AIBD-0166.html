Flint uses predictions to find hazardous water pipes

ActiveRemediation helped Flint prioritize homes for inspection and replacement of lead or galvanized-steel service lines.

Benefits

Benefit

The model helped direct inspections toward homes likely to have hazardous pipes. [1]

Category

Improved accuracy in locating hazardous service lines. [1]

Status

The system was used in the field. Accuracy was tested retrospectively, and monetary savings were modeled. [1]

Beneficiary groups

Residents and the city’s water-infrastructure program. [1]

Beneficiaries

Residents whose hazardous service lines were identified and replaced. [1]

Reach

By September 2017, the program had replaced hazardous lines at 4,419 homes and identified pipe materials at 6,506. [1]

Distribution and access

Predictions varied by address and neighborhood. The benefit depended on whether officials used them to set priorities. [1]

Duration

Replacement permanently changed the identified service lines. The paper did not measure residents’ later health. [1]

Expected?

Finding and removing hazardous pipes was the intended benefit. [1]

Costs and harms

Residents and officials also sought broad coverage and certainty about individual homes. These priorities could change the order of excavation. [2]

AI system

System

ActiveRemediation. [1]

Model

XGBoost classification trees with a hierarchical Bayesian model for spatial patterns. An active-learning policy selected further inspections. [1]

Release

The paper names the algorithms but does not identify an XGBoost release or a reproducible deployment configuration. [1]

Provider

A research team from the University of Michigan, Georgia Tech, and Brigham Young University. [1]

Role

The system estimated pipe materials and ranked homes for further investigation. [1]

Configuration

A shared database and a password-protected Flask application let city staff and contractors review maps and add excavation results. [1]

Inputs and outputs

Parcel records, property characteristics, and confirmed pipe materials produced address-level probabilities and inspection priorities. [1]

Autonomy

The system recommended priorities. City officials and contractors decided where to excavate and replaced the pipes. [1]

Workflow

Crews inspected pipes and recorded their materials. Researchers used those results to update predictions and recommend further inspections. [1]

Human oversight

City officials set priorities, contractors confirmed pipe materials, and researchers maintained the model. [1]

Relation to AI

The system used machine learning to predict pipe materials and guide inspections. [1]

AI contribution

Predictions informed inspection and replacement priorities. The paper did not randomize the city’s decisions. [1]

Measurement

Studies

AIBD-0166-E01, AIBD-0166-E02 [1]

Study design

Researchers tested predictions on held-out homes and compared inspection strategies in simulations. [1]

Measurement

Excavation results supplied the pipe-material labels. Validation and simulated work sequences used those records. [1]

Setting and sample

The database covered 55,893 parcels. The main accuracy test held out 1,606 homes with known pipe materials. [1]

Measures

Pipe-material prediction, inspection success, and modeled cost per replacement. [1]

Comparison

The cost simulation compared the proposed strategy with the sequence of work in the city’s records. [1]

Results

The held-out prediction accuracy was 91.6%. The simulated strategy lowered estimated cost per replacement by 10.7%. [1]

Additional value from AI

The estimated cost improvement came from a simulation. The field results do not isolate the model from crews, funding, and policy decisions. [1]

Limitations

Known pipe materials came from a selected set of inspected homes. The simulated costs omitted travel and other logistical constraints. [1]

Denominators

Predictions were updated as crews recorded new inspections and replacements. [1]

Materials for replication

The paper describes the algorithms and simulations. It does not supply all settings needed to reproduce the deployed system. [1]

Money

Kind of value

Modeled cost savings. [1]

Amount

The simulation estimated about US$622 less per replacement, or US$11.18 million if applied to 18,000 replacements. [1]

Currency and prices

U.S. dollars, using the unit costs assumed in the study. A separate inflation base year is not stated. [1]

Period and unit

Per replacement, with an extrapolation to 18,000 replacements. [1]

Realization

The savings were simulated. They are not an audited reduction in city expenditure. [1]

Recipients and payers

The modeled savings would reduce the cost of the publicly funded replacement program. [1]

Gross or net

The estimate includes excavation and replacement costs, but not every cost of operating the program. [1]

Included costs

The simulation assumed US$250 for a hydrovac inspection, US$2,500 for an unnecessary excavation, and US$5,000 for a needed replacement. [1]

Comparison

Estimated average cost was US$5,818 under the recorded work sequence and US$5,196 under the proposed strategy. [1]

Method

Researchers simulated inspection and replacement sequences, applied fixed unit costs, and extrapolated the difference. [1]

Return and payback

No complete return-on-investment or payback calculation is reported. [1]

People and places

Participants

University researchers developed the system. Flint officials managed the program, and contractors inspected and replaced service lines. [1]

Jurisdictions

Flint, Michigan, United States. [1]

Ownership and control

The research team developed the models. The city controlled the replacement program and its contracts. [1]

Dependencies

The system depended on digitized parcel records, reliable excavation labels, a shared database, and crews who could inspect and replace pipes. [1]

Cross-border connections

The research institutions and deployment were in the United States. No international data transfer is documented. [1]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Useful parcel records, verified inspection results, and a working relationship with the people who choose excavation sites are necessary. [1]

Later findings

A March 2019 court order required the city to prioritize addresses identified by the predictive model, subject to defined exceptions. [3]

Institutional decision

The federal court amended the settlement governing Flint’s service-line replacement program. [3]

Grounds

The parties agreed that the city should first excavate homes with the highest predicted likelihood of hazardous lines. [3]

Action and result

The city had to prioritize the model’s address lists, document exceptions, and report completed work. [3]

Success criterion

The city had to excavate the listed addresses and replace hazardous lines by 1 January 2020. [3]
