Smartwatch flags a slow heart rate that an antibiotic likely caused

A smartwatch of unstated brand alerted a woman taking ciprofloxacin to a heart rate below 40 beats per minute; she went to hospital, and her rate recovered after the antibiotic was replaced.

  • 2 a person

    The alert led physicians to recognize symptomatic bradycardia caused by a drug and to change her antibiotic, after which her heart rate returned to normal. The authors say that a potential adverse event was prevented, and they document no imminent event.

The system’s part
The watch's sustained low-heart-rate alert, combined with her symptoms, is what the authors say prompted the hospital visit. Telemetry confirmed the rate and clinicians identified ciprofloxacin by exclusion and re-challenge-free withdrawal. The threshold alert is a simple rule on a photoplethysmographic heart-rate estimate, and the brand is unknown, so no learned model can be credited.
Limits
The alert was a sustained-rate threshold, and the report does not establish that a learned AI model produced it. Clinical exclusion of alternatives and improvement after withdrawal support the suspected medication cause, not a controlled causal test. A severe adverse event prevented remains hypothetical.

What happened

Cordova Sanchez, Chohan, Olatunde and White of State University of New York Upstate Medical University, Syracuse, published the case in the Journal of Investigative Medicine High Impact Case Reports on 24 January 2022 without giving the date of the event. A 32-year-old woman had a hospital stay for cholangitis, during which she had an endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography. She was sent home on ciprofloxacin and metronidazole. She developed dizziness on minimal exertion, and her smartwatch triggered an alert for a sustained heart rate below 40 beats per minute (bpm). Two days after discharge she attended an outside emergency department, where telemetry confirmed 30–50 bpm. She was transferred to the authors' hospital for cardiology review. Her electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus bradycardia at 51 bpm, against 66 bpm before the antibiotics. The drugs given for the procedure two days earlier have short half-lives. Other work-up (blood tests, thyroid function, Lyme serology, drug screen, echocardiogram) was unremarkable. Ciprofloxacin was replaced with ceftriaxone, and by the next day her heart rate was 60–70 bpm. She was discharged on cefpodoxime and metronidazole. The watch's brand and model are not stated. The authors describe wearable heart-rate detection generically as photoplethysmography. The journal received the manuscript on 23 October 2021, so the alert and the admission happened before that date. They concluded that ciprofloxacin was the likely cause and that early recognition of the bradycardia prevented a potential adverse event. Similar cases have been reported elsewhere. In 2019 Michigan emergency physicians reported that bradycardia detected by a wearable was the first sign of complete heart block. In 2025 Duke cardiologists reported that smartwatch low-rate alarms led to a diagnosis of lamin A/C cardiomyopathy.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

A sustained heart-rate reading below 40 beats per minute produced the smartwatch warning.

Autonomy

The watch flagged a low pulse; clinicians identified the likely drug reaction through testing and withdrawal of ciprofloxacin.

Workflow

The warning and dizziness prompted emergency assessment; clinicians confirmed bradycardia and replaced ciprofloxacin, after which the heart rate rose.

Human oversight

The woman sought care after the alert and her dizziness. Telemetry in the emergency department confirmed the low rate, and clinicians excluded other causes before they replaced ciprofloxacin. [1]
