HelioLinc3D finds asteroid 2022 SF289 in sparse ATLAS detections

The HelioLinc3D algorithm linked sparse detections from the ATLAS survey into the potentially hazardous asteroid 2022 SF289, which the Minor Planet Center published on 18 July 2023.

  • 2 an organization

    The Rubin Observatory and the DiRAC team at the University of Washington obtained a real-data validation of the linking software before the ten-year survey began.

  • 2 society

    One potentially hazardous asteroid about 600 feet across was added to the catalog. The sources state that it poses no foreseeable risk to Earth, so the benefit is knowledge of the object.

What happened

ATLAS, a NASA-funded survey operated by the University of Hawaii, first imaged the asteroid 2022 SF289 on 19 September 2022. It observed the object three times across four nights. Conventional discovery requires more same-night detections. HelioLinc3D was developed by Ari Heinze and colleagues at the University of Washington's DiRAC Institute for the Vera C. Rubin Observatory. The sources call HelioLinc3D an algorithm and do not describe it as a learned model. On 18 July 2023, in a test on archival ATLAS data, HelioLinc3D identified the object as an unrecognized near-Earth asteroid. The Minor Planet Center published it in its electronic circular MPEC 2023-O26, issued the same day. It is an Apollo-type potentially hazardous asteroid about 600 feet across with an Earth MOID of 0.0015 AU and no foreseeable risk to Earth. On 5 August 2023 The New York Times reported that ATLAS and similar surveys had failed to see the asteroid. Meg Schwamb of Queen's University Belfast co-chairs the LSST Solar System Science Collaboration, and the paper describes her as not involved with the work. She told the paper that HelioLinc3D "is the engine" of the Rubin Observatory. On 2 April 2026 the University of Washington and Rubin Observatory announced over 11,000 new asteroids, including 33 near-Earth objects, that the Minor Planet Center confirmed. Discovery software that Ari Heinze and Jacob Kurlander developed at DiRAC found them. That release does not use the name HelioLinc3D.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

HelioLinc3D linked sparse asteroid detections from different nights to derive an orbit for 2022 SF289.

Workflow

Survey detections too sparse per night for conventional linking. Heliocentric linking across multiple nights. Submission to and designation by the Minor Planet Center.

Human oversight

Researchers submitted the linked object to the Minor Planet Center, which published its designation and orbit.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Astrometry from several nights must be precise enough to constrain a heliocentric orbit. The Minor Planet Center must confirm the linkage as a new object.

Evidence

Original evidence

The Minor Planet Center publishes the observations and the orbit. The press releases of the University of Washington, the University of Hawaii and Rubin Observatory report the same discovery and name HelioLinc3D as the software. The New York Times restated the discovery from those releases and interviewed Meg Schwamb, whom the paper describes as not involved with the work. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]
