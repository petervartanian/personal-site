What happened

In a case report of 29 March 2019 (event date not stated; publication date used), a 59-year-old woman attended a United Kingdom emergency department. The acute medicine service saw her (the report does not name the hospital; the author is at Ipswich Hospital). She attended because her Apple Watch Series 2 had alarmed several times overnight that her pulse exceeded 120 bpm while she was inactive. A transient ischemic attack had occurred two months earlier. An electrocardiogram (ECG) showed atrial fibrillation with a fast ventricular response. She started bisoprolol and a direct oral anticoagulant, and clopidogrel was stopped. Later case reports describe the watch's heart-rate alerts, irregular-rhythm notification or ECG app leading to confirmed atrial flutter, supraventricular and ventricular tachycardia and heart block.

Treatment was by cardioversion, ablation, pacemaker or defibrillator.

What it was built to do. Apple Watch high/low heart-rate notifications, the Irregular Rhythm Notification and the ECG app are built to flag possible arrhythmias so the wearer seeks medical review. This benefit was that purpose.

The system's part

The watch's heart-rate algorithm generated the overnight alarms that brought the patient to hospital. Clinicians made the diagnosis on an ECG and chose treatment. In the echoes the notification or the ECG app's single-lead trace likewise triggered evaluation, with clinicians confirming the rhythm.

Replication

What produced it. The wearer owns an Apple Watch with heart-rate notifications, Irregular Rhythm Notification or the ECG app enabled. An arrhythmia occurs while the watch is worn and the alert or recording leads the wearer to seek care. A clinician confirms the rhythm on a 12-lead ECG or monitoring and treats it.

What must hold again. The alert or trace reaches a clinician rather than being dismissed. Confirmatory ECG or electrophysiology testing is available. A treatment pathway (anticoagulation, cardioversion, ablation, device) exists.
