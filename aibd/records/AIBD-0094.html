Nigeria uses machine-learning poverty maps to target COVID-19 cash aid

Nigeria used machine-learning poverty maps from satellite imagery to select 1,163 of its poorest urban wards for a COVID-19 cash transfer that counted 49,000 beneficiary households by 15 October 2021.

  • 2 a person

    Households in selected wards were beneficiaries of the government's COVID-19 cash transfer. The PNAS authors say that disbursement began in mid-January 2021. The World Bank's status report records 49,000 beneficiary households as of 15 October 2021 without saying how many had been paid.

  • 2 an organization

    The National Social Safety Nets Coordinating Office stood up an urban registry within months through self-registration by mobile phone in the wards selected with the poverty map. The World Bank's appraisal document of November 2021 reports over 1.75 million registrations.

  • Not rated society

    The sources report that the 1,163 selected wards, with over 20 million residents, were made eligible, and the 61.7% cut in inclusion errors is a simulation estimate. No source reports a realized change across that population.

The system’s part

Payment and registration counts come from World Bank operational documents, not from the map developers. The PNAS paper and World Bank blog are co-authored by the developers.
Limits
The population in selected wards, registrations, and paid households are different denominators. The evidence does not isolate the maps' effect on household welfare or establish that every resident of a selected ward received assistance.
Costs and harms
Human Rights Watch researchers argued that the targeting methods made it very likely that large numbers of people in need were excluded. AI Incident Database incident 546 describes a similar harm from another poverty-estimation algorithm. That incident does not show that the harm occurred here.
Later
Scaled up, 29 January 2025. The World Bank's 2021 scale-up project adopted the same machine-learning poverty maps to choose expansion wards for the RRR, and its January 2025 status report counts over 5 million households supported by the direct benefit transfer program after delays and a switch to biometric-verified payments, without breaking out the RRR-targeted share.

Brief from Peter

Nigeria had no urban social registry, so its National Social Safety Nets Coordinating Office built the Rapid Response Register for a government-funded COVID-19 cash transfer. Using ward-level poverty maps made by applying machine-learning algorithms to satellite imagery, the government selected 1,163 of the poorest urban wards, with a population of over 20 million.1 Residents of those wards then registered by mobile phone and were verified in person. The developers of the maps report that, in targeting the poor, the machine-learning approach raises ward-level precision to 0.92, against 0.79 for a survey-based benchmark.2 No source shows how many people the maps helped. A World Bank status report of 29 January 2025 records 49,000 beneficiary households in the cash transfer as of 15 October 2021 and does not say how many had been paid. It also counts over 5 million households supported by the direct benefit transfer program, with no breakdown of the share that the register targeted.3 Human Rights Watch researchers argued in July 2021 that the targeting methods had in all likelihood excluded, or would exclude, large numbers of people in need.4

Details

AI system

Autonomy

The poverty map selected eligible wards. Individual registration, verification, de-duplication, and payment onboarding occurred through separate administrative processes.

Workflow

Deep-learning poverty map trained on geo-located household surveys. Government decision to adopt the ward ranking for eligibility. SMS blasts and USSD self-registration. In-person verification, de-duplication and payment onboarding.

Human oversight

The government decided to adopt the map's ward ranking for eligibility. Within the selected wards, residents registered themselves and were verified in person before payment. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Benefits

Reach

Operational monitoring reported 49,000 beneficiary households by 15 October 2021. The more than 20 million residents of selected wards were an eligible-area population, not the number of paid households.

Duration

Disbursement began in mid-January 2021. The program counted 49,000 beneficiary households by 15 October 2021. The World Bank's scale-up project adopted the same maps to choose expansion wards. By 29 January 2025 the direct benefit transfer program had supported over 5 million households, with no breakdown of the share that the register targeted. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Ground-truth survey data for training labels. Ward boundaries compatible with map resolution. Mobile-phone access in target wards. Government funding for transfers and identity verification capacity.

Replication attempts

1 follow-up entry. Benefit recurred: 1.
