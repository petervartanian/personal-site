Wearable rhythm strips let a 59-year-old go straight to ablation

A consumer wearable of a brand that the report does not name recorded a 59-year-old's atrial fibrillation, so that he skipped 30 days of monitoring; after ablation he was free of it without drugs.

  • 2 a person

    The patient's own recordings replaced repeat 30-day monitoring and took him directly to ablation. After it he was asymptomatic and free of atrial fibrillation without antiarrhythmic drugs. That is material relief of a two-year symptom burden, with the authors' note that ablation is not curative.

  • 1 an organization

    The care team skipped the recommended 30 days of monitoring. The authors say that this minimized the burden and costs of redundant diagnostic processes, and they give no cost figure.

Depth runs from 1, a convenience or time saved, to 5, a life or a livelihood preserved.

What happened

Joy S. Elliott of Riverside Family Medicine Medical Center, Newport News, Virginia, and Andrew H. Hagen of Edward Via College of Osteopathic Medicine, Blacksburg, Virginia, reported a case. It appeared in Osteopathic Family Physician (volume 18, number 3, Summer 2026, pages 56–59), published 24 July 2026. The event dates are not given. The patient is a 59-year-old man with two years of weekly episodes of fast heart rate, palpitations and fatigue, lasting up to 24 hours. He had documented several episodes through his smartphone using a consumer wearable device. A cardiologist friend had judged them paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF), but he had not sought formal evaluation. At the family medicine clinic his examination and bloods were normal. Initial management used the wearable for ambulatory rhythm monitoring to separate AF from premature beats. A confirmed episode lasting several hours prompted laboratory work-up and cardiology referral. Echocardiography showed a 4.1 cm aortic root and ascending aorta with normal ventricular function. Thirty-day monitoring was recommended. Because the wearable's rhythm strips already documented paroxysmal AF, his care went straight to treatment options. He chose pulmonary vein isolation ablation and reported dramatic improvement. He had one recurrence while skiing that responded to flecainide. At follow-up he was asymptomatic and AF-free without antiarrhythmic drugs, declining anticoagulation at a CHA2DS2-VASc score of 1. The device's brand is not named. The PDF was downloadable from the ACOFP site, although the article page says membership is required.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

The patient captured rhythm strips during symptoms and shared them through his smartphone. Clinicians used those recordings as evidence of intermittent atrial fibrillation.

Workflow

A wearer who records rhythm strips on a consumer wearable during symptomatic episodes over an extended period. A clinician (here a cardiologist friend, then the clinic) willing to accept those strips as documentation of paroxysmal AF. A referral pathway that treats the documentation as sufficient to proceed to ablation without repeat 30-day monitoring.

Human oversight

A cardiologist friend first interpreted the recordings, and clinicians later confirmed the arrhythmia, investigated it, and discussed treatment with the patient.

Relation to AI

The documented benefit came from wearable recordings and clinical interpretation. An AI contribution is unconfirmed. [1]

AI contribution

The wearable recorded heart rhythms that clinicians interpreted as atrial fibrillation. Those recordings let them proceed without further monitoring. The report does not establish that an automated classifier contributed. [1]

Benefits

Reach

One patient benefited: a 59-year-old man with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. [1]

Duration

The recordings allowed clinicians to skip another proposed 30 days of monitoring. The report does not give the actual reduction in waiting time to ablation.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The episodes are frequent and long enough to be captured on a user-triggered device. The wearer has the device, the smartphone and the skills to record and share the strips (the authors list these as barriers for other patients).

Sector

Healthcare (cardiology; primary care). Consumer wearables.

Purpose

Consumer wearables with electrocardiogram (ECG) recording are built to let wearers capture their rhythm during symptoms. Using those strips to document paroxysmal AF and shorten the path to ablation was that purpose.
