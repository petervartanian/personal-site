Wearable rhythm strips let a 59-year-old go straight to ablation

Virginia family physicians report that AF episodes a 59-year-old recorded on a consumer wearable, confirmed by a cardiologist, expedited referral and ablation; AF-free off drugs at follow-up.

ⓘ

What happened

Joy S. Elliott of Riverside Family Medicine Medical Center, Newport News, Virginia, and Andrew H. Hagen of Edward Via College of Osteopathic Medicine, Blacksburg, Virginia, reported a case. It appeared in Osteopathic Family Physician (volume 18, number 3, Summer 2026, pages 56–59), published 24 July 2026. The event dates are not given. The patient is a 59-year-old man with two years of weekly episodes of fast heart rate, palpitations and fatigue, lasting up to 24 hours. He had documented several episodes through his smartphone using a consumer wearable device. A cardiologist friend had judged them paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF), but he had not sought formal evaluation. At the family medicine clinic his examination and bloods were normal. Initial management used the wearable for ambulatory rhythm monitoring to separate AF from premature beats.

A confirmed episode lasting several hours prompted laboratory work-up and cardiology referral. Echocardiography showed a 4.1 cm aortic root and ascending aorta with normal ventricular function. Thirty-day monitoring was recommended. Because the wearable's rhythm strips already documented paroxysmal AF, his care went straight to treatment options. He chose pulmonary vein isolation ablation and reported dramatic improvement. He had one recurrence while skiing that responded to flecainide. At follow-up he was asymptomatic and AF-free without antiarrhythmic drugs, declining anticoagulation at a CHA2DS2-VASc score of 1. The device's brand is not named. The PDF was downloadable from the ACOFP site, although the article page says membership is required.

Purpose. Consumer wearables with electrocardiogram (ECG) recording are built to let wearers capture their rhythm during symptoms. Using those strips to document paroxysmal AF and shorten the path to ablation was that purpose.

AI’s contribution

The device recorded rhythm strips during symptomatic episodes. The AF determination is attributed to a cardiologist friend and later to clinical confirmation. The report does not say whether the device's own algorithm classified any episode. The device's contribution was therefore the documentation that let clinicians forgo further monitoring, not an automated diagnosis. The brand is unknown. Clinicians interpreted the wearable’s recordings. The report does not establish that AI contributed to the patient’s care.

Beneficiaries

  • a person

    The patient's own recordings replaced repeat 30-day monitoring and took him directly to ablation. After it he was asymptomatic and AF-free off antiarrhythmics. That is material relief of a two-year symptom burden, with the authors' note that ablation is not curative.

  • an organization

    The authors say the approach minimized the burden and costs of redundant diagnostic processes by avoiding repeat ambulatory monitoring. No figure is given.

Further evidence

Setting. A wearer who records rhythm strips on a consumer wearable during symptomatic episodes over an extended period. A clinician (here a cardiologist friend, then the clinic) willing to accept those strips as documentation of paroxysmal AF. A referral pathway that treats the documentation as sufficient to proceed to ablation without repeat 30-day monitoring.

Conditions. The episodes are frequent and long enough to be captured on a user-triggered device. The wearer has the device, the smartphone and the skills to record and share the strips (the authors list these as barriers for other patients).
