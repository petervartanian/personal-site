ML ink detection reads 2,000+ characters of a Herculaneum scroll

ML ink detection let contestants read 2,000+ characters of a sealed Herculaneum scroll; papyrologists confirmed the text (Feb 2024).

ⓘ

What happened

On 12 October 2023 the Vesuvius Challenge announced that contestant Luke Farritor, a 21-year-old student, had read ΠΟΡΦΥΡΑϹ (“purple”) inside the unopened Herculaneum scroll PHerc.Paris.4. He used a machine-learning model trained on the “crackle” pattern of ink visible in X-ray computed tomography (CT) scans. On 5 February 2024 the organizers gave the $700,000 grand prize to Youssef Nader, Luke Farritor and Julian Schilliger. Their TimeSformer-based ink-detection models recovered 15 columns and more than 2,000 characters of Greek text. Papyrologists Federica Nicolardi, Richard Janko and Robert Fowler reviewed the passages, which discuss pleasure, food and music and are probably by Philodemus. The University of Kentucky, whose virtual-unwrapping software underlies the contest, called the result a new, readable Herculaneum text.

Purpose. Built to detect ink in X-ray CT scans of carbonized Herculaneum papyri so that the text can be read. This benefit was that purpose.

AI’s contribution

The contestants' ink-detection models produced the ink images from which papyrologists read the text. The ink is not visible in the raw CT data. Virtual unwrapping (non-AI) was a necessary precondition. The readings were validated by an independent papyrological review organized by the contest.

Beneficiaries

  • a person

    Nader, Farritor and Schilliger shared a $700,000 prize announced by the organizers on 5 February 2024. Farritor had earlier won the First Letters prize for the first word.

  • an organization

    The University of Kentucky program behind the virtual unwrapping, described by Seales as a 20-year dream, was validated with a readable text.

  • society

    A previously unknown Greek philosophical text became readable. Nature reports that the method paves the way to deciphering the rest of the scrolls.

Further evidence

Setting. High-resolution synchrotron CT scan of a carbonized scroll. Virtual unwrapping into flattened segments. Ink-detection model trained on labeled crackle/ink texture. Independent papyrological review of the output.

Conditions. Ink must produce a detectable texture or density signal in the CT data. Segmentation must be accurate enough to preserve letter order. Readings must be confirmed by papyrologists, not only by model output.
