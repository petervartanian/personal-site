Chicago's model would have found food violations 7.5 days earlier

In a double-blind retrodiction of 1,637 Chicago food inspections from 2014, the city's Food Inspection Forecasting model would have found critical violations on average 7.5 days earlier.

  • Not rated an organization

    The sources document a retrospective simulation, in which 69% of the establishments with critical violations would have been found in the first half of the work against 55% in the normal order. No source documents an outcome of live use that can be attributed to the model.

  • Not rated society

    The sources give earlier discovery of violations as the rationale, since discovery otherwise may come “potentially after patrons became ill”. No source documents an outcome for illness.

The system’s part
The model is a logistic regression built by the City of Chicago with pro bono partners and used by the Department of Public Health to order inspections; inspectors carry out every inspection. An independent re-run confirms the simulated gain of about 7.4 days, with a standard deviation of 25.2 days. The same analysis found that the most influential feature was the group of inspectors who last visited an establishment, and its authors argue that this feature unfairly raises risk scores. The authors could not separate the model's effect in live use from restaurant turnover and stricter inspection after improved training.
Limits
The 7.5-day gain comes from re-ordering inspections that had already been completed. The 2019 independent analysis could not attribute the higher violation rates after 2015 to the model, since restaurant turnover or stricter inspectors may explain them. No source documents fewer illnesses.

What happened

Chicago's Department of Innovation and Technology and Department of Public Health built a model. Pro bono data scientists from Allstate Insurance and the Civic Consulting Alliance helped. It assigns each of the city's 15,000+ food establishments a likelihood of a critical violation. Predictors include previous critical or serious violations, three-day average high temperature, nearby garbage and sanitation complaints and facility type. Others are nearby burglaries, licenses, time since last inspection, length of operation and the inspector assigned. In a double-blind retrodiction, the Department of Public Health inspected 1,637 establishments in its normal order during September and October 2014. Of these, 258 (almost 16%) had at least one critical violation, and 141 of those (55%) were found in the first month. Re-ordering the same inspections by the model's probabilities would have found 178 (69%) in the first half of the work, on average 7.5 days earlier. Another 37 would have been cited in the first month. The Department of Public Health began using the model in January 2015. On 19 May 2015 the Harvard Ash Center's Data-Smart City Solutions published an article on the pilot. The city's own page on the pilot is undated, and the code is open source in R. In a 2019 analysis, independent researchers at the Illinois Institute of Technology re-ran the city's simulation and found an average gain of 7.4 days, with a standard deviation of 25 days. They also found that the strongest predictor was the group of inspectors who last visited. They report that the rise in violation rates after 2015 cannot be shown to come from the model.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

The model combined previous violations, weather, nearby complaints and crime, establishment characteristics, inspection timing, and inspector identity to assign each establishment a probability of a critical violation.

Autonomy

In this pilot, the model only reordered completed inspections in a simulation. It did not direct inspectors or change the actual order of their visits.

Workflow

Open inspection, 311 complaint, business license and weather data on the city's data portal. Fewer than three dozen inspectors for 15,000+ establishments, so ordering matters. A fixed two-month inspection record to re-score.

Human oversight

The model sets the order of inspections, and Department of Public Health inspectors carry out every inspection. The 2019 independent analysis found that the strongest predictor was the group of inspectors who last visited, and its authors recommended that the city drop it. [1] [2] [3]

Benefits

Reach

No source counts beneficiaries. The model scores each of the city's 15,000+ food establishments, and the simulation covered 1,637 inspections in September and October 2014. [1] [2] [3]

Duration

The Department of Public Health began using the model in January 2015. In 2019 independent researchers reported that the rise in violation rates after 2015 cannot be shown to come from the model. [1] [2] [3]

Measurement

Measurement

Study 1: Double-blind retrodiction: inspections done in the usual order were re-ordered by the model's predicted risk, and the timing of critical-violation findings was compared. Study 2: Independent re-run of the city's released model and test set, plus a before and after comparison of monthly violation hit rates in public inspection records from January 2010 to June 2018. [1]

Setting and sample

Study 1: 1,637 canvass inspections by the Chicago Department of Public Health, September and October 2014; 258 had at least one critical violation. Study 2: The city's 1,637-inspection test set; 50,462 canvass and 17,088 complaint inspections from the public data. [1]

Measures

Study 1: Share of establishments with critical violations found in the first half of the work; average days earlier. Study 2: Days earlier to find establishments with a critical violation; monthly hit rates by violation code before and after January 2015. [1]

Results

Study 1: 69 percent found in the first half against 55 percent; violations found on average 7.5 days earlier. Study 2: 7.438 days earlier on average in simulation, standard deviation 25.156 days; hit rates rose after 2015 for several violation codes. [1]

Additional value from AI

Study 1: The whole difference is attributed to the model's ordering, because the same inspections are re-ordered. Study 2: The authors argue that the post-2015 rise cannot be attributed to the model, because restaurant turnover and stricter inspection could explain it. [1]

Limitations

Study 1: No inspection was changed; the simulation assumes that results would be the same on an earlier date, and the city reports no confidence interval. Written by the city that built and uses the model. Study 2: The authors could not observe when establishments would have been inspected without the model, and the city does not release inspector assignments or inspection cycles. Presented at a symposium; not a journal article. [1]

Denominators

Study 1: Simulation only. Study 2: Model in live use from January 2015. [1]

Materials for replication

Study 1: Code and data released by the City of Chicago on GitHub. Study 2: Uses the city's public code and data on GitHub and the Chicago data portal. [1]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The predictor set (prior violations, nearby sanitation complaints, facility type, days since last inspection, inspector) remains available. A critical-violation base rate near 15%.

Evidence

Original evidence

The pilot figures come from the city's own page, and the Harvard Ash Center's Data-Smart City Solutions reports the same pilot. In 2019 researchers at the Illinois Institute of Technology, who have no stake in the model, re-ran the city's simulation. [1] [2] [3]
