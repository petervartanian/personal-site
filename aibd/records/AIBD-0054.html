Amputee with 48 years of phantom limb pain reaches pain-free periods

BioPatRec pattern recognition decoded a man's stump muscle signals for an augmented-reality limb, and his 48-year phantom limb pain eased to pain-free periods.

  • 2 a person

    A patient with 48 years of treatment-resistant pain reached pain-free periods and regained phantom movement. The case report gives no long-term follow-up, so a lasting effect is not established.

  • Not rated society

    The case report describes one patient. The same group later reported a trial in 14 patients and a randomized trial in 81 patients, and in the randomized trial the authors found no evidence that the decoded execution was superior to guided motor imagery. No realized outcome across a population is documented.

The system’s part
Pattern-recognition classifiers turned stump muscle signals into predicted phantom movements that a virtual limb carried out. The 2014 authors argue that this overt motor execution drives the relief. In 2024 the same group's randomized trial compared the method with guided motor imagery that decodes nothing and found no difference in pain reduction. The trial does not support a specific benefit from the machine-learning decoding, and the therapy as a whole was followed by large pain reductions in both arms.
Limits
Pain relief was self-reported by one treatment-resistant patient who received combined motor training, augmented reality and gaming. The case cannot isolate the classifier's effect or exclude expectancy and concurrent changes. In the 2024 randomized trial the authors found no evidence that the decoded execution was superior to guided motor imagery.

What happened

A 72-year-old man's below-elbow amputation in 1965 left him with 48 years of phantom limb pain, resistant to mirror therapy, drugs, acupuncture and hypnosis. A team from Chalmers University of Technology and Sahlgrenska University Hospital, both in Gothenburg, treated him. The paper does not name the treatment site. Treatment ran for 18 weeks from March 2013. The exact start date is not reported. Myoelectric pattern recognition classified surface electromyography from his stump, using linear discriminant analysis and a multilayer perceptron in the open-source BioPatRec platform. The decoded movements drove a virtual limb shown in augmented reality and a racing game. After four weeks he reported episodes of lower pain intensity, and after ten weeks near-absent pain developing into completely pain-free periods. His phantom fist relaxed and he could move the phantom freely (Frontiers in Neuroscience, 2014). In December 2016 the same group reported in the Lancet a single-group trial in 14 patients with intractable phantom limb pain, in which pain decreased by 47% for weighted pain distribution. An independent review in 2022 by authors at Loma Linda University and the Department of Veterans Affairs scored the 2014 case report 9 on the MINORS scale and urged caution about the evidence base. In September 2024 the same group reported a double-blind randomized trial in 81 patients at 9 clinics in 7 countries. Pain fell by 64.5% with machine-learning phantom motor execution and by 68.2% with guided motor imagery, and the authors found no evidence that the decoded execution was superior.

Details

AI system

Model

Linear discriminant analysis and multilayer perceptrons within BioPatRec.

Role

The classifiers converted muscle signals into movements of a virtual limb.

Configuration

BioPatRec used one-versus-one linear discriminant analysis and a multilayer perceptron for each degree of freedom.

Inputs and outputs

Electrodes measured activity in the residual arm muscles. BioPatRec classified intended phantom movements and used them to control a virtual arm and a racing game.

Workflow

Decodable residual muscle activity in the stump. Classifier-driven virtual limb that responds to the patient's own effort. Repeated sessions with gamified motor execution.

Human oversight

Staff placed electrodes using the patient’s attempted movements, checked signal quality, and trained the decoder. A facilitator administered pain questionnaires after sessions while the patient rated pain intensity.

Benefits

Reach

The case report describes one patient. The 2016 Lancet trial included 14 patients. The 2024 randomized trial included 81 patients. In that trial pain fell by 64.5% with machine-learning phantom motor execution and by 68.2% with guided motor imagery. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Duration

Improvement emerged after four weeks, progressed to near-absent pain after ten weeks, and was observed during an 18-week course.

Measurement

Measurement

Change in the Pain Rating Index from baseline to the end of treatment, and the rate of clinically meaningful pain reduction of more than 50%. [3]

Setting and sample

81 people with phantom limb pain randomized at 9 outpatient clinics in Sweden, the Netherlands, Slovenia, Ireland, Germany, Canada and the United States. [3]

Measures

Percentage change in phantom limb pain. [3]

Results

Pain fell 64.5% with phantom motor execution and 68.2% with imagery; 71% and 68% of each group reached a clinically meaningful reduction. [3]

Additional value from AI

None detected; the authors found no evidence that decoded execution beat imagery. [3]

Limitations

No untreated control group, so the shared effect of training in extended reality cannot be separated from natural change. The method's developer is senior author and two authors list Integrum AB, which part-funded the original case report. [3]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Patient can produce distinguishable EMG patterns for phantom movements. Weekly or twice-weekly sessions sustained over months. Augmented-reality display of the restored limb.

Replication attempts

2 follow-up entries. Benefit recurred: 2.

The 2022 systematic review comes from independent authors at Loma Linda University and the Department of Veterans Affairs. [1] [2] [3] [4]
