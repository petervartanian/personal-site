An Apple Watch called for help after a runner’s heart stopped

Fall Detection on an Apple Watch Series 10 called emergency services and alerted emergency contacts when a 69-year-old runner collapsed in cardiac arrest, and he made a full neurological recovery.

  • 5 a person

    The man was alone and hidden from view in cardiac arrest. The automatic alerts brought family members, who began cardiopulmonary resuscitation about 6–7 minutes after the collapse. He left hospital neurologically intact, and the authors present the outcome as life-saving.

The system’s part
The paper reports that the watch recognized the fall and automatically called 112 and alerted emergency contacts. Those alerts brought responders to the man behind the hedge. Humans provided resuscitation, defibrillation, and surgery. The watch's contribution is documented in the clinical narrative, but the time to rescue or outcome without it cannot be measured from this case. Limits
The case report gives no date or place. The identification of the patient as Alwi Siebers, the date and the street are inferred from interviews and a pager message. Costs and harms
The device shortened the path from an unwitnessed collapse to human help. Survival still depended on CPR, defibrillation, and hospital care, and this case cannot measure the outcome without the alert.

Brief from Peter

A case report published on 12 August 2026 in Resuscitation Plus describes a 69-year-old man who collapsed in ventricular fibrillation while running alone, hidden behind a hedge on a dead-end road.1 When his Apple Watch detected the fall, it automatically called emergency services on 112 and alerted his emergency contacts. Family members began cardiopulmonary resuscitation about 6–7 minutes after the collapse. An ambulance crew arrived about 1.5 minutes later and defibrillated him, and he made a full neurological recovery. The paper documents that the alerts brought his family to him. It cannot show how long he would have lain there without them, and the comparison rests on an interview in which a son says that he would not otherwise have found his father so quickly.2 This is a single documented case, echoed by an earlier German report.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

The watch detected a fall and sent emergency calls and contact alerts that helped responders locate the runner. It did not diagnose ventricular fibrillation.

Autonomy

The fall alert initiated the call to 112 and the emergency-contact notifications without requiring the unconscious runner to request help.

Workflow

Fall Detection is enabled on a worn Apple Watch with cellular or phone connectivity. The wearer collapses with an impact the classifier recognizes and then stays immobile. Emergency services and contacts can reach the shared location quickly.

Human oversight

Family members found the runner and started CPR. Emergency clinicians provided defibrillation and medication, and hospital clinicians performed subsequent treatment.

AI contribution

The clinical report states that the watch detected the fall, called emergency services, and alerted emergency contacts. Responders found the man behind a hedge and treated his cardiac arrest. The report does not establish how quickly he would have been found without the watch. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Dependencies

The benefit depended on the watch being worn with Fall Detection enabled, a working emergency connection, and people who could reach the transmitted location and begin resuscitation.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The watch has connectivity at the scene. The collapse produces a detectable fall signature. Responders arrive within the window in which resuscitation can succeed.
