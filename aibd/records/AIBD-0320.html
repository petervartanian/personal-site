Microsoft Translator adds Inuktitut and Inuinnaqtun with Nunavut

Neural MT for Inuktut dialects built with the Government of Nunavut and volunteer speakers; Nunatsiaq News and VICE report launches and translation errors; no measured user outcome documented.

What happened

Microsoft added Inuktitut text translation to Microsoft Translator, Office and Translator for Bing on 27 January 2021 (Arctic Portal, relaying the Microsoft Translator blog; date used here), after working with the Government of Nunavut for language data, with Inuktitut speakers volunteering to validate and test translations. VICE (18 February 2021) reported Nunavut language officials finding the output 'riddled with mistakes' (Solomon Awa: 'Sometimes it's close, but sometimes it gave the opposite meaning, or one that was misleading') while languages commissioner Karliin Aariak called it 'a great tool to be like a first step' and retired Université Laval researcher Louis-Jacques Dorais called it a 'tour de force' despite a wrong test sentence. Nunatsiaq News (28 February 2022) reported that Inuinnaqtun and romanized Inuktitut were added during Uqausirmut Quviasuutiqarniq, with fewer than 600 fluent Inuinnaqtun speakers per the Kitikmeot Heritage Society; Microsoft's own feature (1 February 2022) says 'Text translation is based on neural machine translation models', that the Government of Nunavut 'turned to the community to help build and test the language models', and lists intended uses (unilingual patients communicating with doctors, younger people with elders, emails in Office) without data. Nunatsiaq News (9 December 2024) reported the 5 December launch of Inuktitut text-to-speech voices 'Taqqiq' and 'Siqiniq', built from thousands of government documents, hired speakers' recordings and Bible Society and Nunavut Inuit Heritage Trust audio, noting presenters' accounts of the translator confusing 'condo and condom, and prime rib and prime minister'; voice contributor Leah Ayaruaq McKay said she plans to use it to help her husband learn Inuktitut. Government of Nunavut web pages were behind a bot check and no Wayback capture existed. No fetched source measures use, accuracy or an outcome for any user.

What it was built to do. Built to translate text between Inuktut dialects and other languages (and, from 2024, to read Inuktitut aloud) so that residents, learners and government users can read and write in Inuktut; the launches are that intended use.

The system's part

Microsoft states the translation is neural machine translation trained on Government of Nunavut data and validated by volunteer speakers. Independent reporting records both the launches and specific wrong translations (opposite meanings in 2021; 'condo and condom' confusions recounted in 2024), and translators said the output needs a fluent reviewer. No fetched source reports a user outcome, usage figure or accuracy measurement, so what the system contributed beyond availability is undocumented.

Replication

What produced it
  • Language data supplied by the Government of Nunavut and recordings by hired and volunteer speakers
  • Volunteer fluent speakers validating and testing translations before launch
What must hold again
  • Output is checked by a fluent speaker; Nunavut translators and the languages commissioner describe it as a first step needing review
  • Dialect differences across the Arctic are tolerated by users of a standardised model
