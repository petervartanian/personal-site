Microsoft Translator adds Inuktitut and Inuinnaqtun with Nunavut

Microsoft Translator added neural machine translation for Inuktut dialects with the Government of Nunavut; a Nunavik translator finds such tools helpful, and officials report errors.

  • 1 a person

    A Nunavik translator says that she uses Google Translate or Microsoft Translator to look up uncommon words and to proofread and finds them helpful for quick translations. That is a convenience in her work, and other sources report errors that need a fluent reviewer.

  • Not rated an organization

    The Government of Nunavut's and Microsoft's stated uses (healthcare communication, Office email, compliance with language law) are expectations without reported data.

  • Not rated society

    Language-vitality effects are asserted by officials. VICE notes that Inuktut use fell from 72 to 65 percent between 2001 and 2016, without any link to the tool.

The system’s part
Microsoft states the translation is neural machine translation trained on Government of Nunavut data and validated by volunteer speakers. Independent reporting records both the launches and specific wrong translations (opposite meanings in 2021; “condo and condom” confusions recounted in 2024). Translators said the output needs a fluent reviewer. No cited source reports a user outcome, usage figure or accuracy measurement. What the system contributed beyond availability is undocumented.
Limits
No cited source measures use, accuracy or an outcome. A translator in Nunavik says that she uses Google Translate or Microsoft Translator for uncommon words and proofreading, and officials and presenters report opposite meanings and word confusions that need a fluent reviewer.
Costs and harms
Nunavut language officials reported in 2021 that the output sometimes gave the opposite meaning or a misleading one, and presenters in 2024 recounted confusions such as condo and condom. Nunavut translators and the languages commissioner describe the tool as a first step that needs review.

What happened

Microsoft added Inuktitut text translation to Microsoft Translator, Office and Translator for Bing on 27 January 2021. The date is from Arctic Portal, relaying the Microsoft Translator blog. Microsoft worked with the Government of Nunavut for language data; Inuktitut speakers volunteered to validate and test translations. VICE (18 February 2021) reported that Nunavut language officials found the output “riddled with mistakes”. Solomon Awa said: “Sometimes it's close, but sometimes it gave the opposite meaning, or one that was misleading”. Languages commissioner Karliin Aariak called it “a great tool to be like a first step”. Retired Université Laval researcher Louis-Jacques Dorais called it a “tour de force” despite a wrong test sentence. Microsoft's own feature (1 February 2022) says that “Text translation is based on neural machine translation models”. It says that the Government of Nunavut “turned to the community to help build and test the language models”. It lists intended uses (unilingual patients communicating with doctors, younger people with elders, emails in Office) without data. Nunatsiaq News (28 February 2022) reported that Inuinnaqtun and romanized Inuktitut were added during Uqausirmut Quviasuutiqarniq. Fewer than 600 people speak Inuinnaqtun fluently, according to the Kitikmeot Heritage Society. Nunatsiaq News reported on 29 October 2024 that Jeannie Nayoumealuk, a translator in Nunavik, uses Google Translate or Microsoft Translator to research uncommon words and to proofread, and that she finds them helpful for a quick translation. She and another translator, Suzie Napayok, said that Google Translate's new Inuktut service helps with simple translations but not with complicated tasks. Nunatsiaq News (9 December 2024) reported the 5 December launch of Inuktitut text-to-speech voices “Taqqiq” and “Siqiniq”. They were built from thousands of government documents, hired speakers' recordings and Bible Society and Nunavut Inuit Heritage Trust audio. The report noted presenters' accounts of the translator confusing “condo and condom, and prime rib and prime minister”. Voice contributor Leah Ayaruaq McKay said that she plans to use it to help her husband learn Inuktitut. Government of Nunavut web pages are not among the sources. No cited source measures use, accuracy or an outcome.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

The service translated text between supported languages and later converted Inuktitut text into synthesized speech.

Workflow

Language data supplied by the Government of Nunavut and recordings by hired and volunteer speakers. Volunteer fluent speakers validating and testing translations before launch.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Output is checked by a fluent speaker; Nunavut translators and the languages commissioner describe it as a first step needing review. Dialect differences across the Arctic are tolerated by users of a standardized model.

Purpose
