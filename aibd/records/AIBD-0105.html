Subscribing to GLAD alerts lowers deforestation risk in Africa by 18%

Four researchers, two of them from the operator and the producer of the alerts, found that subscriptions to GLAD Landsat alerts cut the probability of deforestation in Africa by 18%.

The system’s part
The alerts are a machine-learning product (bagged decision trees). The evaluation attributes the 18 percent reduction to subscriptions to those alerts rather than to their mere availability. The evaluation's lead authors are independent economists, but co-authors are from WRI (operator) and the University of Maryland (producer).
Limits
The effect applies to areas in Africa with subscribers. The authors found no effect on other continents and no significant effect from the availability of alerts alone. The authors priced the modeled avoided emissions at the social cost of carbon and estimated their value at US$149 million to US$696 million. The Peruvian trial's estimate of a 52 percent cut is imprecise, with a confidence interval that includes zero.

What happened

Global Forest Watch's GLAD deforestation alerts are produced by the University of Maryland from Landsat imagery. Bagged decision trees estimate the per-pixel likelihood of forest loss. The method was published on 2 March 2016. Moffette, Alix-Garcia, Shea and Pickens evaluated the alerts' effect in Nature Climate Change on 4 January 2021, analyzing subscriptions to alerts across 22 tropical countries. They found that subscriptions decreased the probability of deforestation in Africa by 18 percent relative to 2011–2016 baseline levels. Effects were stronger in protected areas and concessions. There was no detectable effect on other continents, and little effect where alerts existed without subscribers. They valued the avoided emissions at USD 149–696 million. Two of the study's four authors worked for the operator and the producer of the alerts: Katherine Shea at the World Resources Institute and Amy Pickens at the University of Maryland. The World Resources Institute funded the work. Oregon State University said that the study compared 2011 to 2018, the five years before GLAD launched in 2016 and the first two years after. In a separate randomized trial in the Peruvian Amazon, published in PNAS in July 2021, independent researchers estimated that a community monitoring program built on such alerts cut first-year tree cover loss by 52 percent, an imprecise estimate. Global Forest Watch kept the Landsat-based GLAD alerts running and in 2022 folded them into an integrated layer of disturbance alerts with GLAD-S2 and RADD. On 13 January 2026 Global Forest Watch announced that DIST-ALERT had joined the layer.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

Bagged decision trees estimated forest loss from Landsat imagery and supplied alerts through Global Forest Watch subscriptions.

Workflow

Weekly Landsat coverage and a conservative classifier. Free public platform with subscription-based alert delivery. Subscribers with enforcement or management capacity.

Human oversight

The alerts flag forest loss, and any response depends on subscribers who can act on them. The authors found effects that were stronger in protected areas and concessions, and little effect where alerts existed without subscribers. [1] [2] [3]

Benefits

Reach

The benefit falls on society through avoided carbon emissions, and the sources give no count of people who benefited. The study covered subscriptions to alerts across 22 tropical countries. [1] [2] [3]

Distribution and access

The measured reduction was concentrated in Africa and was stronger in protected areas and concessions. No detectable effect was reported on other continents.

Duration

Oregon State University said that the study compared 2011 to 2018, the five years before GLAD launched in 2016 and the first two years after. Global Forest Watch kept the Landsat-based alerts running and in 2022 folded them into an integrated layer of disturbance alerts. [1] [2] [3]
