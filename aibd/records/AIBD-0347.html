What happened

INTERPOL reported on 22 July 2019 that the MV NIKA had been detained by Indonesian authorities. The vessel was suspected of illegal fishing in the Southern Ocean. Its owner also owned the blacklisted STS 50 intercepted in Indonesia in 2018. INTERPOL tracked the vessel's movement with support from the UK not-for-profit OceanMind, sending daily reports to Indonesia, New Zealand, Panama, the UK and the US. Hourly updates aided by Global Fishing Watch (GFW) followed as it approached Indonesian waters. GFW's account (23 July 2019) says GFW analyst Imam Prakoso helped track the vessel from February 2019 after it illegally switched off its tracking system. It says a live multi-way communication between Indonesian naval and fishery officials, INTERPOL and GFW analyst Charles Kilgour exchanged information. GFW analysts helped predict its course and identify rendezvous points.

Indonesian patrol boats ORCA 2 and ORCA 3 intercepted it on 12 July 2019 at 08:06 local time. The court outcome comes from a UK Government Blue Belt blog of 5 June 2021. That blog was no longer available as of 20 October 2026. The Indonesian Supreme Court gave its verdict on 27 February 2020, the captain was convicted, the owner fined Rp300,000,000 and the vessel forfeited. None of the sources describes a machine-learning or AI detection. OceanMind's homepage says only, in general terms, that it uses satellites, AI and human expertise.

What it was built to do. Vessel tracking and analysis to support fisheries enforcement against illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing. The capture was the intended use, but the sources attribute it to analysts and inter-agency coordination rather than to an AI output.

The system's part

Not assessed. No source on file documents an AI or machine-learning role. INTERPOL's release says it tracked the vessel with support from OceanMind and that hourly updates were aided by contributions from Global Fishing Watch. GFW's own article credits named analysts who tracked AIS data, predicted the course and identified rendezvous points. It does not mention algorithms, machine learning or automated detection. OceanMind's homepage states only that it uses satellites, AI and human expertise in general. The AI role is thus undocumented rather than established. The record rests on INTERPOL's release, GFW's own account and the Blue Belt blog. The court outcome (Supreme Court verdict of 27 February 2020, conviction, Rp300,000,000 fine, forfeiture) comes from the Blue Belt blog. That review read a UK Government Blue Belt blog post of 5 June 2021 that no longer resolves and has no archived copy.

Replication

What produced it. A blacklisted vessel whose identity and ownership were already known to INTERPOL and CCAMLR. Continuous tracking by OceanMind and GFW analysts with daily, then hourly, reports to coastal states. Indonesian patrol vessels positioned at predicted rendezvous points. Indonesian prosecution leading to a Supreme Court verdict.

What must hold again. A coastal state willing and able to intercept and prosecute. Analyst capacity to follow a vessel that has switched off its tracking system. Information sharing between INTERPOL, NGOs and national agencies in near real time. Documentation of what, if anything, an automated system contributed, which is absent here.
