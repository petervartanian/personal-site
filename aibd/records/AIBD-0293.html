What happened

Zakaria, Roman, Almarzooqi and Hashemi of GluCare.Health, Dubai, reported in Cureus (16 June 2025) a man in his early 40s with type 2 diabetes and a smoking history who had been issued an ŌURA ring under the clinic's hybrid care model and attended for cardiac risk assessment before half-marathon training. The paper says a treadmill stress test was pursued because of his risk profile, “which included poorly controlled diabetes and an elevated resting heart rate observed during wearable-based remote monitoring”; the test on 18 July 2024, the date the cardiologist made the diagnosis, showed frequent PVCs in bigeminy and abnormal heart-rate recovery.

The team then reviewed the ring's data for the previous two weeks retrospectively and found a persistently elevated daytime heart rate and a downward HRV trend; a 24-hour Holter recorded 2,078 ventricular events (1.75% of beats, no couplets or runs). The PVCs were judged idiopathic, of right ventricular outflow tract origin; management was non-pharmacological (stop smoking, cut caffeine from more than five cups of coffee a day, stress reduction), and a 12-lead ECG three days later showed no PVCs. Follow-up ring data showed resting heart rate falling from 83 to 74 bpm and RMSSD rising from 15.2 to 32.8 ms over two months, which the authors say supported deferring drug therapy. The paper states that the ring “does not detect arrhythmias like PVCs”, that data review “was conducted retrospectively”, and describes no alert and no machine-learning component.

It is internally inconsistent on sequence: the case presentation says the stress-test findings prompted the retrospective ring review, while the data-analysis section says the ring data “raised clinical suspicion and prompted further evaluation through ECG and Holter monitoring”.

What it was built to do. The ring tracks heart rate, HRV and sleep for consumers; the clinic used those daily summaries as remote-monitoring markers in its diabetes program, and the paper does not claim the ring was built to find arrhythmias.

The system's part

The ring generated no alert and, per the paper, cannot detect PVCs; its heart-rate and HRV summaries were read by clinicians, once before the stress test (an elevated resting heart rate seen in remote monitoring is listed among the reasons for testing) and once retrospectively afterwards; the ECG, stress test and Holter made the diagnosis. The paper does not describe any machine-learning component, so whether an AI system was involved is not shown by the text on file. The only source is the treating clinic's case report.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. Continuous ring wear under a clinic hybrid care model in which staff review daily synced heart-rate and HRV summaries. Risk factors (type 2 diabetes, smoking) that led to a treadmill stress test. Access to ECG, 24-hour Holter and echocardiography to confirm and characterize the PVCs.

What must hold again. Clinicians act on wearable trend deviations; the clinic later wrote thresholds into its workflow (an RMSSD drop of more than 30% or a resting heart rate above 110 bpm prompts review). The patient keeps wearing and syncing the ring so that trends can be compared with a baseline.
