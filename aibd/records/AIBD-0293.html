Oura ring trends feed into a Holter showing 2,078 PVCs in 24 h

Dubai clinic case: Oura ring heart-rate and HRV summaries, read by clinicians, fed into a stress test and Holter showing 2,078 PVCs in 24 h; the ring detects no arrhythmia and issued no alert.

The system’s part
The ring generated no alert and, per the paper, cannot detect PVCs. Clinicians read its heart-rate and HRV summaries twice. Once was before the stress test (an elevated resting heart rate seen in remote monitoring is listed among the reasons for testing). Once was retrospectively afterwards. The ECG, stress test and Holter made the diagnosis. The paper describes no machine-learning component, so whether an AI system was involved is not shown by the source text. The ring did not detect PVCs or issue an alert. Its readings contributed context to a clinician-led assessment, partly retrospectively, and the case describes inconsistent sequencing of that review. Neither the presence of a learned model nor an outcome caused by it is established.

What happened

Zakaria, Roman, Almarzooqi and Hashemi of GluCare.Health, Dubai, reported a case in Cureus (16 June 2025). A man in his early 40s had type 2 diabetes and a smoking history. He had been issued an ŌURA ring under the clinic's hybrid care model. He attended for cardiac risk assessment before half-marathon training. Per the paper, testing followed his risk profile, “which included poorly controlled diabetes and an elevated resting heart rate observed during wearable-based remote monitoring”. The treadmill stress test on 18 July 2024, the date on which the cardiologist made the diagnosis, showed frequent PVCs in bigeminy and abnormal heart-rate recovery. The team then reviewed the ring's previous two weeks of data retrospectively and found a persistently elevated daytime heart rate and a downward HRV trend. A 24-hour Holter recorded 2,078 ventricular events (1.75% of beats, no couplets or runs). The PVCs were judged idiopathic, of right ventricular outflow tract origin. Management was non-pharmacological (stop smoking, cut caffeine from over five cups of coffee a day, stress reduction). A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) three days later showed no PVCs. Follow-up ring data showed resting heart rate falling from 83 to 74 bpm and RMSSD rising from 15.2 to 32.8 ms over two months. The authors say that this supported deferring drug therapy. The paper states that the ring “does not detect arrhythmias like PVCs” and that data review “was conducted retrospectively”. It describes no alert and no machine-learning component. It is inconsistent on sequence. The case presentation says that the stress-test findings prompted the retrospective ring review. The data-analysis section says that the ring data “raised clinical suspicion and prompted further evaluation through ECG and Holter monitoring”. The four authors declare that they are full-time employees or interns at GluCare and that no organization gave financial support for the work.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

The ring supplied resting-heart-rate and heart-rate-variability trends, not an arrhythmia diagnosis or automatic PVC alert.

Workflow

Continuous ring wear under a clinic hybrid care model in which staff review daily synced heart-rate and HRV summaries. Risk factors (type 2 diabetes, smoking) that led to a treadmill stress test. Access to ECG, 24-hour Holter and echocardiography to confirm and characterize the PVCs.

Human oversight

Clinicians considered the wearable trends alongside diabetes and smoking risk, then used stress testing, ECG and Holter monitoring to establish the diagnosis.

Benefits

Duration

After the lifestyle changes, ring data showed resting heart rate falling from 83 to 74 bpm and RMSSD rising from 15.2 to 32.8 ms over two months. No correction or follow-up report on the case had appeared by 29 October 2026. [1]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Clinicians act on wearable trend deviations. The clinic later wrote thresholds into its workflow (an RMSSD drop of more than 30% or a resting heart rate above 110 bpm prompts review). The patient keeps wearing and syncing the ring so that trends can be compared with a baseline.
