Oura ring trends feed into a Holter showing 2,078 PVCs in 24 h

Dubai clinic case: Oura ring heart-rate and HRV summaries, read by clinicians, fed into a stress test and Holter showing 2,078 PVCs in 24 h; the ring detects no arrhythmia and issued no alert.

ⓘ

What happened

Zakaria, Roman, Almarzooqi and Hashemi of GluCare.Health, Dubai, reported a case in Cureus (16 June 2025). A man in his early 40s had type 2 diabetes and a smoking history. He had been issued an ŌURA ring under the clinic's hybrid care model. He attended for cardiac risk assessment before half-marathon training. Per the paper, testing followed his risk profile, “which included poorly controlled diabetes and an elevated resting heart rate observed during wearable-based remote monitoring”. The treadmill stress test on 18 July 2024, the date the cardiologist made the diagnosis, showed frequent PVCs in bigeminy and abnormal heart-rate recovery. The team then reviewed the ring's previous two weeks of data retrospectively and found a persistently elevated daytime heart rate and a downward HRV trend. A 24-hour Holter recorded 2,078 ventricular events (1.75% of beats, no couplets or runs).

The PVCs were judged idiopathic, of right ventricular outflow tract origin. Management was non-pharmacological (stop smoking, cut caffeine from over five cups of coffee a day, stress reduction). A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) three days later showed no PVCs. Follow-up ring data showed resting heart rate falling from 83 to 74 bpm and RMSSD rising from 15.2 to 32.8 ms over two months. The authors say this supported deferring drug therapy. The paper states that the ring “does not detect arrhythmias like PVCs” and that data review “was conducted retrospectively”. It describes no alert and no machine-learning component. It is inconsistent on sequence. The case presentation says the stress-test findings prompted the retrospective ring review. The data-analysis section says the ring data “raised clinical suspicion and prompted further evaluation through ECG and Holter monitoring”.

Purpose. The ring tracks heart rate, HRV and sleep for consumers. The clinic used those daily summaries as remote-monitoring markers in its diabetes program. The paper does not claim the ring was built to find arrhythmias.

AI’s contribution

The ring generated no alert and, per the paper, cannot detect PVCs. Clinicians read its heart-rate and HRV summaries twice. Once was before the stress test (an elevated resting heart rate seen in remote monitoring is listed among the reasons for testing). Once was retrospectively afterwards. The ECG, stress test and Holter made the diagnosis. The paper describes no machine-learning component, so whether an AI system was involved is not shown by the source text. The only source is the treating clinic's case report.

Beneficiaries

Further evidence

Setting. Continuous ring wear under a clinic hybrid care model in which staff review daily synced heart-rate and HRV summaries. Risk factors (type 2 diabetes, smoking) that led to a treadmill stress test. Access to ECG, 24-hour Holter and echocardiography to confirm and characterize the PVCs.

Conditions. Clinicians act on wearable trend deviations. The clinic later wrote thresholds into its workflow (an RMSSD drop of more than 30% or a resting heart rate above 110 bpm prompts review). The patient keeps wearing and syncing the ring so that trends can be compared with a baseline.
