ABAIR's Irish voices power an NVDA screenreader for blind pupils

Trinity College Dublin's ABAIR team reports that its Irish synthetic voices power an NVDA screenreader plugin with Braille output, tested with visually impaired pupils; no outcome is reported.

  • Not rated a person

    The paper reports extensive testing with visually impaired school children and the motivating need (three Irish Braille books). No identified pupil, date or measured gain.

  • Not rated society

    Access to written Irish for the 4,701 school-age blind or visually impaired people cited from the 2016 census is the stated aim. No uptake figure is given.

Depth runs from 1, a convenience or time saved, to 5, a life or a livelihood preserved.

Status
Intended benefit. The developers report a functioning Irish screenreader and user testing. A measured pupil-level benefit remains unestablished.

Not isolated. The evidence is limited to the developers' account. The ABAIR team's SLPAT 2022 paper says the Irish NVDA plugin with their synthetic voices was built with a blind researcher. What happened

Ní Chasaide, Barnes, Ní Chiaráin, McGuirk, Morrin, Nic Corcráin and Cummins of the ABAIR initiative described three assistive applications of ABAIR's Irish speech technology on 27 May 2022. They presented the paper at the Ninth Workshop on Speech and Language Processing for Assistive Technologies (SLPAT-2022). The first is “A screenreading facility for Irish has been developed as a plugin for the NVDA screenreader, prompted by urgent pleas from parents and grandparents of visually impaired children in the Gaeltacht or attending Irish-medium schools”. Only three books existed in Irish Braille for them. “With funding from the National Council for the Blind in Ireland (NCBI) a blind researcher worked with the ABAIR team to develop the NVDA screenreader plugin”. The plugin adds Braille output through the Liblouis translator. It lets users choose a male or female voice and the reading speed. The paper states: “The system was extensively tested with visually-impaired school children and a full technical description is available in McGuirk (2005)”. The reference list has McGuirk 2015, an M.Phil. dissertation. The paper names no child, school, date or measured result. It describes the voices as text-to-speech synthesis without stating the synthesis method. ABAIR staff wrote in a paper presented at SIGUL 2023, a meeting on under-resourced languages in Dublin (18 to 20 August 2023), that the project's accessibility applications include a downloadable screen-reading facility for the visually impaired. The Irish Braille tables in the Liblouis translator credit Ronan McGuirk (2019) and the Irish National Braille and Alternative Formats Authority (INBAF), which issued an updated version in 2025. ABAIR's screen reader page, which carries a 2026 copyright line, explains how to set up NVDA for Irish Braille output and says that users can also have the text read aloud. No cited source is independent of the developers.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

Irish text became synthesized speech through an NVDA plugin, with optional Braille output through Liblouis.

Workflow

Multidialect Irish text-to-speech voices from the ABAIR initiative, funded under the 20-Year Strategy for the Irish Language. A blind researcher co-developing the NVDA plugin with NCBI funding, with Liblouis Braille output.

Benefits

Duration

ABAIR staff wrote in August 2023 that the screen-reading facility was available for download. ABAIR's screen reader page, which carries a 2026 copyright line, still explains how to set up NVDA for Irish Braille output. Neither source reports how many people use it. [1] [2]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Users and their support networks (teachers, family) are involved in design and testing, which the authors state as a guiding principle. The free NVDA screenreader is the pupil's platform.

Evidence

Original evidence

The 2022 workshop paper by the ABAIR team is the original account of the plugin and of its testing with school children. A 2023 demonstration paper by ABAIR staff and ABAIR's own screen reader page add only that the facility is offered. No cited source is independent of the developers. [1] [2]
