TIAA's AI helps stop a $3 million scam withdrawal, CEO says

withdraw his entire $3 million retirement portfolio

TIAA's AI flagged a 76-year-old's bid to withdraw his whole $3M retirement; staff and his daughter stopped a scam, its CEO told Fortune.

What happened

TIAA president and CEO Thasunda Brown Duckett, on Fortune's Titans and Disruptors of Industry podcast released on 17 June 2026, said that a 76-year-old participant had recently asked to withdraw his entire $3 million retirement portfolio. TIAA's AI tool flagged the request as out of pattern with his history; a portfolio manager escalated it to the fraud team, which spent hours trying to convince him he was being deceived. A fraud specialist eventually reached his daughter, and TIAA stopped the money from moving; the participant later told the firm, 'You saved my bacon.' The date, place, scam and system are not reported, and Duckett said AI by itself would not necessarily have protected him. The date used is the podcast's.

What it was built to do. TIAA's monitoring system exists to spot transactions that do not fit a participant's history and raise a flag for human review; catching an out-of-pattern $3 million withdrawal was that purpose. Duckett framed the case as AI and humans working together, saying AI by itself would not necessarily have protected the participant.

The system's part

not assessed Duckett says TIAA's AI tool was first to notice something was off, flagging the full-liquidation request as out of pattern with the participant's history. The flag's counterfactual contribution is not shown: a request by a 76-year-old to withdraw an entire $3 million portfolio might have drawn human review without it, and Duckett herself says AI by itself would not necessarily have protected him. The persuasion took hours of human work and the turning point, by her account, was a fraud specialist reaching the daughter. The system is unnamed, no date, location or scam type is given, and no source independent of TIAA's CEO describes the case.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it
  • A transaction-monitoring model with enough of the participant's history to score a request to liquidate a $3 million portfolio in one go as out of pattern
  • A portfolio manager who treated the flag as grounds to escalate rather than execute, and a fraud team willing to spend hours with a customer who, in Duckett's words, had almost been trained to defend the scam
  • A family member the fraud specialist could reach, which Duckett credits as the turning point
What must hold again
  • The flagged request must actually be fraud; Duckett gives no false-positive rate and the scam itself is not described
  • The firm must be able to hold a transfer while it persuades the customer; Duckett says AI by itself would not necessarily have protected him
  • The customer or a relative must be reachable and open to persuasion before the funds move
