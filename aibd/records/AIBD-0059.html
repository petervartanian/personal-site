CapisciAMe recognizes words from severely impaired speech

CapisciAMe, a speech recognizer tailored to a woman with aphasia and dysarthria, identified 72.65% of 936 recorded test words; twelve professional listeners averaged 56.75% on a separate task.

What happened

A 34-year-old right-handed woman took part in a single-case proof of concept at the University of Messina and IRCCS Centro Neurolesi Bonino-Pulejo. She has right hemiparesis, global aphasia and dysarthria from a massive left-hemisphere hemorrhage at age 19. A compact convolutional neural network in CapisciAMe used recordings from her and 69 other Italian speakers with dysarthria. She supplied 1,120 utterances of 13 target words. The methods describe 21,947 training and 1,162 validation utterances, with her November 2022 recordings held out for testing. The tailored recognizer identified 680 of 936 test utterances (72.65%). The developers first posted the study as a JMIR preprint on 7 August 2025, with a listener mean of 56.2%. The case report appeared in Frontiers in Rehabilitation Sciences on 3 June 2026. In it, twelve rehabilitation professionals averaged 56.75% on a separate open-response listening task with 26 recordings each. The authors state that the items heard by listeners were not logged, so the recognizer and the listeners cannot be compared item by item. No real-world communication use is documented.

Details

AI system

Model

Cnn-trad-fpool3.

Role

The personalized network recognized spoken words for a communication aid.

Configuration

The cnn-trad-fpool3 model used a combined training corpus from the participant and 69 other Italian speakers with dysarthria. The participant’s held-out test contained 936 recordings of 13 words. [1]

Inputs and outputs

CapisciAMe classified recorded utterances into 13 Italian target words. The methods describe training data from the participant and 69 other speakers with dysarthria, with her November recordings held out for testing.

Workflow

Speaker-dependent training on hundreds of the patient's own utterances. Closed, small target vocabulary. Multiple prompting modalities during recording.

Benefits

Reach

One woman took part, and no source documents that she used the recognizer for real communication. The tailored recognizer identified 680 of 936 test utterances (72.65%). [1] [2]

Duration

The test recordings date from November 2022. No later report describes the woman using CapisciAMe in daily life. [1] [2]

Measurement

Measurement

Word-level accuracy of a speaker-dependent recognizer on held-out recordings, set against open-response transcription by familiar rehabilitation professionals. [1]

Setting and sample

One 34-year-old woman with global aphasia and dysarthria in Messina, Italy; 936 test utterances of 13 words; 12 professional listeners with 26 recordings each. [1]

Measures

Word-level recognition accuracy. [1]

Results

72.65% for the recognizer against a listener mean of 56.75%. [1]

Limitations

One participant and a 13-word vocabulary. The listeners heard different items in a different format, and the authors state that the items were not logged for a paired comparison. [1]

People and places

Dependencies

The setup depended on repeated clinician-led recording and labeling in a quiet room with a headset microphone.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Patient can produce consistent vocalizations for each target word. Quiet recording environment with a headset microphone. Clinician time to collect and label training data.

Availability

The app was tested as a single-person proof of concept; the report does not document everyday communication use.

The Frontiers in Rehabilitation Sciences case report and the JMIR preprint of 7 August 2025 are two versions of one study by the developers of CapisciAMe and the clinical team. The preprint gives a listener mean of 56.2%, and the published report gives 56.75%. [1] [2]
