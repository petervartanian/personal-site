Ithaca raises historians' accuracy from 25 to 72 percent

off the scale

raised historians' accuracy from 25 to 72 percent when they worked with it

Ithaca restores and dates Greek inscriptions with measured accuracy gains; no specific accepted restoration verified (general benefit without an event).

What happened

On 9 March 2022 Nature published Ithaca, a deep neural network built by DeepMind with the Universities of Venice Ca' Foscari, Oxford and Athens (Assael, Sommerschield, Prag, de Freitas and others) to restore missing text in damaged ancient Greek inscriptions and to attribute them to a place and date. Ithaca restored text with 62 percent accuracy on its own and raised historians' accuracy from 25 to 72 percent when they worked with it; it attributed inscriptions geographically with 71 percent accuracy and dated them to within about 30 years. Its average date of 421 BC for a group of disputed Athenian decrees agreed with recent independent re-dating. The fetched sources report accuracy studies and a public interface, not a specific inscription whose restoration epigraphers have since accepted.

What it was built to do. Built to restore, geographically attribute and date ancient Greek inscriptions; the reported capability was that purpose.

The system's part

Ithaca's outputs were evaluated against held-out ground truth and in a study of historians working with and without it; the 421 BC dating is a model prediction consistent with evidence established by other means, not an outcome the model caused.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. A large digitized corpus of inscriptions with known provenance and date. A neural network trained on artificially damaged text. Evaluation against held-out inscriptions and with historians in the loop.

What must hold again. A verified category-1 event would require an epigrapher-accepted restoration or re-dating attributable to the model and documented in a non-vendor source.
