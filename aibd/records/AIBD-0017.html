Ithaca raises historians' accuracy on Greek inscriptions from 25 to 72%

DeepMind's Ithaca, a deep neural network, raised historians' accuracy at restoring damaged ancient Greek inscriptions from 25 to 72 percent in a test; no source reports an accepted restoration.

  • Not rated society

    The sources give accuracy measurements and a predicted date that agrees with independent re-dating of Athenian decrees. No source reports a verified result in scholarly use that came from Ithaca.

What happened

On 9 March 2022 Nature published Ithaca, a deep neural network built by DeepMind with the Universities of Venice Ca' Foscari, Oxford and Athens. Authors include Assael, Sommerschield, Prag and de Freitas. Ithaca restores missing text in damaged ancient Greek inscriptions and attributes them to place and date. Ithaca restored text with 62 percent accuracy alone and raised historians' accuracy from 25 to 72 percent when they worked with it. It attributed inscriptions geographically with 71 percent accuracy and dated them to within about 30 years. Its average date of 421 BC for a group of disputed Athenian decrees agreed with recent independent re-dating. The cited sources report accuracy studies and a public interface. No cited source reports that epigraphers have accepted a restoration that Ithaca proposed for a specific inscription. In 2024 Eric Cullhed of Uppsala University reran Ithaca in a study posted on arXiv. In the arXiv version he reports that Ithaca reached 60.9 percent top-1 restoration accuracy on a test set shared with the original paper, close to the reported figure. He also reports that Ithaca reached 46.5 percent on six inscriptions edited after its training data were assembled. He reports that a fine-tuned Llama model did slightly better at restoration and worse at place and date. On 23 July 2025 Nature published Aeneas, the successor model from DeepMind for Latin inscriptions. The study by Cullhed appeared in Digital Scholarship in the Humanities on 25 November 2025.

Details

AI system

Model

Ithaca, a transformer-based neural network.

Role

Ithaca proposed missing text, dates, and places of origin for ancient inscriptions.

Configuration

Researchers used the 2022 Ithaca system.

Inputs and outputs

Ithaca processed transcriptions of damaged Greek inscriptions and returned ranked text restorations, possible regions, and a distribution of dates.

Workflow

A large digitized corpus of inscriptions with known provenance and date. A neural network trained on artificially damaged text. Evaluation against held-out inscriptions and with historians in the loop.

Human oversight

Two historians assessed ranked suggestions in an evaluation using artificially hidden text. Their restoration accuracy rose from 25% without Ithaca to 72% with it.

Benefits

Reach

The human test involved two participants, and no source counts the people who use the public interface. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Duration

In a study posted in 2024, Eric Cullhed reports that Ithaca reached 60.9 percent top-1 restoration accuracy on the shared test set, close to the original figure. On six inscriptions edited after its training data were assembled, Ithaca reached 46.5 percent. DeepMind followed Ithaca with Aeneas, a successor model for Latin inscriptions published in Nature on 23 July 2025. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Measurement

Measurement

Study 1: Character error rate and top-1 accuracy for restoring 1 to 10 hidden characters in held-out inscriptions, with scores averaged for each sequence length. Study 2: Character error rate and top-1 and top-20 accuracy for restoring up to 10 characters, with Ithaca rerun by the author. [1] [5]

Setting and sample

Study 1: Inscriptions held out from the I.PHI corpus; two graduate students of ancient history restored a subset with and without Ithaca's top 20 suggestions. Study 2: A test set shared with the Ithaca paper (PHI identifiers ending in 3) and 4,111 samples drawn from six inscriptions that were not in PHI or online as of December 2023. [1] [5]

Measures

Study 1: Top-1 restoration accuracy. Study 2: Top-1 restoration accuracy. [1] [5]

Results

Study 1: Top-1 accuracy rose from 25 percent to 72 percent when the evaluators used Ithaca; Ithaca alone reached 62 percent. Study 2: Ithaca reached 60.9 percent top-1 accuracy on the shared set and 46.5 percent on the new inscriptions; the fine-tuned model reached 61.4 and 48.9 percent. [1] [5]

Additional value from AI

Study 1: 47 percentage points of top-1 accuracy for the evaluators. [1] [5]

Limitations

Study 1: Two evaluators only. The authors write that the setup is not a direct comparison of historians and the model and that the evaluators do not stand for all historians. They also write that Ithaca cannot make choices on the basis of historical and material context. Study 2: The new-inscription set comes from six inscriptions only. The author retrained his model on a different split for part of the comparison. [1] [5]

Denominators

Study 1: Ranked list of Ithaca's top 20 restoration hypotheses for each damaged text. [1] [5]

Materials for replication

Study 1: Code at github.com/deepmind/ithaca under Apache License 2.0, with trained weights and a public interface. [1] [5]
