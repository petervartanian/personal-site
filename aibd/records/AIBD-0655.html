NICE backs AI help for spotting fractures on X-rays

What happened

NICE notes that missed fractures are reported to be the most common diagnostic error in emergency departments. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) assessed AI software that marks suspected fractures on X-rays for the clinician who reads them. Its external assessment group found 16 eligible studies, most of them on BoneView and RBfracture. In these studies, clinicians who read with the software detected more fractures without a loss of specificity. In a BoneView study of emergency physicians, sensitivity rose from 61% to 74%. RBfracture raised it from 74% to 83%, and Rayvolve from 79% to 94%. In guidance published on 14 January 2025, the committee concluded that the evidence suggests that these technologies have the potential to improve fracture detection, and it allowed Rayvolve, TechCare Alert, BoneView and RBfracture in NHS urgent care while more evidence is generated. Most studies were retrospective case-control reader studies, none took place in UK urgent care, and reading times changed by only a few seconds. The committee noted that unassisted readers were less accurate than expected, so the gain may be overstated.

Sources

  1. Peer-reviewed study, by the makers.

    Duron L, Ducarouge A, Gillibert A, et al. Assessment of an AI Aid in Detection of Adult Appendicular Skeletal Fractures by Emergency Physicians and Radiologists: A Multicenter Cross-sectional Diagnostic Study. Radiology. 2021;300(1):120-129.

    The AI aid improved the sensitivity of physicians by 8.7% (95% CI: 3.1, 14.2; P = .003 for superiority)

  2. Regulatory document, by authors with no tie to the system.

    National Institute for Health and Care Excellence. Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies to help detect fractures on X-rays in urgent care: early value assessment. HealthTech guidance HTG739, section 3: Committee discussion. 14 January 2025.

    Diagnostic accuracy studies typically found improved sensitivity of fracture detection, without reduced specificity, by healthcare professionals assisted by AI software compared with unassisted interpretation.

  3. Regulatory document, by authors with no tie to the system.

    National Institute for Health and Care Excellence. HTG739, section 1: Recommendations. 14 January 2025.

    Four artificial intelligence (AI) technologies can be used in the NHS during the evidence generation period as options to help healthcare professionals detect fractures on X-rays in urgent care.

Details

AI system

Workflow

The software marked suspected fractures on the X-ray for the reader. A trained clinician made the final interpretation.

Measurement

Measurement

Sensitivity and specificity of clinicians reading with and without the software against a reference standard. [2]

Setting and sample

16 studies, mostly on BoneView (8) and RBfracture (5). [2]

Measures

Sensitivity and specificity for fractures. [2]

Results

Sensitivity 61% to 74% (BoneView), 74% to 83% (RBfracture), 79% to 94% (Rayvolve); no loss of specificity. [2]

Additional value from AI

Higher sensitivity by 9 to 15 percentage points. [2]

Limitations

Mostly retrospective case-control designs; no UK urgent care studies; readers saw X-rays without patient history. Unassisted readers were less accurate than expected. [2]

Denominators

Reading sessions with and without the software. [2]

Materials for replication

External assessment group report on the NICE website. [2]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Clinicians in urgent care read with the software as they did in the studies. The software performs on local X-ray equipment as it did in testing.

Replication attempts

1 follow-up entry. Benefit recurred: 1.
