LAARMA warnings slow drivers at a Queensland cassowary crossing

The LAARMA machine-learning system detected cassowaries with 97% recall and 77% precision, and drivers slowed by an average of 6.30 km/h and 5.06 km/h at two trial zones when its signs warned them.

What happened

The Large Animal Activated Roadside Monitoring and Alert System (LAARMA) was developed by the University of Sydney's Australian Centre for Robotics and CARRS-Q at the Queensland University of Technology. The developers worked with the Queensland Department of Transport and Main Roads under an iMOVE project. The system uses a machine-learning pipeline to detect large animals up to 200 m away, and it triggers roadside variable message signs. The project summary states that the system was installed in late January 2024. The consortium's iMOVE project page describes a five-month field trial: Three months of data collection came first, and two months of on-road trialing followed. The researchers report 97% recall and 77% precision. They report that drivers slowed by an average of 6.30 km/h and 5.06 km/h at two trial zones when the signs showed a warning. Their injury-risk estimates use the Nilsson model, and the research summary does not establish an observed fall in collisions. On 30 July 2025 the ABC reported that the department had used the AI for three months at a busy cassowary crossing on the Kennedy Highway at Kuranda, Far North Queensland. The ABC gave no trial dates. The department told the ABC that fatal crashes fell by 31%. About two dozen cassowaries were killed on Queensland roads the previous year. In May 2026 a department spokesperson told Mongabay that the technology shows strong potential. No wider rollout has been reported.

Details

AI system

Autonomy

Detection automatically activated a warning sign. Drivers retained control of their vehicles and had to respond by slowing down and watching for cassowaries.

Workflow

A known animal crossing hotspot with high strike rates. Self-training detection pipeline that improved over the trial. Variable message signs drivers can see in time to slow.

Human oversight

The system only switched on roadside warning signs, and drivers decided whether to slow down. The department said that the equipment would be removed after the trial. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Benefits

Reach

No source counts the drivers or cassowaries that benefited. The measured effect is an average speed reduction at two trial zones. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Duration

The field trial lasted five months: Three months of data collection came first, and two months with warnings shown to drivers followed. The department said that the equipment would be removed after the trial, and no wider rollout had been reported by May 2026. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Measurement

Measurement

Speed sensors at four sites (two in the approach zone, two in the event zone) recorded vehicle speeds while the sign showed or did not show a warning; detections were checked against manually verified cassowary sightings; crash effects were modeled with the Nilsson power model. [1]

Setting and sample

One cassowary crossing on the Kennedy Highway at Kuranda, Far North Queensland; five-month trial from late January 2024 with two months of on-road warnings; 287 manually verified cassowary sightings. [1]

Measures

Average vehicle speed change when the warning showed; detection precision and recall. [1]

Results

Speeds fell by 6.30 km/h and 5.06 km/h at the two event-zone sites and by 4.26 km/h and 3.44 km/h at the two approach-zone sites; precision 0.77 and recall 0.97. [1]

Additional value from AI

The detector triggered every warning, so the speed changes measure the effect of the AI-triggered alerts. [1]

Limitations

The summary gives no confidence intervals, counts of vehicles or absolute speeds. Crash reductions are modeled from speeds and were not observed. The posted limit at the site was 60 km/h during roadwork. The developers wrote the evaluation. [1]

Denominators

Drivers passing one site during two months of on-road warnings. [1]

Materials for replication

The developers state that the source code is freely available in two GitHub repositories, acfr/CassDetect and acfr/laarma. [1]

People and places

Dependencies

The roadside system combined visible-light and thermal cameras, an edge computer, and an electronic warning sign. Reliable power and unobstructed sensor placement were practical requirements.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Detection accuracy on the target species. Driver compliance with flashing warnings. Permanent funding, which TMR said it was still pursuing.

Replication attempts

1 follow-up entry. Outcome unresolved: 1.

Availability

The project released source code for CassDetect and LAARMA. Permanent roadside deployment still required equipment, maintenance, and funding.
