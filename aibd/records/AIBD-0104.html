LAARMA alerts cut fatal crashes 31% in Kuranda cassowary trial

off the scale

fatal crashes fell 31 percent during the trial

TMR three-month trial at Kuranda: 97% cassowary detection, drivers slowed 6.6 km/h, fatal crashes down 31% (reported 30 Jul 2025).

What happened

The Large Animal Activated Roadside Monitoring and Alert System (LAARMA) was developed by the University of Sydney's Australian Centre for Robotics and QUT's CARRS-Q with the Queensland Department of Transport and Main Roads under an iMOVE project; it uses a machine-learning pipeline to detect large animals up to 200 m away and triggers roadside variable message signs. The ABC reported on 30 July 2025 that TMR had used the AI for three months at a busy cassowary crossing on the Kennedy Highway at Kuranda, Far North Queensland (trial dates not given, so the report date is used), whereas the consortium's iMOVE project page describes a five-month field trial, three months of data collection followed by two months of on-road trialling. TMR told the ABC that the system identified cassowaries with 97 percent accuracy, that drivers slowed by an average of 6.6 km/h at the flashing sign and that fatal crashes fell 31 percent during the trial; the iMOVE page reports speed reductions averaging 6.30 km/h and 5.06 km/h at two test sites and gives no collision figure. About two dozen cassowaries were killed on Queensland roads the previous year.

What it was built to do. Detect large animals near the road in real time and warn drivers via variable message signs to reduce animal-vehicle collisions.

The system's part

Machine-learning detection of cassowaries activated the warning signs; TMR attributes the speed reduction and the 31 percent fall in fatal crashes to the system, but the figures are trial aggregates without a peer-reviewed analysis: the 31 percent figure is reported only by the ABC citing TMR, and the consortium's project page reports detection and speed results without a collision figure.

Replication

What produced it

  • A known animal crossing hotspot with high strike rates
  • Self-training detection pipeline that improved over the trial
  • Variable message signs drivers can see in time to slow

What must hold again

  • Detection accuracy on the target species
  • Driver compliance with flashing warnings
  • Permanent funding, which TMR said it was still pursuing
