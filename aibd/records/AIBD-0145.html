What happened

HM Revenue & Customs launched Connect in 2010–11. Its annual report calls it an IT system that joins a billion pieces of data across its tax regimes to help identify fraud. In a freedom-of-information response reported by Pinsent Masons on 8 October 2025, HMRC said Connect cases generated an average £3.4 billion of additional annual yield. It was about £4.6 billion in 2024–25, with around 4,300 staff using it. The Comptroller and Auditor General's report in HMRC's 2024–25 accounts records that HMRC used TRUCE (Transaction Risking Upstream in a Connect Environment). It served to identify and stop VAT and income-tax repayments needing investigation. IDRS replaced it from August 2023. Official sources describe data analytics and risk rules, not AI.

What it was built to do. Match and analyze data from many sources to identify tax non-compliance and risky repayment claims

The system's part

Not assessed. Yield attribution to Connect comes from HMRC's FOI response as reported by Pinsent Masons. No independent evaluation apportions yield between the system and caseworkers. The NAO's audit report describes TRUCE/IDRS as automated risk-assessment of repayment claims whose controls it reviewed, without quantifying stopped repayments. The cited sources describe the system as data analytics and risk rules rather than machine learning.

Replication

What produced it. Linking of bank, marketplace, land-registry and other data to taxpayer records. Risk rules and scorecards feeding compliance cases to about 4,300 staff. Upstream risking of VAT and Self Assessment repayment claims (TRUCE, then IDRS).

What must hold again. Legal gateways to third-party data. Staff capacity to work cases generated by the system. Governance of risk rules, which the C&AG reviews annually.
