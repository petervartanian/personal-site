What happened

Harmon, Witt, Friedman and Attia reported a case in the Cardiovascular Digital Health Journal on 24 August 2021. A 41-year-old man with class III obesity and sleep apnea had no cardiac history. Two electrocardiograms (ECGs) on Mayo's AI-ECG dashboard gave probabilities of reduced ejection fraction of 97.88% and 98.67%. Echocardiography the next day confirmed reduced function. Guideline-directed therapy for new heart failure with reduced ejection fraction began, and at four months his ejection fraction had improved to 55%. The authors attributed the improvement to therapy, recovery from post-viral sequelae and weight loss. All four authors are at Mayo Clinic, which developed the algorithm and licensed it to Eko and Anumana. The authors disclose that Friedman, Attia and other Mayo inventors may receive financial benefit. On 5 January 2022 an Imperial College London team with no competing interests reported in The Lancet Digital Health that it had tested a single-lead version of the Mayo AI-ECG on 1,050 patients of the National Health Service (NHS). The team found that the system detected an ejection fraction of 40% or lower. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) cleared Anumana's Low Ejection Fraction AI-ECG Algorithm on 28 September 2023. It cleared Eko's stethoscope version, trained on Mayo's ECG data, on 28 March 2024. Mayo Clinic authors published further cases. In 2024 Dhawan and colleagues reported in Pacing and Clinical Electrophysiology that AI-ECGs taken six years before presentation had predicted a cardiomyopathy induced by left bundle branch block. Also in 2024, Kenyon and colleagues reported in the Journal of Personalized Medicine that an AI-ECG had prompted an early diagnosis of AL amyloidosis. In 2025 Liu and colleagues reported in HeartRhythm Case Reports on the use of the AI-ECG in a decision about defibrillator therapy. No cited source outside Mayo describes these cases. The FDA cleared Anumana's ECG-AI Low Ejection Fraction 12-Lead algorithm on 28 July 2025. In a review published on 30 May 2026, Baker Heart and Diabetes Institute researchers reported that externally tested AI-ECG models, the Mayo model among them, showed promising accuracy for systolic dysfunction in people without symptoms. They warned of a high risk of bias.
