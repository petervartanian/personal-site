Utrecht implanted BCI gives a woman with ALS seven years of use

seven years of independent at-home use

A fully implanted ECoG interface turned band-power changes from attempted hand movement into clicks; she typed independently from 2016 and used it at home about seven years until ALS atrophy ended it.

What happened

Vansteensel et al. (NEJM, 12 November 2016) described a fully implanted brain-computer interface, subdural electrodes over the motor cortex with a subcutaneous transmitter, in a woman with late-stage ALS at UMC Utrecht: 28 weeks after electrode placement she controlled a typing program at about two letters per minute, used it outdoors where eye tracking failed as her only means of communication, and the authors note that suppressing unintended clicks 'required several months of refining the algorithms'. The 2024 NEJM follow-up reports seven years of independent at-home use, starting seven months after implantation; features from a single electrode pair (low- and high-frequency band power during attempted hand movement) were converted into click commands for spelling and a caregiver call; use declined close to six years after implantation as click accuracy fell, ended by March 2023, and the authors attribute this to ALS-related atrophy rather than device failure. Neither fetched text names a machine-learning decoder; the earlier verifier treated the decoder as threshold-based. The date is the 2016 publication date.

What it was built to do. Provide autonomous communication for a person with late-stage ALS who could no longer rely on eye tracking

The system's part

The interface's decoding is described as converting low- and high-frequency band-power changes from one electrode pair into click commands, with algorithms refined over months; no machine-learning decoder is named in the fetched texts, so whether this is an AI system in the database's sense is not established. The benefit itself (seven years of home communication) is documented in two peer-reviewed reports by the developers.

Replication

What produced it

  • Subdural electrodes over motor cortex with an implanted transmitter
  • Band-power features from attempted hand movement converted into click commands
  • Months of algorithm refinement to suppress unintended clicks

What must hold again

  • Neural signal amplitude remains sufficient (it declined with ALS-related atrophy after about six years)
  • The participant can attempt hand movement reliably
