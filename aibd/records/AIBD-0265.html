An implanted interface restored communication for nearly seven years

A woman with locked-in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis used the Utrecht NeuroProsthesis, an implanted brain-computer interface, for nearly seven years of independent at-home use, until March 2023.

What happened

A woman with locked-in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) received the Utrecht NeuroProsthesis in October 2015. After training, she selected letters and called a caregiver by attempting to move her hand. The makers reported that 28 weeks after electrode placement she controlled a computer typing program accurately and independently, at the equivalent of two letters per minute. Seven to nine months after surgery, she rated the implant and her own eye tracker on the Psychosocial Impact of Assistive Devices Scale. She scored the implant 1.1 for competence, 2.2 for adaptability and 1.0 for self-esteem, and the eye tracker 1.3, 1.5 and 0.6. Writing in Scientific American on 17 November 2016, R. Douglas Fields reported that Andrew Schwartz of the University of Pittsburgh, who was not involved in the study, called the result a significant achievement. Niels Birbaumer of Tübingen University warned that such implants may carry an unknown risk for people with advanced ALS, and Phillip Kennedy of Neural Signals said that one to two letters a minute did not justify a craniotomy. Caregivers set up the equipment, but she then used it without research staff. It became her only technological means of communication after she lost reliable eye tracking. Home use continued for nearly seven years. Nighttime use stopped in October 2022, and home use ended in March 2023 as control became unreliable. Researchers considered ALS-related brain changes the likely explanation, without proving a single cause. The documented decoder combined brain-signal features and fitted thresholds. The sources name no machine-learning model.

Details

AI system

Model

A decoder based on normalized brain-signal power and fitted thresholds. No machine-learning model is identified. [1]

Provider

UMC Utrecht developed the interface. Medtronic supplied the implant, and Tobii Dynavox supplied the communication software. [1]

Role

The implant recorded brain activity. A decoder converted attempted movements into clicks for spelling and calling a caregiver. [1]

Configuration

The decoder combined low- and high-frequency brain activity and applied individually fitted click rules. The tablet ran Communicator 5. [1] [2] [3]

Inputs and outputs

Electrical activity from the motor cortex became click commands in a scanning communication interface. [1]

Autonomy

The participant chose when to generate a click. The software scanned letters and converted her selections into text or a caregiver call. [1]

Workflow

A caregiver positioned the antenna and connected the tablet. The participant then selected letters or called for attention by attempting hand movements. [1]

Human oversight

Clinicians implanted and monitored the device. Researchers adjusted decoding settings, and caregivers helped set up and maintain the home equipment. [1]

AI contribution

Fitted signal-processing rules converted attempted hand movements into selections. The evidence supports this decoding contribution without identifying a learned AI model. [1]

Benefits

Beneficiaries

One woman with advanced ALS who was 58 when she entered the study. [1]

Reach

One participant, followed repeatedly. The two papers do not describe two separate beneficiaries. [1] [2]

Distribution and access

This was one participant with suitable motor signals and caregiver support. The study does not establish the same result for all people with locked-in ALS. [1]

Duration

Independent home use lasted nearly seven years. Nighttime use stopped in October 2022, and home use ended in March 2023 as control became unreliable. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Measurement

Measurement

Researchers tested clicks and spelling, collected satisfaction ratings, logged when the home system was available, and monitored brain signals and imaging. [1]

Setting and sample

One participant at UMC Utrecht and in her home. Repeated task runs are observations from that same person. [1]

Measures

Selection accuracy, spelling speed, time with the system available at home, satisfaction, and persistence of usable control. [1]

Results

Initial spelling accuracy averaged 89%. With word prediction, she selected about two letters per minute. Home use continued for nearly seven years. [1] [2]

Additional value from AI

The study demonstrates the whole implanted interface. It does not isolate a machine-learning contribution, and its decoder is described through signal processing and fitted thresholds. [1]

Limitations

The result concerns one person. Home logs show availability, not continuous active communication. Declining control coincided with ALS progression, without proving a single cause. [1]

Denominators

The initial report describes 67 two-hour research sessions over 262 days. Home use without investigators began on day 197 after implantation. [1]

Materials for replication

The original paper links two example task runs in Dryad. The follow-up supplement describes signal analysis. The thesis documents click thresholds and reproduces the follow-up supplement. [1]

People and places

Participants

UMC Utrecht researchers developed and evaluated the interface. Medtronic supplied the implant, and the participant and her caregivers used the home system. [1]

Dependencies

The system required implanted electrodes, a chest transmitter, an external antenna and receiver, a tablet, communication software, and continuing caregiver support. [1]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Preserved motor signals, adequate cognition, caregiver support, individual training, and access to replacement equipment were necessary. [1]

Replication attempts

The 2024 report follows the same woman, rather than a second user of this device. [2]

Availability

The interface was investigational and used implant components off label. Replacement-device availability became a concern during follow-up. [1]

Institutional decision

UMC Utrecht tested an implanted means of communication for people with severe paralysis. [1] [2]

Grounds

The study received ethics approval and informed consent. Implant components were used off label. [1] [2]

Action and result

Clinicians implanted the system and researchers supported its use and adaptation at home. [1] [2]

Success criterion

Researchers evaluated communication control, usability, and long-term stability. The case report did not define a single financial or clinical pass threshold. [1] [2]

Evidence

Language

English. No translation was used. [1]

Original evidence

The two New England Journal of Medicine papers and the 2025 dissertation come from the Utrecht team. The dissertation reproduces the 2024 report and its supplementary material. Scientific American reported the 2016 result and quoted researchers at three other institutions. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Evidence links

The original paper establishes communication, the follow-up establishes its duration, and the thesis supplies decoder and safety details. [1] [2] [3]

Grades

Alternative assessments

Depth 4 would require a separately established serious harm averted. The technical AI boundary remains distinct from the depth of the documented benefit.
