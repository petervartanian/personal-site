Algebra software shows an uncertain gain in a trial's second year

Students using Cognitive Tutor Algebra I scored 0.21 standard deviations higher in the second year, with no statistically significant difference after correction for multiple comparisons.

What happened

Researchers randomly assigned 147 schools in seven U.S. states to Cognitive Tutor Algebra I or their usual curriculum during the 2007–08 and 2008–09 school years. The program combined classroom teaching, materials, and adaptive tutoring. Each year involved a new cohort of students, so no student had a second year of exposure. The revised analysis included 11,066 high-school students and 5,519 middle-school students. The researchers estimated a gain of 0.21 standard deviations for the second high-school cohort. First-year and middle-school results were not statistically significant. In its revised review, the What Works Clearinghouse found that the second-year result also failed its corrected significance threshold. Two analyses of the trial's software logs by Adam Sales and John Pane, one of them with Anita Israni, were posted in 2018, and one was published in December 2020. They found that students often left sections without showing mastery, more often in the second year, when the effect was larger. Their methods gave conflicting answers on whether hints raised or lowered posttest scores.

Details

AI system

Model

An ACT-R-based cognitive model tracked students’ solution steps and mastery of individual skills. [4]

Provider

Carnegie Learning. [1] [2]

Role

The tutor checked students’ steps, provided hints, and selected practice for skills that needed work. [1] [2]

Configuration

Classes generally used the textbook and classroom activities three days a week and tutoring software two days a week. [1] [2]

Inputs and outputs

Students entered algebra steps. The tutor returned feedback and selected further exercises. [1] [2]

Autonomy

The tutor selected exercises and gave feedback. Teachers remained responsible for instruction. [1] [2]

Workflow

Teachers taught the curriculum and supervised students’ work with the software. [1] [2]

Human oversight

Teachers received three days of training before implementation and another day during the school year. [1] [2]

Relation to AI

The intelligent tutor used a cognitive model of students’ algebra skills. [1] [2]

Benefits

Beneficiaries

Students who took Algebra I at participating high schools. [1] [2]

Reach

The revised analysis included 11,066 high-school students and 5,519 middle-school students across two annual cohorts. [1] [2]

Distribution and access

The reported gain was in the second high-school cohort. First-year and middle-school estimates were not statistically significant. [1] [2]

Duration

Each cohort studied Algebra I for one school year. Each year involved a new cohort, so no student had a second year of exposure. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] [8]

Expected?

Improved algebra learning was the intended benefit. [1] [2]

Measurement

Measurement

Students took standardized algebra readiness and proficiency tests. Researchers adjusted the end-of-year comparison for baseline scores and other characteristics. [1] [2]

Setting and sample

The trial included 73 high schools and 74 middle schools. The revised analysis excluded students without observed baseline tests. [1] [2]

Measures

Algebra proficiency. [1] [2]

Results

The revised high-school estimate was −0.07 standard deviations in year one and +0.21 in year two. [1] [2]

Additional value from AI

The software’s separate contribution was not estimated. [1] [2]

Limitations

The second-year estimate depended on adjustment for student characteristics. Its p-value of .04 did not meet the Clearinghouse’s corrected threshold of .025. [2] [3]

Denominators

Students generally used the tutor for about 40% of class time during the school year. [1] [2]

Materials for replication

The 2024 reanalysis provides public code. Its full replication data are withheld to protect the participating schools’ identities. [6]

Money

Kind of value

Annualized curriculum adoption cost. [5]

Amount

Excluding Houston, estimated annual cost was US$97.18 per student for Cognitive Tutor and US$27.88 for comparison curricula. [5]

Currency and prices

U.S. Dollars. The estimate used the vendor’s 2006 price guide to represent ordinary district adoption. [5]

Period and unit

Per student per year, with five-year adoption and equipment lives. [5]

Realization

These are estimated adoption costs, not the trial’s discounted invoices. [5]

Recipients and payers

School districts would pay for materials, software, equipment, and training. [5]

Gross or net

The estimates compare expenditure. They do not value learning gains or calculate net benefits. [5]

Included costs

Costs include textbooks, software, computers, and curriculum-specific teacher training. [5]

Comparison

The comparison is the cost of the curricula that control schools used. [5]

Method

RAND combined vendor prices and district reports, annualized the costs, and weighted them by the number of schools. [5]

People and places

Participants

Carnegie Learning supplied the curriculum. Participating schools taught it, and RAND researchers evaluated it. [1] [2]

Ownership and control

Carnegie Learning supplied the commercial curriculum and tutoring software. [1] [2]

Dependencies

Schools needed computers for regular sessions, the curriculum materials, and trained algebra teachers. [1] [2]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Schools needed regular computer sessions, trained teachers, and time to implement the curriculum. [1] [2]

Replication attempts

The Clearinghouse reviewed five qualifying Algebra I studies and rated their combined results as mixed. [3]

Availability

Cognitive Tutor Algebra I was supplied commercially to schools during the trial.
