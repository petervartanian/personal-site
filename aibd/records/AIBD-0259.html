Crash warning and automatic braking cut front-to-rear crashes by 49%

In U.S. police-reported crash data, forward collision warning with automatic emergency braking cut front-to-rear crashes by 49%, and automatic braking cut pickup rear-end crash rates by 43%.

Brief from Peter

In a final rule of 9 May 2024, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) required automatic emergency braking on new light vehicles in the United States from September 2029. It found that the systems had reduced injuries and saved lives.1 On 16 November 2022, VOA Learning English reported that PARTS, a partnership of automakers and NHTSA, had found 49% fewer front-to-rear crashes when the striking vehicle had both forward collision warning and automatic emergency braking. It also reported that the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS) had found a 43% lower rear-end crash rate for pickups with automatic emergency braking.2 In the peer-reviewed version of the IIHS study, Jessica Cicchino found in her main analysis that automatic emergency braking was associated with a 34% lower risk of rear-end-striking crashes.3 In a second study, published on 6 January 2025 and covering model years 2015 to 2023, PARTS again measured a 49% reduction in front-to-rear crashes.4 The estimates cover the whole system. They do not separate the camera and radar software from the brakes or from the driver who responds to the warning. False positives in Nissan's automatic emergency braking have posed traffic risks to drivers.

Details

AI system

Autonomy

Forward collision warning alerted the driver, while automatic emergency braking could apply the brakes without waiting for a response.

Workflow

Large multi-state police-reported crash databases linked to vehicle equipment. Comparison of equipped and unequipped versions of the same models. Industry-regulator data sharing (PARTS) and an insurer-funded institute (IIHS).

Human oversight

Forward collision warning alerts the driver, and automatic emergency braking brakes the vehicle. The studies credit the whole system and do not separate the perception software from the brakes or from the driver's response to the warning. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Benefits

Reach

No source counts the people who avoided a crash. The 2022 PARTS study examined more than 12 million police-reported crashes in 13 states involving 93 vehicle models. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Duration

The 2025 PARTS study, on model years 2015 to 2023, again measured a 49% reduction in front-to-rear crashes for vehicles equipped with automatic emergency braking. Effectiveness rose from 46% for 2015–2017 models to 52% for 2021–2023 models. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Measurement

Measurement

Police-reported rear-end crashes with a pickup as the striking vehicle, linked to optional FCW or AEB equipment; quasi-induced exposure with logistic regression and crash rates per registered vehicle year with Poisson regression. [4]

Setting and sample

Pickups with and without optional FCW or AEB in 25 U.S. States, 2017 to 2020. [4]

Measures

Risk of a rear-end-striking crash of any severity, with any injuries, and with serious or fatal injuries. [4]

Results

AEB: 34% lower risk of a rear-end-striking crash of any severity and with any injuries; 76% lower risk with serious or fatal injuries (p = 0.09). FCW: 22% lower risk with any injuries and 71% with serious or fatal injuries; no change in all rear-end-striking crashes. [4]

Limitations

Observational; the serious-injury estimate for AEB was not statistically significant (p = 0.09); the systems were optional equipment on the pickups studied. [4]

Denominators

Police-reported crashes in 25 states, 2017 to 2020. [4]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Equipment status can be identified per vehicle. Crash reporting thresholds are comparable across states.

Replication attempts

1 follow-up entry. Benefit recurred: 1.

Availability

The function was built into equipped vehicles. IIHS reported that AEB was standard on only 5% of registered U.S. Pickups in 2021 and optional on another 10%.
