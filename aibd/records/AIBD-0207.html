Sheng Siong's facial recognition catches a repeat wine thief

depth 1 for an organization

matched a wine thief to six prior thefts and flagged her return

Sheng Siong's facial recognition matched a wine thief to six prior thefts and flagged her return; arrest, restitution, eight days' jail.

What happened

Between 2 and 11 September 2025 Catherine Tan Li Eng, 51, took 19 bottles of Jacob's Creek wine worth about S$556 from the Sheng Siong supermarket at Block 622D Punggol Central, Singapore, bagging the wine unscanned at self-checkout. On 10 September staff found stock discrepancies, reviewed CCTV and uploaded her face to the chain's AI-driven facial recognition system, which automatically identified the earlier thefts. When she returned at noon on 11 September the retail manager received an alert on his phone, intercepted her at the self-checkout kiosk and found three unscanned bottles, and police arrested her. On 12 May 2026 District Judge Brenda Chua jailed her for eight days on an amalgamated theft charge, after full restitution of about S$470.

What it was built to do. Sheng Siong rolled out its AI-driven facial recognition CCTV system in April 2024 as a measure against shoplifters, in collaboration with the Singapore Police Force under its Shop Theft for Retailers (STAR) program, and announced in August 2025 that it would extend it to all 83 outlets; staff upload the photograph of a suspect identified from footage and the system alerts store managers' phones when that person enters any branch. Sheng Siong said that thefts per store rose from 4.91 in 2023 to 8 in 2024, which its CEO attributed to catching cases the human eye had missed. Identifying a repeat shoplifter and flagging her return was exactly its designed purpose.

Who benefited

    organizational

    Sheng Siong's gain was small and specific: three bottles kept on 11 September 2025, full restitution of about S$470 for the rest of the 19 bottles (about S$556 in all), and the end of a seven-visit theft series at one outlet. The amount is minor for an 83-outlet chain, and the first detection was a human stock check and a manual review of CCTV, not the system.

The system's part

The system's documented contribution came after a human detection. On 10 September 2025 a staff member found discrepancies in a stock check and colleagues reviewed CCTV and saw Tan leaving without paying; they uploaded her face, and The Straits Times reports that the system 'then automatically identified the other instances' of theft, which turned one observed theft into an amalgamated charge covering seven visits. On 11 September the system's alert reached the retail manager's phone when she entered, and he intercepted her at the self-checkout kiosk with three unscanned bottles; the interception, the bag check, the police call, the prosecution and the restitution were human and institutional. No source names the vendor or the model, gives an accuracy figure, or reports a false match in this case.

Replication

What produced it. A chain-wide facial recognition layer on existing CCTV (rolled out from April 2024, extended to all 83 outlets after August 2025) with a workflow in which staff upload a suspect's face and managers get app alerts when that person re-enters any branch. A human trigger: a stock check on 10 September 2025 that revealed missing bottles, and staff who reviewed the footage and uploaded the face the same day. A repeat offender who came back to the same outlet at noon the next day, which gave the alert something to fire on. A manager on the premises with his phone, able to intercept at the self-checkout kiosk, and a police and court process (the STAR program, an amalgamated theft charge) that turned the identification into restitution and a sentence.

What must hold again. The system identifies, it does not detect: the first theft still has to be noticed by stock counts or staff, as Sheng Siong's CEO implied when he said the human eye had missed cases. Someone must act on the alert within minutes; the gain here was an interception before she left the store, not the alert itself. The match must be right and lawful: a wrong face flagged at the door would produce a wrongful stop. No source reports a false match in this case, and none names the vendor or any accuracy figure.
