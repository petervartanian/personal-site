Waymo's driverless cars draw fewer insurance claims than human drivers

Over 3,868,506 rider-only miles, the Waymo Driver had no bodily-injury claims and 76% fewer property-damage claims than a Swiss Re human baseline; a later independent study found 68% fewer crashes.

The system’s part
The ADS drove all rider-only miles. Claims were adjudicated by Waymo's claims administrator. The anonymized data were reviewed by Swiss Re, which built the human baseline from its own portfolio. The comparison is observational (different drivers, roads and reporting pathways). The rider-only sample in the peer-reviewed paper is 3.9 million miles, and Waymo authors co-wrote both papers.
Limits
Zero observed bodily-injury claims in the initial sample does not imply zero risk. The comparison is observational, uses different reporting pathways, and imperfectly matches road exposure. The larger follow-up and initial peer-reviewed sample must remain separate.
Costs and harms
The safety advantage may depend partly on the service's selected roads, because residential zip codes do not fully match driving exposure. The independent study found a 4% higher crash rate in Austin on a small sample. A linked incident reports that a Waymo vehicle struck a child near an elementary school in Santa Monica, California.
Later
Persisted, 23 July 2026. An independent IIHS study of crash reports from 2021 to 2024 found that Waymo's driverless vehicles were involved in 68% fewer police-reportable crashes per mile than human drivers in the same four cities. The rate was 4% higher in Austin, where the sample was small, and IIHS called for better federal data to monitor a larger rollout.

Brief from Peter

As driverless ride-hailing expands, insurance claims are one way to compare it with human driving. In a peer-reviewed paper of 14 July 2024, Swiss Re and Waymo authors reported zero bodily-injury claims over 3,868,506 driverless miles, against a human baseline of 1.11 per million miles. Waymo's property-damage claims were 0.78 per million miles against 3.26. Waymo authors co-wrote that paper and its 25-million-mile follow-up. The comparison is observational, and the authors note that the baseline cannot exclude freeway miles.1 On 23 July 2026, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS) published an independent study with no Waymo authors. IIHS found that Waymo's driverless vehicles were involved in 68% fewer police-reportable crashes per mile than human drivers in the same areas. It also called for better federal data on crashes and mileage to monitor a larger rollout.2

Details

AI system

Autonomy

Waymo Driver performed the driving throughout the rider-only miles, with no human driver behind the wheel.

Workflow

Geofenced, mostly non-freeway urban service areas in Phoenix and San Francisco. Liability claims adjudicated under state negligence law and reported for any incident regardless of automation level. A human baseline calibrated by zip code of the insured and by mileage exposure. Rider-only operation with no human in the driver's seat.

Human oversight

The Waymo Driver drove all rider-only miles with no human behind the wheel. Waymo's claims administrator adjudicated the claims, and Swiss Re reviewed the anonymized data. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Benefits

Reach

No source counts people who avoided an injury. The results are rates per mile over 3,868,506 rider-only miles in the first study and 25.3 million miles in the follow-up. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Duration

The peer-reviewed claims comparison covered January 2018 to August 2023, and the follow-up ran through July 2024. In a separate peer-reviewed study, Waymo authors compared crash rates over 56.7 million rider-only miles through January 2025. A further study by Waymo authors, published on 28 August 2026, covered 127 million autonomous miles through September 2025. The independent study of 2021 to 2024 also found fewer crashes than human drivers in the same cities. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Measurement

Measurement

Study 1: Liability claims (property damage and bodily injury) per million miles for Waymo, adjudicated by its claims administrator, compared with a Swiss Re baseline of over 600,000 claims calibrated to the zip codes of Waymo's service areas. Study 2: Manual coding of NHTSA Standing General Order crash narratives to decide which crashes a person would report to police, then rates per million miles using Waymo's mileage and Federal Highway Administration mileage for humans. [1] [5]

Setting and sample

Study 1: 3,868,506 rider-only miles in Phoenix and San Francisco from 1 January 2018 to 1 August 2023; human baseline from 2016 to 2021 claims and 125 billion miles. Study 2: 89 police-reportable Waymo crash involvements (64 in driverless operation) over about 50 million driverless miles in San Francisco, Phoenix, Los Angeles and Austin, 2021 to 2024; human drivers over about 222 billion miles in the same places and years. [1] [5]

Measures

Study 1: Claims per million miles. Study 2: Police-reportable crash involvements per million vehicle miles. [1] [5]

Results

Study 1: Bodily injury 0.00 against 1.11 claims per million miles (100% lower); property damage 0.78 against 3.26 (76% lower), with non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals. Study 2: 68% lower overall; 85% fewer single-vehicle crashes and 81% fewer injury crashes; 91% lower rate of rear-ending another vehicle. [1] [5]

Additional value from AI

Study 1: The automated driving system drove every rider-only mile, so the comparison is the system against human drivers in the same zip codes. Study 2: The comparison is the driverless system against human drivers on the same roads; it does not isolate any part of the system. [1] [5]

Limitations

Study 1: Observational; the baseline uses the insured's registered zip code and cannot exclude freeway miles; small number of rider-only miles; Waymo staff co-wrote the paper. Study 2: Police-reportability coded by hand from narratives; 4% higher rate in Austin on a small sample; the authors call the coding method unsustainable as deployments grow. [1] [5]

Denominators

Study 1: Rider-only operation with no human behind the wheel. Study 2: Driverless operation from 2021 to 2024. [1] [5]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Access to an insurer's claims portfolio for the same zip codes and years. Comparable claim-reporting thresholds for ADS and human drivers. Sufficient autonomous mileage for non-overlapping confidence intervals. Operational design domain similar to the baseline's road mix.
