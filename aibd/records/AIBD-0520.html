What happened

In a project account for the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), current as of June 2025, Catalink described a driver-monitoring prototype meant to detect fatigue across diverse users and driving conditions. The team wrote that "Current Driver Monitoring Systems (DMS) struggle to accurately detect drowsiness leading to false negatives and reduced reliability due to biases in the models." Fewer crashes and safer public transport were the intended outcomes. In a July 2025 plan, the European Union ALFIE project, which Catalink coordinates, set out to test the models in Catalink's IRIS phone app. In July 2026 Catalink researchers reported at the ITU Kaleidoscope conference that the drowsiness models they tested generalized poorly across datasets and that the versions converted for phones fell to near chance-level accuracy. The work remained offline research. No source reports a prevented crash or a change for transport operators.

Details

AI system

Model

The research plan uses a vision transformer for drowsiness detection, with later adaptation to individual users.

Role

The prototype is intended to detect driver drowsiness in real time as part of driver monitoring.

Workflow

Conditions that could produce the benefit are unspecified.

Benefits

Duration

No source reports a realized benefit, so no duration can be established. The work remained offline research as of July 2026, when its makers reported poor generalization across datasets. [1] [2] [3]

Measurement

Measurement

Offline accuracy and F1 scores on held-out images, with and without face cropping and contrast equalization, and after conversion to the LiteRT format for phones. [2]

Setting and sample

Eight convolutional and transformer models tested on public drowsiness datasets and a custom dataset of 16,788 images assembled from public sources. [2]

Measures

Accuracy and F1 score. [2]

Results

The authors report that mini-Xception gave the most even scores across datasets, with an F1 score of 0.91 on the custom dataset. They report that the LiteRT phone versions of mini-Xception and YoloClassifier fell to near chance-level accuracy, about 0.50. [2]

Limitations

Tests used single images. The authors state that the custom dataset represents racial and gender groups equally, but they report scores per dataset and give no scores by group. No test took place in a vehicle or with drivers. [2]
