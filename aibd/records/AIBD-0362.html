Rapiscan's AI detects marine wildlife in CT scans at 86–96% in lab tests

In laboratory tests on real and synthetic CT scans, Rapiscan's AI algorithms detected shark fins, seahorses and sea cucumbers at 86–96%, with 13% overall false alarms; no customs use is reported.

What happened

Pirotta and colleagues tested AI detection of marine wildlife in three-dimensional X-ray scans. Their paper of 8 June 2026 states that the scans were collected from September 2024 to February 2025. It reports 298 scans from 68 specimens, including preserved specimens and fresh shark material. The researchers expanded the scans by inserting specimen images into baggage images. A 512-bag test set contained both original scans and these synthetic composites, drawn from the same specimen population. Table 3 reports detection rates of 95% for shark fins, 96% for seahorses, and 86% for sea cucumbers. The overall false-alarm rate was 13%; the class-specific rates were 2%, 9%, and 1%. Rapiscan supplied equipment and funded staff participation in analysis and writing. The authors report laboratory detection only, and the paper reports no operational seizure or customs evaluation. Mongabay reported on 16 June 2026 that outside researchers and a spokesperson for the UN Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) welcomed the study as a complementary tool. Toby Breckon of Durham University told Mongabay that major airports already have 3D scanners for hold luggage. Michelle Anagnostou of Oxford noted that the study left out sea freight. Pirotta told Mongabay that the next step is to share the method so that other species can be detected in other regions.

Details

AI system

Model

Experimental three-dimensional convolutional neural networks.

Release

The experimental convolutional networks have no published release identifier. [1]

Role

The networks identified marine-wildlife specimens in CT images of baggage.

Configuration

Researchers trained the networks on CT scans and synthetic images with inserted marine-wildlife specimens.

Workflow

Rapiscan 3D X-ray CT scanners already used for security screening. Preserved marine specimens and fresh shark material scanned with the Rapiscan RTT110. Synthetic data expansion with 3D Threat Image Projection. Vendor funding of the research.

Benefits

Beneficiaries

Customs and biosecurity screeners are the intended direct users, with potential conservation benefits for trafficked species. The laboratory study measured detection, not animals saved or trafficking reduced.

Reach

No source counts beneficiaries. The study reports 298 scans from 68 specimens and a 512-bag test set. [1] [2]

Duration

Pirotta told Mongabay that the next step is to share the method so that other species can be detected in other regions. No customs trial or deployment of the marine algorithms had been reported by 29 October 2026. [1] [2]

Measurement

Measurement

Laboratory benchmark: a 3D convolutional network trained on CT scans and synthetic composites, scored on a separate test set sampled from the same population as the training data. [1]

Setting and sample

512 test bags scanned on a Rapiscan RTT110 in Homebush, New South Wales: 108 with shark fins, 131 with seahorses, 75 with sea cucumbers and 198 without marine items; most positive bags were synthetic composites. [1]

Measures

Probability of detection and false alarm rate for each class. [1]

Results

Detection of 95% for shark fins, 96% for seahorses and 86% for sea cucumbers, with class false alarm rates of 2%, 9% and 1%. [1]

Limitations

No confidence intervals are reported. The test set was sampled from the same population as the training data, and most positive test bags were synthetic composites. Only dead, mostly dried specimens in small luggage were tested. Five authors are employed by Rapiscan, which funded the study and supplied the scanner and staff for analysis and writing. [1]

Materials for replication

The datasets are not openly available. Requests go to the corresponding author and need approval by Rapiscan and the Australian Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water. [1]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Access to CT scanners at mail and airport screening points. Algorithm performance on concealed, mixed consignments. Customs authority adoption. Legal basis for seizure on algorithm alerts.

Replication attempts

Availability

The algorithms were a laboratory proof of concept. The study did not report operational customs deployment or a seizure prompted by an alert.

Evidence

Mongabay's report of 16 June 2026 relays the study and adds interviews with outside researchers and a statement from UNODC, none of whom retested the detection rates. [1] [2]
