Apple Watch ECG T-wave leads a 76-year-old to quadruple bypass

Koster et al. (EHJ Case Reports, 30 Jan 2024): a patient-initiated lead-I watch ECG showed a negative T-wave normalizing after 22 min; exercise test, CT and angiography found 3-vessel disease; CABG.

ⓘ

What happened

Koster, de Winter, Verberne, Spijkerboer and Chamuleau of Amsterdam UMC reported a case in European Heart Journal Case Reports on 30 January 2024. The manuscript was received 26 June 2023 and the event is undated. The patient was a 76-year-old asymptomatic man with a strong family history of coronary disease. He had 30 years of treated hypercholesterolemia and four years of type 2 diabetes. He exercised weekly on a cross trainer. Very infrequently and “solely out of interest”, he recorded a lead-I electrocardiogram (ECG) with his Apple Watch right afterwards. On the last recording he noticed a negative T-wave that deepened over about seven minutes and normalized after 22 minutes. He had no chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness or palpitations. He consulted a cardiologist, whose resting ECG and examination were normal.

A bicycle exercise test two days later reproduced the lead-I T-wave change with ST depression up to 4 mm in V5. Cardiac computed tomography (CT) showed stenoses in all coronary arteries (CAD-RADS 4). Invasive angiography showed three-vessel disease and a perfusion scan reversible ischemia in 16% of the myocardium. The heart team chose surgery and he received four bypass grafts. Recovery was uneventful and a repeat exercise ECG at 11 weeks was normal. The authors note Apple does not recommend the watch for detecting infarction or ischemia. They note lead I is not ideal for ischemia and the finding requires systematic study before clinical use. The paper reports no output from the ECG app's rhythm classifier. The wearer saw the abnormality on the trace and clinicians confirmed it.

Unexpected use. The ECG app is built to record a single-lead ECG and classify atrial fibrillation. Using the trace to spot exercise-induced ischemia is, as the authors say, not endorsed by Apple.

AI’s contribution

The watch recorded a 30-second lead-I trace at the wearer's initiative. The report gives no classification result from the app. The negative T-wave was noticed by the patient and interpreted by cardiologists, who ordered the exercise test, imaging and surgery. No machine-learning output contributed to the finding as described. The single source is an independent peer-reviewed case report. No maker material was used.

Beneficiaries

  • a person

    Silent, extensive, reversible ischemia from three-vessel disease was found and treated with quadruple bypass in an asymptomatic man. The authors' learning point is that such ischemia can end in exercise-induced sudden death. The counterfactual is not established.

Further evidence

Setting. A wearer who records a watch ECG right after exercise and looks at the waveform, not only the rhythm label. A cardiologist willing to investigate a watch trace in an asymptomatic patient. Exercise testing, CT, angiography and perfusion imaging to confirm and grade the disease.

Conditions. The lead-I trace reproduces T-wave changes faithfully, which the authors say appeared accurate though frequency characteristics are undocumented. Ischemia that shows in lead I, which the authors call not ideal for the purpose. Tolerance of false positives and investigation cascades, which the authors flag as unstudied.
