What happened

On 23 July 2025 an X user posting as Shreya.tsx (@Life_of_coder, profile location “india”) wrote about her mother. Her mother had had a nonstop cough for 1.5 years. That was despite top doctors, big hospitals in and outside their city, homeopathy, ayurveda and allopathy. Internal bleeding had begun. Doctors had warned the condition could be fatal within six months. She described everything to OpenAI's ChatGPT, which listed possible causes including blood pressure medication. It asked whether the prescription contained a specific ingredient. It did. A doctor, told of this, confirmed it at once and changed the medication. Her mother was “finally healing”. Four Indian outlets reported the post on 24 July 2025. It has since been deleted.

Not what it was built to do. ChatGPT is OpenAI's general-purpose conversational assistant, not a diagnostic or drug-safety tool. Surfacing a medication side effect as the likely cause of a chronic cough was outside what it was built for. So the benefit is marked unexpected.

The system's part

ChatGPT generated the hypothesis. Among several possible causes it listed blood pressure medication. When told the mother was on such medication, it asked whether the prescription contained a specific ingredient, which it did. The confirmation and the prescription change were made by a doctor (“He confirmed it instantly and changed the meds”). NDTV, Mint, Mashable India and the Financial Express quote a commenter. They say cough is a known side effect of BP medicine that doctors “should've checked”. That frames the contribution as surfacing a documented side effect rather than a novel diagnosis. No clinician comments on the chatbot's role. The whole sequence rests on the daughter's account.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. A family member with an 18-month symptom history (nonstop cough, then internal bleeding). She described it to ChatGPT after top doctors, big hospitals, homeopathy, ayurveda and allopathy had not helped. ChatGPT listed several possible causes, including blood pressure medication. Told the mother was on BP medication, it asked whether it contained a specific ingredient, which it did. A doctor who, told of the suggestion, confirmed it at once and changed the prescription.

What must hold again. The chatbot's suggestion is taken to a prescribing doctor who confirms it before any medication is changed. The post credits the doctor with both the confirmation and the change. The cough must actually be the drug's side effect. The post names neither the drug nor the “ingredient”, so the mechanism cannot be checked against the drug's label. The account rests on one since-deleted post by the patient's daughter, relayed by independent outlets. No clinician, hospital or record corroborates it.
