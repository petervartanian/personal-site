Observe's AI registers salmon activity rising with gill disease

At two Scottish salmon farms in 2023, Observe Technologies' neural network detected that fish activity rose from 25.6% to 43.6% (Farm A) and 24.9% to 32.6% (Farm B) after gill health deteriorated.

The system’s part
The vendor's neural network turned feed-camera video into an activity measure, and two Stirling observers checked that measure against their own fish counts on 55 video stills (R² of 0.70). Farm staff identified the gill disease by weekly scoring. The study shows that activity rose with the disease and does not show that the signal came first or changed a decision.
Limits
The activity change followed compromised gill health and was correlated with disease. The study does not show earlier warning, changed treatment, reduced mortality or an improvement in welfare. Three of the six authors work at Observe Technologies, so the paper is the developer's own evidence.

What happened

Researchers at the University of Stirling's Institute of Aquaculture applied Observe's algorithm to feed-camera video at two commercial Atlantic salmon marine farms in Scotland. Three of the six authors work at Observe Technologies, which is based in Richmond, United Kingdom, so the paper is the developer's own evidence. Innovate UK funded the study in partnership with Observe and AKVA Group. The algorithm, a patented convolutional neural network (EP3644717), ran on video in every cage, with 5 and 4 extra cameras in one cage per farm. It outputs “activity” (%), which combines fish abundance, speed and shoal cohesion. Two Stirling observers found that the algorithm's activity output tracked their own fish counts on 55 video stills (R² of 0.70). Gill health, operational welfare indicators, mortality and specific feeding rate were scored weekly. Data were collected from 10 May to 10 September 2023 at Farm A and from 22 June to 13 October 2023 at Farm B. Gill health problems arose at both farms in summer 2023. Mean activity in the two months before was 25.6 ± 10.5% (A) and 24.9 ± 7.0% (B). It rose for two months after to 43.6 ± 15.1% and 32.6 ± 9.6%, with fish moving to the cage center. In a mixed model, the authors found that proliferative gill disease was the main driver. In the paper, published on 15 August 2025, they propose behavior as a non-invasive welfare indicator. They report no alert that preceded diagnosis and no change in treatment.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

The CNN converted feed-camera video into an activity percentage based on fish abundance, swimming speed, and shoal cohesion.

Workflow

Feed cameras in all cages plus 4–5 extra cameras in one cage per farm. Weekly gill scoring, welfare indicators, mortality and feeding-rate records. A summer 2023 gill-health episode at both farms.

Human oversight

Researchers compared the video-derived activity with weekly gill-health, welfare, mortality, and feeding records.

Benefits

Duration

Farm A was observed from 10 May to 10 September 2023, and Farm B from 22 June to 13 October 2023.

Measurement

Measurement

Algorithm activity output from feed cameras compared with weekly gill scores (amoebic and proliferative gill disease), specific feeding rate and mortality, with a mixed model for drivers of activity.

Setting and sample

Two commercial Atlantic salmon marine farms in the Western Isles of Scotland, 13 and 10 cages, May to October 2023.

Measures

Mean fish activity (%) in the two months before and after the activity rise.

Results

Activity rose from 25.6% to 43.6% at Farm A and from 24.9% to 32.6% at Farm B; proliferative gill disease was the main driver in the mixed model.

Additional value from AI

Not measured; no comparison with detection by farm staff alone.

Limitations

Three authors work for the vendor. The algorithm's speed and cohesion components were not validated separately, and other causes of the behavior change could not be ruled out.

Denominators

Algorithm applied to all cages; four or five extra cameras in one cage per farm.

Materials for replication

Raw data in the DataSTORRE repository, http://hdl.handle.net/11667/241.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Gill disease severe enough to alter shoaling and distribution. Camera coverage of the cage volume.
