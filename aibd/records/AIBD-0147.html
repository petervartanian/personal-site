Taichung EPB's AI smoke detection catches a serial waste burner

depth 2 for society, depth 1 for an organization

a fine of up to NT$100,000

Taichung EPB AI smoke detection flagged roadside waste burning in Dadu District; a 74-year-old repeat offender was caught and fined.

What happened

Taichung's Environmental Protection Bureau (EPB) installed its AI smoke-recognition system, air micro-sensors and cameras beside Hugan Road, Dadu District, after a March 2025 complaint about smoke from a dumping spot. CNA reports that shortly after 08:00 on 4 July 2025 the instruments flagged abnormal air quality; inspectors found dumped waste burning, and the system recorded the same man in July and August. His motorcycle plate was too faded to read, so inspectors collected neighbors' CCTV and dashcam footage, and a Wuri Precinct officer recognized a 74-year-old man surnamed Zhao, who confessed. On 13 September 2025 the EPB announced a fine of up to NT$100,000 under the Air Pollution Control Act and asked the land manager to clear the site.

What it was built to do. The EPB introduced AI smoke-recognition cameras in 2022 to recognize abnormal smoke around the clock at open-burning hotspots and send automatic alerts so inspectors can locate and stop pollution sources and reduce fire risk; detecting and recording this roadside burning was exactly that intended use.

Who benefited

  • 2
    societal

    Residents near Hugan Road in Dadu District: the EPB says the case removed 'a potential pollution source threatening air quality' for them, and CTS and PTS describe repeated burns at the same spot in February, July and August 2025 next to weeds; no exposure figures or air-quality measurements are reported, so the benefit is material but local and unquantified.

  • 1
    organizational

    The EPB and Wuri Precinct police closed a repeat-offender case that the faded plate had stalled: the system's alert and video supplied the inspection trigger and the core evidence, and the EPB presents the case as proof that its 'smart technology city' enforcement works.

The system's part

The AI smoke-recognition system and micro-sensors supplied the detection (first dated to 4 July 2025 by CNA, then repeatedly in July and August) that sent inspectors to the burning site and the video that recorded the offender; it could not read his faded motorcycle plate, so identification came from private CCTV, dashcam footage and a police officer's recognition of a local resident. The system's contribution is detection and documentation; removal of the source depended on human investigation, and no measured air-quality change or proven counterfactual is reported, which is why the record sits at protective response with prevention unproven rather than specific harm prevented.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. Fixed AI smoke-recognition cameras and air micro-sensors installed at a known open-burning hotspot after a public complaint, with automatic alerts to inspectors. Inspectors able to reach the site while the fire was burning and to assemble third-party CCTV and dashcam footage when the system's video could not read the faded plate. Local police (Wuri Precinct Dadu Station) familiar enough with residents to match the recorded build, clothing and gait to a known 74-year-old resident. A legal basis for penalties (Air Pollution Control Act Article 32) and a land manager that could be asked to clear the dump site.

What must hold again. The AI alert must reach staff who can respond while the fire is burning and who have the mandate to enter the site. Identification must not rest on the camera alone: plate recognition failed here and human evidence-gathering closed the case. The offender must be identifiable and reachable; a stranger with no local ties would likely not have been recognized.
