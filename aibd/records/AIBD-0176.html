Some subprime borrowers get lower-priced credit at LendingClub

LendingClub's rating grades, which relied more and more on alternative data, moved some borrowers whom traditional criteria call subprime into better grades with lower-priced credit.

  • 1 a person

    Borrowers with subprime FICO scores who received better grades paid less for credit, and LendingClub borrowers paid smaller spreads than credit card borrowers with the same default risk. The sources report a cost saving, measured across loan vintages.

  • Not rated society

    Financial-inclusion effects are discussed at the aggregate level only.

Not isolated. The paper attributes the regrading of subprime borrowers to alternative data used by the lender. It does not establish that machine learning produced the grades. LendingClub developed and deployed its own model. The Federal Reserve authors are independent.

The loan comparison does not isolate machine learning from alternative data, pricing, selection into the platform, or other lender differences. Lower interest spreads are not a complete measure of borrower welfare, and the paper’s observational results cannot establish fairness or benefits for all subprime borrowers.

What happened

Julapa Jagtiani of the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia and Catharine Lemieux of the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago wrote Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia working paper 18–15, revised January 2019. Its title is “The Roles of Alternative Data and Machine Learning in Fintech Lending: Evidence from the LendingClub Consumer Platform”. It compares account-level LendingClub loans with similar loans in Y-14M reports from bank holding companies with assets of $50 billion or more. The authors report that the correlation between LendingClub's rating grades and FICO scores declined. It was about 80 percent for 2007 originations and about 35 percent for 2014–2015 vintages. They report that the grades perform well in predicting loan performance over the two years after origination. Alternative data allowed some borrowers who would have been classified as subprime by traditional criteria to be slotted into better grades. They got lower-priced credit. For the same default risk consumers pay smaller spreads at LendingClub than on credit cards. For 2015 loans about 8 percent of A-rated and 28 percent of B-rated borrowers had FICO scores below 680. The working paper itself gives only a month for its revision. The study passed peer review and appeared in Financial Management on 6 November 2019 (DOI 10.1111/fima.12295). In a working paper dated 26 October 2023, Jagtiani, Lemieux and Brandon Goldstein reported that LendingClub's grades kept predicting default through the COVID-19 pandemic. They add that the grades predicted default less well during the pandemic than before it. They also found that nonprime LendingClub borrowers defaulted much less often than nonprime credit card borrowers. They report that LendingClub kept lending to nonprime borrowers during its tightening of 2019.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

LendingClub's grading system used information beyond FICO to assign loan grades that helped determine borrowing costs.

Workflow

Account-level LendingClub data compared with Y-14M bank data for similar loans, 2007 to 2015 vintages. Lender grading that increasingly used information not captured by FICO.

Benefits

Beneficiaries

Borrowers whom FICO classified below prime but whom LendingClub placed in better grades received the clearest reported pricing benefit.

Duration

In a working paper dated 26 October 2023, the same authors with Brandon Goldstein reported that LendingClub's grades kept predicting default through the COVID-19 pandemic, though less well than before it. The sources give no later measure of the prices that borrowers paid. [1] [2] [3]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Alternative data that predicts repayment beyond FICO. Grades that keep predicting loan performance (the paper reports good prediction over two years after origination).
