Corti model beats dispatchers on sensitivity; RCT shows no gain

higher sensitivity than dispatchers (84.1% versus 72.5%)

Copenhagen EMS call model beat dispatchers on sensitivity (84.1% vs 72.5%); RCT found no significant gain in dispatcher recognition.

What happened

Copenhagen Emergency Medical Services used a machine-learning framework, identified by the European Emergency Number Association as Corti's, to listen to 112 calls and flag suspected out-of-hospital cardiac arrest for dispatchers. A retrospective study of 108,607 calls from 2014 found the model recognised cardiac arrest with higher sensitivity than dispatchers (84.1% versus 72.5%) and faster (median 44 versus 54 seconds). A double-masked randomised trial of 169,049 calls from 1 September 2018 to 31 December 2019 then found dispatcher recognition of confirmed arrests of 93.1% with alerts versus 90.5% without, a non-significant difference, although alerts alone were more sensitive than dispatchers (85.0% versus 77.5%). No individual patient outcome attributable to an alert is documented; the date used is the trial start.

What it was built to do. The model was built to recognise out-of-hospital cardiac arrest during emergency calls so dispatchers can start telephone CPR sooner; the measured capability is that intended function, but no discrete changed outcome is documented.

The system's part

The AI listened to live calls and alerted dispatchers; in the randomised trial its alerts were more sensitive than dispatchers alone (85.0% vs 77.5%) but did not significantly change dispatcher recognition (93.1% vs 90.5%, P = .15). The benefit is therefore a demonstrated detection capability, not a verified change in any patient's course.

Replication

What produced it

  • Access to recorded 112 call audio with linked outcome data (7,211 hours of Danish training data per the EENA report)
  • Speech-recognition pipeline running in real time on live calls
  • A dispatch centre whose baseline recognition was already high (90.5% in the control arm)

What must hold again

  • Dispatchers act on alerts; the trial suggests alerts did not change their behaviour enough to shift recognition
  • Call language and acoustics match the training data (EENA reports weaker performance on smaller French and Italian datasets)
