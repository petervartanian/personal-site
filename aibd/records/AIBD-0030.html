Software detects more cardiac arrests than Copenhagen dispatchers do

Corti's machine-learning framework detected more cardiac arrests on Copenhagen emergency calls than dispatchers did, but its alerts did not significantly improve dispatcher recognition in a trial.

Costs and harms

The model detected more cardiac arrests but also generated more false alarms: its specificity was 97.4%, compared with 99.6% for dispatchers without alerts. Giving dispatchers alerts did not significantly improve their recognition rate.

Relation to AI

The AI listened to live calls and alerted dispatchers. In the randomized trial its alerts were more sensitive than dispatchers alone (85.0% vs 77.5%). They did not significantly change dispatcher recognition (93.1% vs 90.5%, P = .15). The benefit is thus a demonstrated detection capability, not a verified change in any patient's course.

Citable summary

Copenhagen EMS call model beat dispatchers on sensitivity (84.1% vs 72.5%); RCT found no significant gain in dispatcher recognition. Higher model sensitivity should not be substituted for an improvement in the deployed workflow. The retained randomized comparison was not statistically conclusive for dispatcher recognition, and the retrospective comparison also reports lower specificity.

Sources

  1. Peer-reviewed study Deployer

    Blomberg SN, Christensen HC, Lippert F, et al. (2021). Effect of Machine Learning on Dispatcher Recognition of Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest During Calls to Emergency Medical Services: A Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA Network Open 4(1):e2032320. Also published at https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC7788469.

    Of 169,049 calls examined, 5,242 flagged calls were randomized. Alerts did not significantly change dispatcher recognition; sensitivity improved with lower specificity and positive predictive value. Abstract and Tables 1–2

    Original

    Machine learning alerts alone had a significantly higher sensitivity than dispatchers without alerts for confirmed OHCA (85.0% vs 77.5%; P < .001)

  2. Peer-reviewed study Deployer

    Blomberg SN, Folke F, Ersboll AK, Christensen HC, Torp-Pedersen C, Sayre MR, Counts CR, Lippert FK (2019). Machine learning as a supportive tool to recognize cardiac arrest in emergency calls. Resuscitation 138:322-329. doi:10.1016/j.resuscitation.2019.01.015.

    Compared with medical dispatchers, the machine learning framework had a significantly higher sensitivity (72.5% vs. 84.1%, p < 0.001) with lower specificity (98.8% vs. 97.3%, p < 0.001).

  3. Report Developer

    This document is authored by EENA and Corti staff members with contributions from individual members of EENA and Corti.

  5. Peer-reviewed study Independent author Context or contrary evidence

    Hildt E, What Is the Role of Explainability in Medical Artificial Intelligence? A Case-Based Approach, Bioengineering (Basel) 12(4):375, 2 April 2025. The author is an ethicist at the Illinois Institute of Technology with no stake in the system; she co-wrote a 2021 trustworthiness assessment of it with Copenhagen EMS staff, and here she argues that the dispatchers tended to ignore the alerts.

  6. Peer-reviewed study Deployer

    Blomberg SN, Jensen TW, Porsborg Andersen M, et al., When the machine is wrong. Characteristics of true and false predictions of Out-of-Hospital Cardiac arrests in emergency calls using a machine-learning model, Resuscitation 183:109689, 9 January 2023. Most authors work for Copenhagen EMS, the deployer; the paper reports the model's accuracy on all calls in the trial period and the reasons for its misses and false alarms.

    The machine-learning model processed 169,236 calls to Copenhagen EMS and suspected 5,811 (3.4%) of the calls as OHCA, resulting in 84.5% sensitivity and 97.1% specificity.
