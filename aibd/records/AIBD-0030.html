Software detects more cardiac arrests than Copenhagen dispatchers do

Corti's machine-learning framework detected more cardiac arrests on Copenhagen emergency calls than dispatchers did, but its alerts did not significantly improve dispatcher recognition in a trial.

  • Not rated a person

    The sources report aggregate sensitivity, time to recognition and trial-level recognition rates, and none attributes a changed outcome to an individual patient. The randomized trial found no significant improvement in dispatcher recognition.

  • Not rated an organization

    The sources report a capability study and a randomized trial that found no significant effect. No source documents an organizational event with an observed outcome.

The system’s part
The AI listened to live calls and alerted dispatchers. In the randomized trial its alerts were more sensitive than dispatchers alone (85.0% vs 77.5%). They did not significantly change dispatcher recognition (93.1% vs 90.5%, P = .15). The benefit is thus a demonstrated detection capability, not a verified change in any patient's course.
Limits
Higher model sensitivity does not show an improvement in the dispatch workflow, because the trial found no significant gain in dispatcher recognition. A 2026 review rated the trial as having some concerns for risk of bias. No source reports an outcome for an individual patient.
Costs and harms
The model detected more cardiac arrests but also generated more false alarms: its specificity was 97.4%, compared with 99.6% for dispatchers without alerts. Giving dispatchers alerts did not significantly improve their recognition rate.
Later
Persisted, 4 January 2021. The randomized trial of 169,049 calls confirmed the model's sensitivity advantage over dispatchers (85.0% vs 77.5%) but found no significant improvement in dispatcher recognition when alerts were shown (93.1% vs 90.5%).

What happened

Copenhagen Emergency Medical Services used a machine-learning framework to listen to calls to the 112 emergency number and flag suspected out-of-hospital cardiac arrest for dispatchers. In a retrospective study of 108,607 calls from 2014, the authors found that the model recognized cardiac arrest with higher sensitivity than dispatchers (84.1% versus 72.5%). Its specificity was lower (97.3% versus 98.8%). It was also faster (median 44 versus 54 seconds). A double-masked randomized trial of 169,049 calls ran from 1 September 2018 to 31 December 2019. The trial authors found that dispatchers recognized 93.1% of confirmed arrests with alerts and 90.5% without, a difference that was not significant (P = .15). Alerts alone were more sensitive than dispatchers (85.0% versus 77.5%). The alerts had lower specificity (97.4% versus 99.6%) and a lower positive predictive value (17.8% versus 55.8%). In a project report published on 13 January 2020, staff of the European Emergency Number Association and Corti name the framework as Corti's. In a 2023 paper, authors from Copenhagen Emergency Medical Services report that the model processed 169,236 calls during the trial period with 84.5% sensitivity and 97.1% specificity. They report that many of its misses involved reports of normal breathing or language barriers. The ethicist Elisabeth Hildt argued in 2025 that the dispatchers tended to ignore the alerts. In a scoping review of 24 September 2026, Yonsei University researchers rated the trial as having some concerns for risk of bias. No source documents an outcome for an individual patient that is attributable to an alert.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

The system analyzed the audio of emergency calls in real time and alerted dispatchers when it detected signs of cardiac arrest.

Workflow

Access to recorded 112 call audio with linked outcome data (7,211 hours of Danish training data per the EENA report). Speech-recognition pipeline running in real time on live calls. A dispatch center whose baseline recognition was already high (90.5% in the control arm).

Benefits

Duration

The model ran on live calls throughout the trial from 1 September 2018 to 31 December 2019, and a 2023 paper by the deployer reports its accuracy over that period. No source reports use or outcomes after the trial. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Dispatchers act on alerts. The trial suggests alerts did not change their behavior enough to shift recognition. Call language and acoustics match the training data (EENA reports weaker performance on smaller French and Italian datasets).

Replication attempts

2 follow-up entries. Benefit recurred: 2.

Purpose

The model was built to recognize out-of-hospital cardiac arrest during emergency calls so that dispatchers can start telephone-guided cardiopulmonary resuscitation sooner. The studies measured that intended function. No source documents a discrete changed outcome.
