What happened

Copenhagen Emergency Medical Services used a machine-learning framework to listen to 112 calls and flag suspected out-of-hospital cardiac arrest for dispatchers. The European Emergency Number Association names it as Corti's. A retrospective study of 108,607 calls from 2014 found the model recognized cardiac arrest with higher sensitivity than dispatchers (84.1% versus 72.5%). It was also faster (median 44 versus 54 seconds). A double-masked randomized trial of 169,049 calls ran from 1 September 2018 to 31 December 2019. It found dispatcher recognition of confirmed arrests of 93.1% with alerts versus 90.5% without, a non-significant difference. Alerts alone were more sensitive than dispatchers (85.0% versus 77.5%). No individual patient outcome attributable to an alert is documented. The date used is the trial start.

What it was built to do. The model was built to recognize out-of-hospital cardiac arrest during emergency calls so dispatchers can start telephone CPR sooner. The measured capability is that intended function, but no discrete changed outcome is documented.

Since then
Persisted on 4 January 2021: The randomized trial of 169,049 calls confirmed the model's sensitivity advantage over dispatchers (85.0% vs 77.5%) but found no significant improvement in dispatcher recognition when alerts were shown (93.1% vs 90.5%).Machine learning alerts alone had a significantly higher sensitivity than dispatchers without alerts for confirmed OHCA (85.0% vs 77.5%; P < .001) but lower specificity (97.4% vs 99.6%; P < .001) and positive predictive value (17.8% vs 55.8%; P < .001). This randomized clinical trial did not find any significant improvement in dispatchers' ability to recognize cardiac arrest when supported by machine learning even though artificial intelligence did surpass human recognition. source

The system's part

The AI listened to live calls and alerted dispatchers. In the randomized trial its alerts were more sensitive than dispatchers alone (85.0% vs 77.5%). They did not significantly change dispatcher recognition (93.1% vs 90.5%, P = .15). The benefit is thus a demonstrated detection capability, not a verified change in any patient's course.

Replication

What produced it. Access to recorded 112 call audio with linked outcome data (7,211 hours of Danish training data per the EENA report). Speech-recognition pipeline running in real time on live calls. A dispatch center whose baseline recognition was already high (90.5% in the control arm).

What must hold again. Dispatchers act on alerts. The trial suggests alerts did not change their behavior enough to shift recognition. Call language and acoustics match the training data (EENA reports weaker performance on smaller French and Italian datasets).
