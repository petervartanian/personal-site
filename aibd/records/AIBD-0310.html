What happened

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) announced REACH VET (Recovery Engagement and Coordination for Health - Veterans Enhanced Treatment) on 3 April 2017. It had begun as a pilot the previous October and was fully implemented across VA. It uses a predictive model on veterans' health records to identify those at statistically elevated risk of suicide and other adverse outcomes. Once a veteran is identified, a VA mental health or primary care provider reaches out. The provider reviews the condition and treatment plan and decides if enhanced care is needed. Nextgov/FCW (17 July 2025) says the model scans records monthly for the top 0.1% risk tier. It says the variable set was cut from 381 to 61 and about 30,000 veterans were identified in the first year. Nextgov (18 October 2024) gives about 6,700 veterans a month.

McCarthy and colleagues published the evaluation in JAMA Network Open on 18 October 2021. They are at the VA Office of Mental Health and Suicide Prevention, which built and runs the program. It covered 173,313 individuals, 40,816 of them in the top 0.1% tier during REACH VET (March 2017 to December 2018). The pre-period (March 2014 to December 2015) had 36,604. Triple-difference analyses found REACH VET associated with more completed outpatient appointments (adjusted triple difference 0.31; 95% CI 0.06 to 0.55). The proportion with new safety plans rose (0.08; 0.06 to 0.10). Mental health admissions (-0.08; -0.10 to -0.05), emergency department (ED) visits (-0.03; -0.06 to -0.01) and documented suicide attempts (-0.05; -0.06 to -0.03) fell. Subcohort analyses found no difference in suicide or all-cause mortality.

Care evaluations of identified veterans rose from 46.1% to 87.5% over the period. A 2022 Psychiatric Services paper reports that by October 2020, 98% of identified veterans (N=6,579) were contacted and had care evaluated. Dent, Cooper and McCarthy (JAMA Network Open, 8 July 2025; 266,246 observations) again found no association with suicide, external-cause or all-cause mortality. The 6-month suicide hazard ratio was 1.18 (95% CI 0.83–1.69). Nextgov (2024) reports research finding the model “considered being a white man more of an indicator of potential self-harm than other factors”. The model omitted military sexual trauma; a REACH VET 2.0 adding such factors was planned for early 2025. The program's benefit is a cohort-level association over 2017–2018, not a dated event.

What it was built to do. Built to identify veterans in the top 0.1% of predicted suicide risk each month so that clinicians re-evaluate and enhance their care. The measured associations are that purpose.

The system's part

The predictive model selects the monthly top 0.1% risk tier. Coordinators and providers then re-evaluate care and reach out, so the clinical response, not the model alone, produces any change. The evaluation is observational, uses a triple-difference design to handle non-parallel trends, and is authored by the VA office that built and runs REACH VET. It found no mortality association, replicated in 2025 by the same group. Independent reporting documents that the original model weighted being a white man heavily and omitted military sexual trauma.

Replication

What produced it. A predictive model run monthly on VHA electronic health records to rank patients and select the top 0.1% risk tier. Facility REACH VET coordinators and providers who acknowledge the list, evaluate care, document safety plans and attempt outreach. A national health system with the data, dashboards and metrics to implement the program at every facility.

What must hold again. Providers act on the list: care evaluations rose from 46.1% to 87.5% of identified veterans between March 2017 and December 2018. The model's variables fit the population; the original model under-weighted factors relevant to women veterans, per Nextgov. Outcome ascertainment is comparable across periods; the authors note that closer attention may increase recorded attempts.
