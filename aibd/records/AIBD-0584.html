Photos labeled by SpeciesNet give wildlife estimates close to experts'

Models built from MegaDetector and SpeciesNet labels came close to expert-based models, with mean occupancy differences of 0.03-0.07 across study areas; some species were missed or misclassified.

  • Not rated an organization

    The study compares AI and expert labels on images that experts had already classified. It reports no labor saving, no adoption by a monitoring program and no changed research decision.

  • Not rated society

    No changed conservation decision or wildlife outcome is documented.

What happened

Thornton, Morris, and colleagues compared ecological occupancy models derived from automated SpeciesNet identifications with models derived from expert identifications. Dan Morris, a developer at Google, coauthored the study. The study used 638 cameras in Washington, 263 in Montana, and 326 in Guatemala. Montana's expert workflow already used MegaDetector for initial sorting. MegaDetector 5a located animals, and SpeciesNet classified the crops, with regional species lists, taxonomic adjustments, and sequence processing. Across six AI configurations, mean absolute differences in occupancy probabilities were 0.04-0.05 in Washington, 0.05-0.07 in Montana, and 0.03-0.06 in Guatemala. These comparisons cover species present in both datasets. They do not measure the accuracy of labels on individual images. Some individual species differed substantially, with a maximum difference of 0.40 in Montana. All 94 mountain-goat detections in Montana were classified as domestic goats and omitted from the AI occupancy models. The authors report broadly similar results for shared species. The authors recommend human review for some research tasks. The study does not measure net labor savings, changed management decisions or wildlife outcomes. The author summary was first dated 17 March 2026, and the full paper appeared on 6 May 2026. On 10 October 2026 Michael Tabak, an ecologist who is not affiliated with Google or the study team, posted a preprint that tested a SpeciesNet-based workflow on 63 cameras in Malawi. People reviewed 5 percent of the images, and a retrained project model labeled the rest. Occupancy estimates differed from a full human review by 0.11 on average. No cited source reports that a monitoring program adopted the fully automated workflow by October 2026.

Details

AI system

Model

MegaDetector and SpeciesNet. [1]

Release

MegaDetector 5a; SpeciesNet checkpoint not reported [1]

Role

MegaDetector located animals. SpeciesNet classified image crops. Statistical occupancy models estimated ecological relationships from the resulting labels. [1]

Configuration

MegaDetector 5a detections at confidence 0.15 or above were passed to SpeciesNet. Researchers applied regional species lists, taxonomic adjustments, sequence smoothing, and six combinations of classification thresholds and sequence cleaning. The SpeciesNet checkpoint is unspecified. [1]

Inputs and outputs

Camera photographs became animal crops and species identifications, which researchers used to estimate occupancy and relationships with environmental variables. [1]

Autonomy

The evaluated AI workflow did not require routine human review of each image. Researchers designed the processing and ecological analysis. No automated conservation action is documented. [1]

Workflow

Researchers processed images with MegaDetector and SpeciesNet, prepared six AI datasets, fitted occupancy models, and compared those models with models based on expert identifications. [1]

Human oversight

Researchers supplied regional filters and taxonomic adjustments and assessed the statistical output. Expert identifications provided the comparator. The authors recommend human review for some rare-species and biodiversity tasks. [1]

Relation to AI

The study evaluates AI-generated species labels as inputs to ecological inference. Montana's expert comparison already included MegaDetector sorting. [1]

Benefits

Beneficiaries

The participating ecological research teams. Benefits to monitoring organizations and conservation managers are prospective unless subsequent use is documented. [1]

Distribution and access

Agreement varied by region and species. Shared-species comparisons do not cover species missing from the AI datasets. Rare or difficult species may require human review. [1]

Duration

The study does not establish how long the benefit persisted after the evaluation. No cited source reports that a monitoring program adopted the fully automated workflow by October 2026. [1] [2] [3]

Expected?

The result concerns the intended use of AI to support wildlife-image analysis. [1]

Measurement

Measurement

The authors compared occupancy probabilities, detection probabilities, species-environment relationships, precision, and spatial projections from fitted models. [1]

Setting and sample

Washington: 638 cameras and about 1.8 million images. Montana: 263 cameras and about 1.7 million images. Guatemala: 326 cameras and about 280,000 images. [1]

Measures

Mean absolute occupancy-probability differences were 0.04-0.05 in Washington, 0.05-0.07 in Montana, and 0.03-0.06 in Guatemala for species shared by the AI and expert datasets. [1]

Results

Mean occupancy estimates were broadly similar for shared species. Individual differences reached 0.40 in Montana, and some species were omitted or added by classification errors. [1]

Additional value from AI

The comparison demonstrates an automated route to useful ecological estimates for many shared species. It does not quantify net time saved or improved conservation decisions relative to the existing workflow. [1]

Limitations

Comparisons of model estimates cover shared species. Rare-species omissions remain outside those comparisons. Regional filtering and sequence processing affect results. Montana's expert workflow already used AI. [1]

Denominators

The study includes 638 Washington cameras, 263 Montana cameras, and 326 Guatemala cameras. These are sampling units, not beneficiary counts or a denominator for conservation success. [1]

Materials for replication

Zenodo version 1.0.1 contains Washington CSV inputs and scripts for all regions. Referenced Montana and Guatemala inputs are absent. The public Washington image subset has a different denominator. The complete supplement was inaccessible. [1] [2]

People and places

Dependencies

Camera datasets, MegaDetector, SpeciesNet, regional species information, and statistical analysis in R were required. A complete historical SpeciesNet checkpoint and all regional data inputs are not preserved in the inspected archive. [1] [2]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The system must retain the species that matter to the intended ecological question. The exact SpeciesNet checkpoint and processing settings must be recoverable. Regional input data and analysis scripts must be available, with appropriate protections for sensitive wildlife locations. [1]

Availability

The full article and versioned code-and-data archive are public. The inspected archive does not contain all regional inputs, and the complete supplement was inaccessible. [1] [2]
