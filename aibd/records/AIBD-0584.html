SpeciesNet supports ecological estimates with some species missed

Researchers used MegaDetector and SpeciesNet to produce ecological estimates similar to expert analysis for many species, while some species were missed or misclassified.

Benefits

Benefit

Thornton, Morris, and colleagues compared ecological occupancy models derived from automated SpeciesNet identifications with models derived from expert identifications. The study used 638 cameras in Washington, 263 in Montana, and 326 in Guatemala. MegaDetector 5a located animals, and SpeciesNet classified the crops, with regional species lists, taxonomic adjustments, and sequence processing. Across six AI configurations, mean absolute differences in occupancy probabilities were 0.04-0.05 in Washington, 0.05-0.07 in Montana, and 0.03-0.06 in Guatemala. These comparisons cover species present in both datasets; they are not image-accuracy percentages. Some individual species differed substantially, with a maximum difference of 0.40 in Montana. All 94 mountain-goat detections in Montana were classified as domestic goats and omitted from the AI occupancy models. Montana's expert workflow already used MegaDetector for initial sorting. The study demonstrates automated ecological analysis with broadly similar results for shared species, while preserving a need for human review in some research tasks. It does not measure net labor savings, changed management decisions, or wildlife outcomes. Google developer Dan Morris coauthored the study. The author summary was first dated March 17, 2026; the full paper appeared on May 6. [1]

Google and MegaDetector contributors [1]

Role

MegaDetector located animals. SpeciesNet classified image crops. Statistical occupancy models estimated ecological relationships from the resulting labels. [1]

Configuration

MegaDetector 5a detections at confidence 0.15 or above were passed to SpeciesNet. Researchers applied regional species lists, taxonomic adjustments, sequence smoothing, and six combinations of classification thresholds and sequence cleaning. The SpeciesNet checkpoint is unspecified. [1]

Inputs and outputs

Camera photographs became animal crops and species identifications, which researchers used to estimate occupancy and relationships with environmental variables. [1]

Autonomy

The evaluated AI workflow did not require routine human review of each image. Researchers designed the processing and ecological analysis. No automated conservation action is documented. [1]

Workflow

Researchers processed images with MegaDetector and SpeciesNet, prepared six AI datasets, fitted occupancy models, and compared those models with models based on expert identifications. [1]

Human oversight

Researchers supplied regional filters and taxonomic adjustments and assessed the statistical output. Expert identifications provided the comparator. The authors recommend human review for some rare-species and biodiversity tasks. [1]

Relation to AI

The study evaluates AI-generated species labels as inputs to ecological inference. Montana's expert comparison already included MegaDetector sorting. [1]

AI contribution

MegaDetector located animals and SpeciesNet supplied identifications for ecological models. Researchers configured and analyzed the workflow. The comparison tests analytical usefulness, not a measured improvement in conservation outcomes or net labor time. [1]

Measurement

Studies

AIBD-0584-evaluation-thornton-2026 [1]

Study design

Comparative ecological analysis of the same regional image datasets using expert labels and six AI processing configurations. The comparator is not uniformly free of AI. [1]

Measurement

The authors compared occupancy probabilities, detection probabilities, species-environment relationships, precision, and spatial projections from fitted models. [1]

Setting and sample

Washington: 638 cameras and about 1.8 million images. Montana: 263 cameras and about 1.7 million images. Guatemala: 326 cameras and about 280,000 images. [1]

Measures

Mean absolute occupancy-probability differences were 0.04-0.05 in Washington, 0.05-0.07 in Montana, and 0.03-0.06 in Guatemala for species shared by the AI and expert datasets. [1]

Comparison

Ecological models fitted to expert identifications of the same images; Montana experts already used MegaDetector for initial sorting. [1]

Results

Mean occupancy estimates were broadly similar for shared species. Individual differences reached 0.40 in Montana, and some species were omitted or added by classification errors. [1]

Additional value from AI

The comparison demonstrates an automated route to useful ecological estimates for many shared species. It does not quantify net time saved or improved conservation decisions relative to the existing workflow. [1]

Limitations

Comparisons of model estimates cover shared species. Rare-species omissions remain outside those comparisons. Regional filtering and sequence processing affect results. Montana's expert workflow already used AI. The exact SpeciesNet checkpoint, some regional inputs, and the complete supplement were unavailable in this review. [1]

Denominators

The study includes 638 Washington cameras, 263 Montana cameras, and 326 Guatemala cameras. These are sampling units, not beneficiary counts or a denominator for conservation success. [1]

Materials for replication

Zenodo version 1.0.1 contains Washington CSV inputs and scripts for all regions. Referenced Montana and Guatemala inputs are absent. The public Washington image subset has a different denominator. The complete supplement was inaccessible. [1] [2]
