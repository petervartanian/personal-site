A learning controller makes a prosthetic arm easier to use at home

In a randomized crossover home trial, eight people with above-elbow amputation and nerve transfer surgery did better on two hand-function tests with pattern-recognition control; seven preferred it.

What happened

Levi Hargrove, Laura Miller, Kristi Turner and Todd Kuiken of the Center for Bionic Medicine at the Shirley Ryan AbilityLab in Chicago ran a randomized crossover trial (NCT03097978) with eight people who had an amputation above the elbow and targeted muscle reinnervation (TMR), a surgery that moves arm nerves onto remaining muscles. Each person used a custom prosthesis built from commercial parts at home for at least six weeks with direct control and for the same time with pattern recognition control, which uses machine learning algorithms to learn patterns in the muscle signals. A random order decided which method came first. With pattern recognition, participants scored better on the Southampton Hand Assessment Procedure (p = 0.04) and moved three clothespins in 90.2 seconds on average against 137 seconds with direct control (p = 0.02). The Box and Blocks Test and the ACMC showed no significant differences. Seven of the eight participants preferred pattern recognition. In a 2021 review, F. Mereu and colleagues at Università Campus Bio-Medico di Roma and the INAIL Prosthetic Center, who declare no conflicts of interest, report the same results. Scientific Reports published the trial on 23 October 2017. The sample is small, participants noted some unintended movements, and Hargrove and Kuiken have a financial interest in Coapt, which sells pattern recognition systems, although the study used no Coapt products.

    At the end of the trial, subjects moved three clothespins from a horizontal bar to a vertical bar in 90.2 ± 39.6 seconds using pattern recognition compared to 137 ± 60.2 seconds using direct control.

Details

AI system

Workflow

Participants had TMR surgery, which created extra muscle sites for recording signals. Each participant used the prosthesis at home for six to eight weeks under each control method.

Measurement

Measurement

SHAP, Clothespin Relocation Task, Box and Blocks Test and ACMC before and after each home period; home usage logs; stated preference. [2]

Setting and sample

Eight transhumeral amputees with TMR, Center for Bionic Medicine, Chicago. [2]

Measures

Function scores and task times. [2]

Results

Better SHAP (p = 0.04) and faster clothespin task (p = 0.02); no difference on Box and Blocks or ACMC; seven of eight preferred pattern recognition. [2]

Additional value from AI

About 47 seconds faster on the clothespin task. [2]

Limitations

Eight participants; developers ran the trial and two hold a financial interest in Coapt; some unintended movements. [2]

Denominators

Six to eight weeks of home use under each control method. [2]

Materials for replication

Data available from the corresponding author on request. [2]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Users need a way to recalibrate the classifier at home; participants recalibrated 32.6 times on average during the trial. Participants were already competent users of myoelectric prostheses before the trial.

Replication attempts

1 follow-up entry. Benefit recurred: 1.

Entry details

Sector

Healthcare.

Purpose

Pattern recognition control was built to let amputees operate several prosthetic joints in sequence without switching modes. The better function and the preference that the trial measured were that purpose.
