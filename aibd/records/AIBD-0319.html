Te Hiku Media's te reo Māori recognition may be 92% accurate

Te Hiku Media's te reo Māori speech recognition, trained on 310 hours of community recordings, went from 86% accuracy in its first model to a reported 92% in 2024.

What happened

Te Hiku Media, a charitable iwi media and technology organization in Kaitaia, New Zealand, built its own te reo Māori automatic speech recognition. MIT Technology Review (22 April 2022) described a ten-day community competition in 2018. It collected “310 hours of speech-text pairs from some 200,000 recordings made by roughly 2,500 people”. From that, bootstrapping an open-source Mozilla model, “Te Hiku created its very first te reo speech recognition model with 86% accuracy”. The tools are offered through the Papa Reo platform. NVIDIA's blog (16 January 2024) says that the model “transcribes te reo Māori with 92% accuracy” using the NeMo toolkit. The University of Auckland announced on 18 March 2024 that its Archive of Māori and Pacific Sound is working with Te Hiku Media. The partners are testing the tool on archival recordings with different dialects, recording qualities and sung forms. Automatic output is compared “against human-generated ones”, with results to be shared with the galleries, libraries, archives and museums sector. The University says that the tool “is performing very well for their purposes” in Te Hiku's broadcasting arm. In September 2024 RNZ and Ngā Taonga Sound & Vision agreed to supply previously broadcast te reo recordings to train and review the tools. An RNZ release of 11 September 2024 quotes Te Hiku chief executive Peter-Lucas Jones in te reo giving the same 92% figure. The release says that RNZ is integrating the Papa Reo application programming interface into its workflows, first for search and then for captions. No cited source reports a measured outcome for a speaker, learner or archive user. The accuracy figures originate with Te Hiku Media and NVIDIA.

Details

AI system

Configuration

The earlier model used Mozilla open-source tooling; the 2024 account describes training with NVIDIA NeMo. These refer to successive development stages.

Inputs and outputs

Recorded te reo Māori speech became draft text through automatic speech recognition on the Papa Reo platform.

Workflow

A community-contributed read-speech corpus (310 hours in ten days) held under Te Hiku Media's data-sovereignty rules. Bootstrapping from open-source speech models and, later, NVIDIA's NeMo toolkit.

Human oversight

Archive partners planned to compare machine transcripts with human transcripts across dialects, audio quality and sung forms.

Benefits

Reach

The sources give no count of speakers, learners or archive users who benefited. Roughly 2,500 people contributed recordings to the training data in the 2018 competition. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Duration

MIT Technology Review reported the 86% figure for the first model in 2022, and NVIDIA and RNZ reported 92% in January and September 2024. On 11 September 2024 RNZ announced that it was integrating the Papa Reo application programming interface into its workflows for search and captions. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Recordings resemble the training data. The 2024 tests deliberately probe regional dialects, recording quality and sung forms such as mōteatea and waiata. Archive holders license recordings on the partners' conditions (no pecuniary gain, previously broadcast material only).
