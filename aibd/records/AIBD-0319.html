Te Hiku Media reports 92% accuracy for te reo Māori recognition

transcribes te reo Māori with 92% accuracy

Iwi broadcaster's ASR, trained on 310 hours of community recordings (86% accuracy, 2022), reported at 92% in 2024; archive tests announced in 2024 with results to come; no speaker outcome documented.

What happened

Te Hiku Media, a charitable iwi media and technology organisation in Kaitaia, New Zealand, built its own te reo Māori automatic speech recognition. MIT Technology Review (22 April 2022) reported that a 2018 community competition collected '310 hours of speech-text pairs from some 200,000 recordings made by roughly 2,500 people' in ten days, from which, bootstrapping an open-source Mozilla model, 'Te Hiku created its very first te reo speech recognition model with 86% accuracy'; the tools are offered through the Papa Reo platform. NVIDIA's blog (16 January 2024) says the model 'transcribes te reo Māori with 92% accuracy' using the NeMo toolkit, and an RNZ release of 11 September 2024 quotes Te Hiku chief executive Peter-Lucas Jones in te reo giving the same 92% figure. The University of Auckland announced on 18 March 2024, the date used here, that its Archive of Māori and Pacific Sound is collaborating with Te Hiku Media to test the tool on archival recordings with different dialects, recording qualities and sung forms, comparing automatic output 'against human-generated ones', with results to be shared with the galleries, libraries, archives and museums sector; it says the tool 'is performing very well for their purposes' in Te Hiku's broadcasting arm. RNZ and Ngā Taonga Sound & Vision agreed in September 2024 to supply previously broadcast te reo recordings to train and review the tools. No fetched source reports a measured outcome for a speaker, learner or archive user; the accuracy figures originate with Te Hiku Media and NVIDIA.

What it was built to do. Built to transcribe te reo Māori recordings so that archives and broadcasts become searchable and readable by learners, under Māori data-sovereignty rules; the transcription tests are that intended use.

The system's part

The accuracy figures come from Te Hiku Media (86% reported by MIT Technology Review in 2022; 92% stated by NVIDIA, a tooling vendor, and by Te Hiku's chief executive through RNZ in 2024). The University of Auckland's independent test compares outputs against human transcripts with results to follow; no fetched source reports an outcome for any speaker, learner or archive beyond the developer's own broadcasting use.

Replication

What produced it
  • A community-contributed read-speech corpus (310 hours in ten days) held under Te Hiku Media's data-sovereignty rules
  • Bootstrapping from open-source speech models and, later, NVIDIA's NeMo toolkit
What must hold again
  • Recordings resemble the training data; the 2024 tests deliberately probe regional dialects, recording quality and sung forms such as mōteatea and waiata
  • Archive holders license recordings on the partners' conditions (no pecuniary gain, previously broadcast material only)
