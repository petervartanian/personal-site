Google neural network finds an eighth planet, Kepler-90i

A Google/UT Austin neural network found Kepler-90i in archival Kepler data; NASA announced it on 14 December 2017 and the AJ published it on 30 January 2018.

ⓘ

What happened

On 14 December 2017 NASA announced a deep convolutional neural network had identified an eighth planet, Kepler-90i, in archival Kepler Space Telescope data. Christopher Shallue (Google Brain) and Andrew Vanderburg (University of Texas at Austin, NASA Sagan Fellow) built it. It was trained on about 15,000 previously vetted Kepler signals and correctly identified true planets and false positives 96 percent of the time in testing. It was then applied to 670 known multi-planet systems to search for weak transit signals missed by earlier methods. Kepler-90i is a rocky planet about 30 percent larger than Earth with a 14.4-day orbit. It was statistically validated together with Kepler-80g, an Earth-sized sixth planet in a resonant chain. The peer-reviewed paper appeared in the Astronomical Journal on 30 January 2018.

Purpose. Built to classify Kepler transit signals as planets or false positives and to recover planets missed by earlier searches. This benefit was that purpose.

AI’s contribution

The neural network ranked the weak Kepler-90i transit signal above false positives in systems that earlier automated and human searches had already examined. The authors then statistically validated both new planets. The AI did the identification. Validation used conventional methods.

Beneficiaries

  • an organization

    NASA's Paul Hertz said that discoveries were lurking in archived Kepler data awaiting the right tool. The archive yielded two newly validated planets without new telescope observations by the authors. Their validation used Kepler data and follow-up observations that other observers had already uploaded to the Kepler Community Follow-up Program.

  • society

    Astronomy gained a validated eighth planet and the first exoplanetary system known to have eight planets, tying the Solar System. This is a knowledge gain for a specialist community rather than a population-level benefit.

Basis

Since then
The same group adapted the network as AstroNet-K2 for the K2 mission and used it to identify and validate two previously unknown exoplanets.We train a convolutional neural network, called AstroNet-K2, to predict whether a given possible exoplanet signal is really caused by an exoplanet or a false positive. AstroNet-K2 is highly successful at classifying exoplanets and false positives, with accuracy of 98% on our test set. source

Further evidence

Setting. Large labeled set of vetted transit signals and false positives. Convolutional network trained on folded light curves. Application to systems with known planets to look for weaker signals. Statistical validation of high-confidence candidates.

Conditions. The host star's light curve must contain the transit at detectable but sub-threshold signal-to-noise. Validation must rule out astrophysical false positives independently of the classifier.
