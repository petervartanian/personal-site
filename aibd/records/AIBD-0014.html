What happened

On 14 December 2017 NASA announced that a deep convolutional neural network built by Christopher Shallue (Google Brain) and Andrew Vanderburg (University of Texas at Austin, NASA Sagan Fellow) had identified an eighth planet, Kepler-90i, in archival data from the Kepler Space Telescope. The network was trained on about 15,000 previously vetted Kepler signals, correctly identified true planets and false positives 96 percent of the time in testing, and was then applied to 670 known multi-planet systems to search for weak transit signals missed by earlier methods. Kepler-90i, a rocky planet about 30 percent larger than Earth with a 14.4-day orbit, was statistically validated together with Kepler-80g, an Earth-sized sixth planet in a resonant chain. The peer-reviewed paper appeared in the Astronomical Journal on 30 January 2018.

What it was built to do. Built to classify Kepler transit signals as planets or false positives and to recover planets missed by earlier searches; this benefit was that purpose.

The system's part

The neural network ranked the weak Kepler-90i transit signal above false positives in systems that earlier automated and human searches had already examined; the authors then statistically validated both new planets. The AI did the identification; validation used conventional methods.

Replication

What produced it. Large labeled set of vetted transit signals and false positives. Convolutional network trained on folded light curves. Application to systems with known planets to look for weaker signals. Statistical validation of high-confidence candidates.

What must hold again. The host star's light curve must contain the transit at detectable but sub-threshold signal-to-noise. Validation must rule out astrophysical false positives independently of the classifier.
