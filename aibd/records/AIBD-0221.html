Dytective detects 80.6% of dyslexia cases in a study later challenged

Dytective, a gamified online dyslexia test with a random-forest classifier, screened 3,644 children with 79.8% accuracy, and two 2026 papers found a demographic confound in its dataset.

  • Not rated a person

    The sources report classifier accuracy (80.6% recall) and aggregate use (over 200,000 people). None reports what followed a screening result for any child.

  • Not rated an organization

    No source documents an outcome for the 60 participating schools or the other organizations beyond supplying participants.

Language-group differences could produce unequal false-positive rates, so pooled accuracy did not establish equally reliable screening for every child.

What happened

Luz Rello (IE University, Madrid), Baeza-Yates, Ali, Bigham and Serra published in PLoS ONE (2 December 2020) a web-based gamified test of 32 linguistic exercises. Its performance measures feed a random-forest classifier with 200 trees. The study enrolled 3,644 participants aged 7–17, 392 with a professional dyslexia diagnosis, recruited through 113 organizations in Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Spain and the USA. They were 60 primary and secondary schools, 22 specialized dyslexia centers, 18 non-profits and 3 universities. The authors report that a trivial classifier that labels every child as not having dyslexia would reach 89.2% accuracy on this sample, and they weighted the classes to offset that imbalance. The classifier reached 79.8% accuracy with 80.6% recall and 79.3% precision for the dyslexia class. A tablet validation with 1,395 participants aged 12–17 gave 78.1% recall and 76.2% precision. The authors state that an online Spanish screening tool based on their methods had already been used by more than 200,000 people. They disclose a patent application (US 15/493,060, Rello and Ballesteros). Sudha and Murali (Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 2 September 2026) trained a different model on the public Dytective dataset. They found that demographic variables contribute more to discrimination than any level of their engineered cognitive features, and a variant without demographics reached an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.7948. The authors call for external validation across language groups before clinical use. In a second independent paper (Frontiers in Digital Health, 23 September 2026), Sudha and Murali re-analyzed the same 3,644-participant dataset. They report that dyslexia prevalence in the dataset differs 4.7-fold by native-language status. Four demographic columns alone reach an AUC of 0.724 against 0.744 for all 192 task metrics. Including them yields a twelve-fold disparity in false-positive rate between language groups.

Details

AI system

Model

A random-forest classifier with 200 trees and no maximum height.

Release

Implemented with Weka 3.8.3. No separate release identifies the fitted classifier.

Role

The classifier estimated dyslexia risk from the screening assessment.

Configuration

The Weka 3.8.3 implementation used 200 trees without a maximum height.

Inputs and outputs

Performance on 32 Spanish-language exercises and participant characteristics fed a classifier that estimated dyslexia risk.

Workflow

Thirty-two gamified linguistic exercises administered in a browser. A labeled sample of 3,644 children, 392 with professional diagnoses, gathered through 113 organizations. A random-forest classifier trained on performance measures.

Human oversight

No source describes human review of individual screening results. The 2020 study used a professional dyslexia diagnosis as its reference standard, and the authors of a 2026 paper call for external validation across language groups before clinical use. [1] [2] [3]

Benefits

Reach

No beneficiary count is established. The 2020 authors state that the online tool had been used by more than 200,000 people, and no source reports what followed a screening result. [1] [2] [3]

Duration

No source follows children after a screening result. The 2020 authors state that the online tool had already been used by more than 200,000 people. In September 2026 two independent papers re-analyzed the same dataset, and neither tested the deployed tool on new children. [1] [2] [3]

Measurement

Measurement

Diagnostic accuracy of a random-forest classifier on 32 gamified exercises, scored against professional dyslexia diagnoses with 10-fold cross-validation, and a second test on tablets. [1]

Setting and sample

3,644 Spanish-speaking participants aged 7 to 17 (392 with a diagnosis) recruited through 113 organizations in Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Spain and the United States; tablet sample of 1,395. [1]

Measures

Accuracy, recall and precision for the dyslexia class. [1]

Results

79.8% accuracy, 80.6% recall and 79.3% precision; 78.1% recall on tablets for ages 12 to 17. [1]

Limitations

Single dataset with no external validation; the makers wrote the study and hold a patent application. Independent re-analyses in 2026 report a demographic confound by native language. [1]

Materials for replication

Dataset public on Kaggle (doi 10.34740/kaggle/dsv/1617514). [1]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Samples balanced on native language and other demographics, which the 2026 re-analysis finds this dataset is not. Professional diagnosis as the reference standard. Spanish-language content for Spanish-speaking populations.

Replication attempts

1 follow-up entry. Benefit did not recur: 1.

Evidence

Original evidence

Both 2026 papers by Sudha and Murali use the public dataset from the 2020 study, so all three studies rest on the same data. The 2020 authors recruited participants with dyslexia through dyslexia centers and associations and participants without dyslexia through schools. They disclose a patent application. The 2026 authors have no tie to the Dytective team. [1] [2] [3]
