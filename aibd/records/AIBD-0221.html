Dytective online dyslexia screener reaches 80.6% recall

79.8% accuracy with 80.6% recall and 79.3% precision

Rello et al.'s random-forest screener, tested on 3,644 children from 113 organizations in five countries, 79.8% accuracy; the tool has been used over 200,000 times. A 2026 paper reports confounding.

What happened

Luz Rello (IE University, Madrid), Baeza-Yates, Ali, Bigham and Serra published in PLoS ONE on 2 December 2020 a web-based gamified test of 32 linguistic exercises whose performance measures feed a random-forest classifier with 200 trees. The study enrolled 3,644 participants aged 7-17, 392 with a professional dyslexia diagnosis, recruited through 113 organizations in Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Spain and the USA (60 primary and secondary schools, 22 specialized dyslexia centers, 18 non-profits and 3 universities); the classifier reached 79.8% accuracy with 80.6% recall and 79.3% precision for the dyslexia class, and a tablet validation with 1,395 participants aged 12-17 gave 78.1% recall and 76.2% precision. The authors state that an online Spanish screening tool based on their methods had already been used by more than 200,000 people and disclose a patent application (US 15/493,060, Rello and Ballesteros). A re-analysis of the same 3,644-participant dataset by Sudha and Murali (Frontiers in Digital Health, 23 September 2026) reports that dyslexia prevalence in the dataset differs 4.7-fold by native-language status, that four demographic columns alone reach AUC 0.724 against 0.744 for all 192 task metrics, and that including them yields a twelve-fold disparity in false-positive rate between language groups. The date observed is the PLoS ONE publication date.

What it was built to do. Dytective was designed to screen children for dyslexia risk cheaply online; the accuracy figures and usage are that purpose.

The system's part

The random-forest classifier is the screening decision; the 2026 re-analysis shows that demographic variables alone reach nearly the same AUC in this dataset and that false-positive rates differ twelve-fold between language groups, so the measured accuracy may partly reflect sample composition.

Replication

What produced it

  • Thirty-two gamified linguistic exercises administered in a browser
  • A labeled sample of 3,644 children, 392 with professional diagnoses, gathered through 113 organizations
  • A random-forest classifier trained on performance measures

What must hold again

  • Samples balanced on native language and other demographics, which the 2026 re-analysis finds this dataset is not
  • Professional diagnosis as the reference standard
  • Spanish-language content for Spanish-speaking populations
