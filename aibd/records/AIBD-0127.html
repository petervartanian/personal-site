A man with a rare blood disorder became well enough for a transplant

Mr. Joseph Coates received three drugs (i) that a consulting physician had considered and (ii) that Every Cure’s early drug-repurposing system had scored highly.

ⓘ

What happened

In January 2024, Joseph Coates, 37, of Renton, Washington, was critically ill with POEMS syndrome. His heart and kidneys were failing, and he was too sick for a stem cell transplant. His partner, Tara Theobald, asked David Fajgenbaum of the University of Pennsylvania and Every Cure for help. Fajgenbaum considered three drugs used for multiple myeloma, a related disease. He later explained that an early Every Cure algorithm also scored those drugs highly for POEMS. He recommended carfilzomib, cyclophosphamide, and dexamethasone to Coates’s treating hematologist and oncologist, Wayne Gao, who agreed to administer them. Coates later reported that he responded within one or two weeks and left intensive care three weeks later. He recovered enough to receive a stem cell transplant and reported full remission five months after that transplant.

The New York Times reported improvement within a week and transplant eligibility about four months later. A brief vignette in The Lancet Haematology describes a matching unnamed patient and labels the result unpublished data. The sources support a contribution from AI alongside the physicians’ judgment, but they do not establish the exact model release or isolate its effect on recovery.

Purpose. Every Cure uses AI to rank existing drugs as candidates for other diseases. Its early algorithms helped Fajgenbaum assess drugs that he had considered for Coates’s POEMS syndrome. The New Yorker notes that Every Cure does not usually make recommendations for individual patients.

AI’s contribution

Fajgenbaum said that he had considered three drugs used for multiple myeloma and that Every Cure’s AI algorithm also scored them highly for POEMS syndrome. In an earlier AAMC interview, he described reviewing the scores and recommending the drugs to Coates’s treating physician. Wayne Gao weighed the risks and administered the treatment. The AI therefore supported the physicians’ assessment of candidate drugs. The sources do not show that it independently devised the regimen or that the benefit would have been impossible without it. The Lancet Haematology commentary contains a brief vignette of a matching unnamed POEMS patient, explicitly labeled unpublished data. No detailed clinical case report, underlying medical records, or case-specific model output was located. The commentary names KGML-xDTD as one of several algorithms in Every Cure’s platform, but it does not establish which algorithm or release was used for Coates.

Further evidence

Setting. Fajgenbaum recognized similarities between POEMS syndrome and multiple myeloma and reviewed high AI scores for drugs that were used for myeloma. Fajgenbaum answered the family’s urgent appeal and discussed treatment with Wayne Gao, who assessed the risks and administered the three drugs.

Conditions. Clinicians must assess the proposed drugs, the patient’s condition, and the risks of treatment. A high model score alone does not establish that a drug is suitable. The patient must respond sufficiently to receive any further treatment that is needed. Coates later underwent a stem cell transplant before he reported full remission.
