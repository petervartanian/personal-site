A man with a rare blood disorder became well enough for a transplant

Every Cure’s early drug-repurposing system gave high scores to three drugs that a consulting physician had considered for Joseph Coates’s POEMS syndrome; he responded and later reached remission.

  • 5 a person

    Coates was critically ill, too sick for a transplant, and facing hospice care. He reported that he began responding to the drug regimen within one or two weeks, later received a stem cell transplant, and reached full remission five months after that transplant. The reported benefit was life-preserving. The available evidence does not isolate AI’s contribution from clinical judgment, the drugs, supportive care, or the transplant.

Brief from Peter

Public money now goes to the search for new uses of old drugs, and the case of Joseph Coates is told in its support.1 In January 2024, Coates, 37, of Renton, Washington, was critically ill with POEMS syndrome and too sick for a stem cell transplant. David Fajgenbaum of the University of Pennsylvania and Every Cure recommended three drugs used for multiple myeloma, and Coates’s hematologist and oncologist, Wayne Gao, agreed to give them. Fajgenbaum later explained that an early Every Cure algorithm had also scored those drugs highly.2 Coates reported that he responded within one or two weeks. He later received a transplant and reported full remission five months after it. Several outlets reported the recovery.3 In a case series published on 31 May 2023, Mayo Clinic authors reported that five of six patients with relapsed POEMS syndrome responded to regimens built on carfilzomib, one of the three drugs, although none of them received this combination.4 The part that the algorithm played rests on accounts from Fajgenbaum and Every Cure, because the sources include no detailed case report or case-specific model output and do not establish which algorithm or release scored the drugs.5

Details

AI system

Application developer

Every Cure [3] [6] [10]

Configuration

The system scored candidate drugs for POEMS syndrome in January 2024. Physicians reviewed the scores and chose the treatment. [3] [6] [7] [8] [9] [10]

Inputs and outputs

Every Cure assigned scores to candidate drugs for POEMS syndrome, including three drugs that Fajgenbaum had considered because they treated a related disease.

Workflow

Fajgenbaum recognized similarities between POEMS syndrome and multiple myeloma and reviewed high AI scores for drugs that were used for myeloma. Fajgenbaum answered the family’s urgent appeal and discussed treatment with Wayne Gao, who assessed the risks and administered the three drugs.

Human oversight

Fajgenbaum recommended the regimen, and Wayne Gao assessed the patient and agreed to administer it.

AI contribution

A consulting physician considered three myeloma drugs and reviewed the high scores that Every Cure’s algorithm gave them for POEMS syndrome. The treating physician assessed the risks and administered them. The available accounts do not isolate the AI’s contribution to recovery. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9]

Conditions for replication

Clinicians need to assess the patient’s condition, the drugs, and their risks. A high AI score does not establish suitability. In this instance, recovery also depended on a response sufficient to permit a later transplant. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9]
