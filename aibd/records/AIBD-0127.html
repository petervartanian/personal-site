Every Cure's AI regimen puts a dying POEMS patient in remission

depth 4 for a person

What happened

In January 2024 (Every Cure's dating) Joseph Coates, 37, of Renton, Washington, was dying of POEMS syndrome, a rare blood disorder. His heart was enlarged, kidneys failing, liters of fluid drained from his abdomen every few days, too sick for a stem cell transplant. His partner Tara Theobald emailed David Fajgenbaum of the University of Pennsylvania, co-founder of the non-profit Every Cure. He replied next morning with an untested combination of chemotherapy, immunotherapy and steroids from Every Cure's repurposing model. The New Yorker names cyclophosphamide, carfilzomib and dexamethasone, a myeloma regimen. He responded within a week and had the transplant about four months later. He was in remission when the New York Times reported the case on 20 March 2025.

What it was built to do. Every Cure's platform was built to score about 4,000 existing drugs against 18,500 diseases to find repurposing candidates. Proposing a myeloma regimen for POEMS syndrome is that function. The New Yorker notes that Every Cure does not usually make recommendations for individual patients.

The system's part

Every Cure says an early version of its algorithms scored carfilzomib, cyclophosphamide, dexamethasone and related agents highly for POEMS syndrome. The reason was its similarity to multiple myeloma. The New York Times states the regimen was generated by an AI model rather than devised by a person. Fajgenbaum chose to query the platform, judged the output and relayed it. The treating doctor, who thought it sounded “a little bit crazy” per the New Yorker, administered three standard myeloma drugs. No clinical write-up of the case was found in PubMed or Europe PMC on 20 October 2026. Every Cure says a separate group described the same regimen for POEMS at a conference in summer 2024. So the drugs' plausibility does not rest on the model alone.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. A rare disease that closely resembles a well-treated one (POEMS syndrome and multiple myeloma), so that established drugs score highly in a drug-disease matrix. A physician-scientist with access to the model who answered a direct appeal within a day. He persuaded the treating doctor to try an untested regimen.

What must hold again. The model's output must be reviewed by a clinician. Every Cure describes a team that vets predictions before laboratory tests or contact with doctors. The patient must survive long enough to respond and then qualify for the definitive treatment, here a stem cell transplant about four months later.
