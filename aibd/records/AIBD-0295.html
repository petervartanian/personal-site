What happened

The Dexcom G6 continuous glucose monitor was classified by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) under De Novo DEN170088 on 27 March 2018. Its “Urgent Low Soon” (ULS) alert fires when an algorithm predicts an estimated glucose value of 55 mg/dL or below within 20 minutes. Dexcom employees Puhr, Derdzinski, Welsh, Parker, Walker and Price compared 1,424 users before and after moving from the G5 (no predictive alert) to the G6. Their anonymized comparison was in Diabetes Technology & Therapeutics (online 22 March 2019, the date used here). With a 70 mg/dL low-threshold setting (n=658) time below 54 mg/dL fell from 1.0% to 0.6%. With an 80 mg/dL setting (n=766) it fell 0.6% to 0.4% (both P<0.001). ULS fired 0.9 and 0.6 times a day. A second Dexcom analysis (Puhr et al., Journal of Diabetes Science and Technology, online 3 April 2019; abstract only) had 15,000 G6 app users.

More than 93% had ULS enabled. Its use was associated with less time below 55 and 70 mg/dL regardless of how often users viewed the app. The FDA decision summary describes the threshold and predictive alerts as intended to warn patients to act before a hypoglycemic event. Independent evidence is thinner. The PACE crossover randomized trial at Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust had 24 adults with type 1 diabetes. Each had ULS on or off for 40 days. It found no difference in hypoglycemia below 3.0 or 3.9 mmol/L in the 24 hours after exercise. Time below 2.8 mmol/L was lower with ULS on (P=0.04). Preechasuk et al. (Imperial College London) had 29 adults who switched from FreeStyle Libre 2 to a Dexcom G7 with ULS. They spent less time below 70 mg/dL (3.0% to 2.0%, P=0.006). That changes device and alert together.

A Prague case report records a patient with impaired hypoglycemia awareness whose CGM with ULS on did not eliminate prolonged hypoglycemia. He sometimes muted the alerts. None of the sources calls the predictive algorithm machine learning.

What it was built to do. Built to warn users 20 minutes before predicted serious hypoglycemia so they can act. The reported reductions in time below 54–55 mg/dL are that purpose.

The system's part

The alert is generated when the G6 algorithm forecasts glucose at or below 55 mg/dL within 20 minutes. Users still have to act on it. The principal outcome evidence is the maker's own real-world analyses (Puhr et al. 2019, 2020). They compare the same users before and after the G6 transition without a control arm. The independent Imperial College PACE randomized trial found no difference at 3.0 or 3.9 mmol/L after exercise and a difference only below 2.8 mmol/L. The Imperial switch study cannot separate the alert from the change of device. None of the sources describes the prediction method as machine learning.

Replication

What produced it. Real-time CGM with the ULS alert enabled (over 93% of G6 app users in Dexcom's sample). Users who respond to the alert by treating or adjusting insulin, which the sources assume rather than measure. A low-threshold alert also set (70 or 80 mg/dL in the Dexcom analysis).

What must hold again. Users keep the alert on rather than muting it for alarm fatigue, as the Prague patient sometimes did. Sensor accuracy sufficient to avoid nuisance alerts, which Puhr et al. cite as a cause of CGM discontinuation. Benefit beyond a plain threshold alert, which the PACE trial found only below 2.8 mmol/L.
