Software flags Swedish children at risk of dyslexia from eye movements

A support-vector machine classifier from Karolinska Institutet identified 9–10-year-olds at risk of persistent reading difficulty from under one minute of eye tracking with 95.6% accuracy.

  • Not rated a person

    The study reports cross-validated classification accuracy (95.6%) on 185 children. No source reports what followed for any child.

What happened

Mattias Nilsson Benfatto, Gustaf Öqvist Seimyr, Jan Ygge, Tony Pansell and Agneta Rydberg of Karolinska Institutet worked with Christer Jacobson of Linnaeus University. In PLoS ONE on 9 December 2016, they reported that eye-movement recordings of under one minute of reading can identify 9–10-year-olds at risk of persistent reading difficulties. They used cross-validation on 97 high-risk and 88 low-risk control subjects. A support-vector machine with recursive feature elimination selected 48 of 168 features. It reached 95.6% (± 4.5%) accuracy with balanced sensitivity and specificity. The competing-interests statement says that Benfatto and Seimyr own equity in Optolexia, a company aiming to offer technologies for assessing reading deficits in school-age children. The venture is funded by Sweden's innovation agency VINNOVA. The Ulla and Ingemar Dahlberg Foundation and the Promobilia Foundation also supported the work. The data are on Figshare. In August 2021 a team at the Slovak University of Technology reanalyzed the same 185 recordings with a different classifier and reported 96.6% accuracy, against 95.6% in the original paper. Lexplore, a Swedish company, sells a reading screener that it says Öqvist Seimyr and Nilsson Benfatto built on the Kronoberg data. The company's own site claims that one Swedish school group of 32 schools and 13,131 students used it from 2021/22 to 2024/25. Schools Week reported on 30 March 2025 that two English academy trusts had piloted Lexplore with 265 and 876 pupils. St Bart's Multi Academy Trust found that 226 of its 876 pupils in the baseline testing had below-average or worse reading ability. Schools Week also reported that a privacy group raised concerns about how pupils' data might be used.

Details

AI system

Model

A support-vector machine with recursive feature elimination used 48 of the 168 measured features.

Inputs and outputs

The classifier used eye movements recorded during less than one minute of reading to classify children as at high or low risk of persistent reading difficulty.

Workflow

Eye-tracking records of under one minute of reading. A labeled sample of 97 high-risk and 88 low-risk 9–10-year-olds. Feature selection (SVM-RFE) within repeated cross-validation.

Human oversight

Researchers trained the classifier and cross-validated it against predefined high-risk and low-risk groups. No source describes how the schools that piloted Lexplore checked its results. [1] [2] [3]

Benefits

Reach

No source counts children who benefited. The study classified 185 children, and the English pilots of Lexplore tested 265 and 876 pupils. The company's own site claims that 13,131 students in one Swedish school group used it. [1] [2] [3]

Duration

No source follows the children over time. Schools Week reported on 30 March 2025 that two English academy trusts had piloted Lexplore, a screener that its maker says rests on this research. The company's own site claims that one Swedish school group used it from 2021/22 to 2024/25. [1] [2] [3]

Measurement

Measurement

Support-vector machine with recursive feature elimination, scored by repeated cross-validation against high-risk and low-risk labels set from earlier reading tests. [1]

Setting and sample

185 Swedish children (97 high-risk, 88 low-risk) from the Kronoberg reading development project, recorded at age 9 to 10. [1]

Measures

Classification accuracy, sensitivity and specificity. [1]

Results

95.6% (± 4.5%) accuracy against 49.1% for chance and 91.1% for random feature selection. [1]

Limitations

Case-control sample of extreme groups; no external validation sample; two authors hold equity in Optolexia. [1]

Materials for replication

Data deposited on Figshare; an independent team reused them in 2021. [1]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Eye-tracking hardware in the screening setting. Reading material and norms for the child's language. Validation on an independent sample, which the cited source does not report.

Replication attempts

1 follow-up entry. Benefit recurred: 1.

The 2021 Slovak reanalysis used the same 185 recordings as the Karolinska study. Lexplore's maker says that its screener rests on this research. Its use in schools is reported by Schools Week and by the company's own site. [1] [2] [3]
