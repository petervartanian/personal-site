ChatGPT's first-aid advice may have saved a teen in Assiut

not rated the dean denies that there was a poisoning

Independently reported, 6 sources.

A Facebook post of 24 May 2025 credits ChatGPT with first aid. Assiut Faculty of Medicine dean Alaa Attia says that the youth had no phosphine symptoms and said he threw the pill into water.

What happened

El Balad (24 May 2025, 12:39) reproduces a Facebook post by Islam Adel, a commercial-control inspector. He wrote it at 1 a.m. after “24 hours” had passed since his cousin Mohamed Yousry (17) swallowed part of a “grain pill” (aluminum phosphide). The pill came from samples Adel had seized and kept at the family's old house. The cousin took it after a quarrel with his father. Adel writes that the Abnoub central hospital refused the case. He writes that within minutes he asked ChatGPT for the best course. It advised paraffin oil to coat the pill and limit phosphine release. The youth drank a bottle from a pharmacy before they went to Assiut University Hospital's poison reception. Doctors there said there was no treatment protocol and the case would die. He then gave instructions from “an advanced AI model and a limited-use trial version of GPT-5”.

Nine bottles of paraffin oil were given in turn with breathing exercises. The cousin stayed conscious, vomited for hours and was discharged stable. Dostor (24 May 2025, 22:40) reports the post and the reply of Dr Alaa Attia, dean of the Faculty of Medicine at Assiut University. He said precise medical examinations showed no signs or symptoms of swallowing the pill. The youth denied taking it and admitted throwing it into a water basin. The dean added that the hospital is researching paraffin oil with acetylcysteine as an adjunct. The protocol is experimental and not officially adopted. Masrawy (25 May 2025) quotes the dean that the youth, about 17, showed no clinical symptoms of phosphine poisoning. The team questioned him and he categorically denied swallowing the pill. He cited a family dispute over travel to Italy.

Dostor (25 May 2025) adds toxicologist Dr Mahmoud Amr, founder of the National Poison Center. He says oils are indeed a necessary first aid in grain-pill poisoning. Al Jazeera Mubasher (26 May 2025) quotes Amr that the claim that paraffin oil rescues such a case “has no scientific basis”. It quotes Cairo University toxicologist Dr Nermin Hamdi that Assiut University denied the case involved the grain pill. Hamdi adds that giving oil is first aid, not treatment. The Monitor lists 13 syndications. Al Arabiya's syndication was not available. The ingestion date is not fixed by any source. The post date is used.

Not what it was built to do. ChatGPT is a general-purpose assistant. The cousin used it for emergency first-aid advice and, by his account, for instructions during hospital care. The claimed benefit (a survival) is outside any stated purpose of the system.

The system's part

ChatGPT's output (advice to give paraffin oil, then nine bottles in turn with breathing exercises) is documented only by the cousin's own post. The chat transcripts were not published (“too many to publish all”, per Masrawy's summary of the post). Dean Alaa Attia contradicts its effect: he says that the patient had no clinical signs of phosphine poisoning and denied swallowing the pill. Dr Nermin Hamdi's report that Assiut University denied the case involved the grain pill also contradicts it. Dr Mahmoud Amr says that oil is a recognized first aid but not a cure. Whether the hospital followed any of the AI's instructions is unsupported by any source other than the post.

Replication

No attempt to repeat it has been recorded.

What produced it. A layperson prompting ChatGPT for first aid during a suspected aluminum phosphide ingestion. Paraffin oil bought from a pharmacy and given before and, by the cousin's account, during hospital care.

What must hold again. That the pill was swallowed at all, which the treating dean says the patient denied. Clinical confirmation of phosphine poisoning and of any effect of the oil. Assiut's paraffin-oil-plus-acetylcysteine protocol is described by the dean as experimental and not officially adopted.
