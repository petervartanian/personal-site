Daejeon's Kkumdori robot hears “I want to die” and brings police

depth 3 for a person, depth 1 for an organization

Independently reported, 8 sources.

At 2 a.m. on 6 August 2025 Daejeon's Kkumdori care robot flagged a 70s woman's “I want to die”; police came and she was admitted next day.

What happened

At about 2 a.m. on 6 August 2025, a woman in her 70s identified as “A” was at home in Deogam-dong, Daedeok-gu, Daejeon, South Korea. She lives alone and has schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. She repeatedly told the city-issued AI care robot Kkumdori “I want to die” and “save me”. The robot's crisis-detection algorithm flagged the words and alerted the operator's control center. A staff member phoned her. When she kept repeating them, staff reported to police on 112 with her location. Officers found her emotionally unstable and contacted her family. Police judged hospital treatment urgent. On the afternoon of 7 August she was admitted with her guardian's consent. Daejeon announced the case on 13 August 2025.

What it was built to do. Daejeon issued 1,000 Kkumdori robots from January 2025, 200 per district, under its community integrated-care project. They went to elderly, disabled and low-income people living alone. They offer companionship, medication and schedule reminders and health monitoring. They detect crisis signs such as suicide risk and depression and automatically notify agencies and guardians. The alert was that function.

The system's part

The robot's crisis-detection algorithm recognized repeated danger words (“죽고 싶다”, “살려줘”) in conversation at 2 a.m. It sent an alert to a human control center. People did everything after that: the check-in call, the 112 report with location, the police visit, the family contact and the admission. The city's welfare director says the alert is what made the rapid dispatch and protection possible. Whether A would have come to harm that night is not established. Every account derives from the city's 13 August briefing.

Replication

What produced it. A municipal program that put conversational robots with a crisis-word detection algorithm into 1,000 homes of people living alone, 200 per district, from January 2025. A 24-hour human control center run by the supplier that receives alerts, phones the resident, and reports to police (112) with the location. A resident who habitually talked, sang and danced with the robot, so her statements at 2 a.m. were spoken aloud to it. Police able to attend at night, and a family and hospital able to complete a protective admission the next day.

What must hold again. Alerts go to humans who act at night. The robot itself does nothing beyond detecting words and raising the alarm. The detection catches explicit statements (“죽고 싶다”, “살려줘”). A silent crisis would not trigger it. Residents keep talking to the device. Her guardian said it was a great support to her.
