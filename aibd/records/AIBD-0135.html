Daejeon's Kkumdori robot hears 'I want to die' and brings police

At 2 a.m. on 6 Aug 2025 Daejeon's Kkumdori care robot flagged a 70s woman's 'I want to die'; police came and she was admitted next day.

What happened

At about 2 a.m. on 6 August 2025, in a home in Deogam-dong, Daedeok-gu, Daejeon, South Korea, a woman in her 70s identified as 'A', who lives alone and has schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, repeatedly told the city-issued AI care robot Kkumdori 'I want to die' and 'save me'. The robot's crisis-detection algorithm flagged the words and alerted the operator's control centre; a staff member phoned her, and when she kept repeating them, reported to police on 112 with her location. Officers found her emotionally unstable and contacted her family; police judged hospital treatment urgent, and on the afternoon of 7 August she was admitted with her guardian's consent. Daejeon announced the case on 13 August 2025.

What it was built to do. Daejeon issued 1,000 Kkumdori robots from January 2025, 200 per district, under its community integrated-care project to elderly, disabled and low-income people living alone, for companionship, medication and schedule reminders, health monitoring, and detection of crisis signs such as suicide risk and depression with automatic notification to agencies and guardians. The alert was exactly that function.

Who benefited

  • -3
    personal

    A woman in her 70s living alone, with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, in acute crisis at 2 a.m., got a check-in call, a police visit that night and protective hospital admission the next afternoon; the city and the headlines call it life-saving, but no attempt was reported, so the counterfactual is unproven. Her guardian said the robot being beside her was a great support.

  • -1
    organizational

    Daejeon's welfare office gained a demonstrated case for its 1,000-robot programme; its director cited it as a model, and a 2026 Yonhap report says the city will continue the policy on the strength of such cases.

The system's part

The robot's crisis-detection algorithm recognised repeated danger words ('죽고 싶다', '살려줘') in conversation at 2 a.m. and sent an alert to a human control centre; people did everything after that: the check-in call, the 112 report with location, the police visit, the family contact and the admission. The city's welfare director says the alert is what made the rapid dispatch and protection possible. Whether A would have come to harm that night is not established, and every account derives from the city's 13 August briefing.

Replication

What produced it

  • A municipal programme that put conversational robots with a crisis-word detection algorithm into 1,000 homes of people living alone, 200 per district, from January 2025.
  • A 24-hour human control centre run by the supplier that receives alerts, phones the resident, and reports to police (112) with the location.
  • A resident who habitually talked, sang and danced with the robot, so her statements at 2 a.m. were spoken aloud to it.
  • Police able to attend at night, and a family and hospital able to complete a protective admission the next day.

What must hold again

  • Alerts go to humans who act at night; the robot itself does nothing beyond detecting words and raising the alarm.
  • The detection catches explicit statements ('죽고 싶다', '살려줘'); a silent crisis would not trigger it.
  • Residents keep talking to the device; her guardian said it was a great support to her.
