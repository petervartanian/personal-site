Azure AI Vision app helps blind, low-vision adults in 354 tasks

Gonzalez Penuela et al. (CHI 2024) log 354 diary entries from 16 BLV participants using an Azure AI Vision app; uses include telling sunglasses from reading glasses without calling a spouse.

ⓘ

What happened

Ricardo Gonzalez Penuela, Jazmin Collins, Cynthia Bennett and Shiri Azenkot (Cornell Tech and Google) ran a two-week diary study. It had 16 blind and low-vision adults in the United States (five men, 11 women, aged 24 to 67). Recruitment was via National Federation of the Blind mailing lists. They used an iPhone scene-description app the researchers built on Microsoft's Azure AI Vision image description application programming interface (API). It was chosen because it “exposes the latest deep learning algorithms”. Seeing AI also uses it. Participants submitted 354 entries (13 to 39 each). Documented uses include P1 photographing glasses. She “was not sure if this was my husband's sunglasses or my old pair of reading glasses from 4 years ago”. She got an answer without calling him.

P5 photographed a hotel bed to check his roommate was not in it, and P15 photographed wine bottles in a shop rather than touching them. Participants scored descriptions 2.76 out of 5 for satisfaction and 2.43 out of 4 for trust on average. The authors say satisfaction did not track accuracy because users inferred information from partially correct descriptions. The paper appeared at CHI 2024 (doi 10.1145/3613904.3642211). The arXiv version was submitted on 22 March 2024. The study dates are not reported. The CHI EA 2025 follow-up used a custom app, VisionPal, and documented failures such as hallucinated braille labels.

Purpose. The study app was built to describe photographed scenes for blind and low-vision users, emulating Seeing AI's scene channel. The documented identifications were that purpose.

AI’s contribution

Azure AI Vision generated every description. The researchers' app only submitted photos and collected ratings. The paper documents individual successful uses and an overall judgment that descriptions “still need significant improvements”. Participants were allowed to use other tools during the study, so the diary covers only what they chose to photograph with this app.

Since then
The same Cornell Tech group's follow-up two-week diary study (CHI EA 2025), with 20 BLV people using a newer app they built on a multimodal large language model, collected 553 entries; a preliminary analysis of 60 entries found trust 3.75/5 and satisfaction 4.15/5, higher than the 2024 scores.We found that participants considered the application's visual interpretations trustworthy (mean 3.75 out of 5) and satisfying (mean 4.15 out of 5). source

Further evidence

Setting. An iPhone app sending photos to the Azure AI Vision image description API. Blind and low-vision users photographing objects they did not want to touch or ask others about. A minimum of one photo per day for two weeks with a short questionnaire per entry.

Conditions. The description is accurate enough for the user to infer what they need. Users sometimes drew answers from partially correct output. The task does not require reading text, which the app could not do.
