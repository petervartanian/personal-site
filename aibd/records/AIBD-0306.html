Blind and low-vision adults use Eyevisor to identify everyday objects

In a two-week diary study, 16 blind and low-vision adults used Eyevisor, an app built on Microsoft Azure AI Vision, and rated its scene descriptions 2.76 out of 5 for satisfaction.

  • 1 a person

    Participants completed small daily identifications without asking family. They rated descriptions 2.76/5 for satisfaction and 2.43/4 for trust on average. The app could not read text.

Depth runs from 1, a convenience or time saved, to 5, a life or a livelihood preserved.

Status
Reported benefit. Participants reported useful everyday scene descriptions alongside low average satisfaction and trust.

Compared with

The system’s part
Azure AI Vision generated every description. The researchers' app only submitted photos and collected ratings. The paper documents individual successful uses and an overall judgment that descriptions “still need significant improvements”. Participants were allowed to use other tools during the study, so the diary covers only what they chose to photograph with this app.
Limits
A small, self-selected two-week diary study describes experiences rather than a causal gain in independence or safety. The 354 entries are repeated uses by 16 people, not 354 beneficiaries. The abstract’s low ratings must accompany positive anecdotes; detailed task accuracy and later follow-up were not reassessed.
Costs and harms
Partially correct descriptions could still help users answer a specific question, but low average satisfaction and trust indicate that reliance required judgment.
Later
Persisted, 7 March 2025. The same Cornell Tech group's follow-up two-week diary study (CHI EA 2025), with 20 BLV people using a newer app they built on a multimodal large language model, collected 553 entries; a preliminary analysis of 60 entries found trust 3.75/5 and satisfaction 4.15/5, higher than the 2024 scores.

What happened

Ricardo Gonzalez Penuela, Jazmin Collins, Cynthia Bennett and Shiri Azenkot (Cornell Tech and Google) ran a two-week diary study. It had 16 blind and low-vision adults in the United States (five men, 11 women, aged 24 to 67). Recruitment was via National Federation of the Blind mailing lists. They used an iPhone scene-description app that the researchers built on Microsoft's Azure AI Vision image description application programming interface (API). It was chosen because it “exposes the latest deep learning algorithms”. Seeing AI also uses it. Participants submitted 354 entries (13 to 39 each). Researchers excluded 22 because of technical failures and 16 setup entries, leaving 316 for analysis. Figure 2 labels the application Eyevisor. Documented uses include P1 photographing glasses. She “was not sure if this was my husband's sunglasses or my old pair of reading glasses from 4 years ago”. She got an answer without calling him. P5 photographed a hotel bed to check that his roommate was not in it, and P15 photographed wine bottles in a shop to avoid touching them. Participants scored descriptions 2.76 out of 5 for satisfaction and 2.43 out of 4 for trust on average. The authors say that satisfaction did not track accuracy because users inferred information from partially correct descriptions. The paper appeared at CHI 2024 (doi 10.1145/3613904.3642211). The arXiv version was submitted on 22 March 2024. The study dates are not reported. The CHI EA 2025 follow-up used a custom app, VisionPal, and documented failures such as hallucinated braille labels.

Details

AI system

Inputs and outputs

Participants submitted iPhone photographs and received scene descriptions generated by Azure AI Vision. The app did not read text.

Autonomy

Users decided what to photograph and interpreted the returned descriptions alongside other tools that they could use.

Workflow

An iPhone app sending photos to the Azure AI Vision image description API. Blind and low-vision users photographing objects they did not want to touch or ask others about. A minimum of one photo per day for two weeks with a short questionnaire per entry.

Human oversight

Participants chose what to photograph and judged each description themselves in a short questionnaire per entry. They were allowed to use other tools during the study. [1] [2]

Benefits

Reach

Sixteen blind and low-vision adults took part and submitted 354 entries, of which 316 were analyzed. The entries are repeated uses by the same 16 people. [1] [2]

Duration

Sixteen participants used the app for two weeks. They submitted 354 diary entries, of which 316 remained after technical failures and setup entries were excluded.

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

The description is accurate enough for the user to infer what they need. Users sometimes drew answers from partially correct output. The task does not require reading text, which the app could not do.

Replication attempts

2 follow-up entries. Benefit recurred: 2.

Evidence

Original evidence

The repeated arXiv and ACM entries are versions of one CHI 2024 work. The 2025 VisionPal paper is a separate follow-up, not confirmation of this app’s performance. No deployed commercial availability follows from this research app. [1]
