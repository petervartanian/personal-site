AI detects two handwriting groups in the Great Isaiah Scroll

Groningen's AI writer-identification method split the Great Isaiah Scroll's 54 columns into two groups, which its authors attribute to two scribes; researchers in Norway questioned it in 2025.

What happened

On 21 April 2021 Mladen Popović, Maruf Dhali and Lambert Schomaker of the University of Groningen published an artificial-intelligence analysis in PLOS ONE. It examined the handwriting of the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaa) from the Dead Sea Scrolls. They used pattern recognition on digitized images of the scroll's 54 columns. The columns fell into two statistically distinct groups, with a transition in columns 27–29. They concluded that two main scribes with different writing patterns were responsible for the scroll. Most scholars had argued or assumed that one scribe copied the whole manuscript. Some earlier scholars had suggested a change after column 27 on other grounds, and the AI analysis supplied quantitative evidence. The authors note that a change of pen or writing conditions could contribute to the difference. The same team developed the method and wrote the finding, and acceptance rests on the journal's peer review. The same day Peter Phillips of Durham University wrote in The Conversation that a change of scribe is the most likely conclusion. In June 2025 Tabita Tobing and Patrick Bours of the Norwegian University of Science and Technology retraced a column-based method on the same images. They argue that the 2021 analysis was framed to validate a two-scribe hypothesis. They found that the two-scribe split separates letters better than other groupings, with only moderate scores. They judge that hinge features alone cannot attribute scribes reliably.

Details

AI system

Model

BiNet.

Role

The software analyzed handwriting features and separated the columns into two groups.

Inputs and outputs

The analysis separated ink from the background of digitized scroll images and compared handwriting features across all 54 columns.

Workflow

High-resolution digital images of all 54 columns. Neural-network separation of ink from background. Textural and allographic features compared statistically across column groups.

Human oversight

The researchers interpreted the statistical division between column groups as evidence for two scribes, drawing on manuscript scholarship as well as the image analysis.
