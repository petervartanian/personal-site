Exomiser ranks gene variants and helps diagnose rare disease in England

In an English National Health Service pilot, Exomiser ranked candidate variants for 2,183 families; 25% of probands got a genetic diagnosis, and a quarter of diagnoses were immediately actionable.

  • 4 a person

    The authors report named proband outcomes. A diagnosis of CTPS1 deficiency in a 10-year-old girl, admitted to intensive care with life-threatening chicken pox after a seven-year diagnostic odyssey, enabled a curative bone marrow transplantation. A diagnosis of transcobalamin II deficiency, made after a male neonate died at four months with no diagnosis, let his younger brother start weekly hydroxocobalamin injections that prevent metabolic decompensation. The authors do not say whether Exomiser or the applied panels found either variant, so the depth rests on the pilot's reported outcomes without an attribution to the software.

  • 2 an organization

    The virtual panel pipeline alone made 322 of the 490 diagnoses that came from single-nucleotide variants or indels, and the panels together with Exomiser's top five candidates made 92% of them. That is a measurable gain in what the diagnostic pipeline could find.

  • Not rated society

    A 25% diagnostic yield across 2,183 families is reported for a national pilot. No change across the population is documented.

The system’s part
Exomiser scored and ranked candidate variants against each proband's phenotype. The pilot reports that diagnoses were detected in 77% of top-ranked candidate variants and that panels and Exomiser's top five were complementary. Clinical confirmation and reporting were performed by NHS diagnostic laboratories and clinicians, not by the software. Neither cited source says who developed Exomiser. The pilot's prioritization analysis was led by Damian Smedley's team at Queen Mary University of London and Genomics England, who are among the paper's authors. An independent evaluation is cited alongside it.
Limits
The pilot's diagnostic yield and clinical actionability apply to the combined workflow of sequencing, panels, prioritization and clinical review. They cannot all be attributed to Exomiser. The authors do not say whether Exomiser or the applied panels found the variants in the two patient cases that they describe. Immediate ramifications for clinical decisions do not establish improved health or survival across the pilot.
Costs and harms

Later

Brief from Peter

From May 2016 through April 2019, a national rare-disease pilot of the 100,000 Genomes Project returned results to the Genomic Medicine Centers of the English National Health Service. Across 2,183 families, genetic diagnoses were found in 25% of probands, and clinicians reported that a quarter of the diagnoses could be acted on at once. Exomiser ranked candidate variants against each patient's phenotype. Of the 490 diagnoses based on single-nucleotide variants or indels, 88% were among Exomiser's top five candidates, and 92% were made with the applied gene panels or those candidates.1 The pilot's authors report that one diagnosis enabled a curative bone marrow transplantation for a 10-year-old girl and that another let the younger brother of a baby who had died undiagnosed start weekly injections. They do not say whether Exomiser or the applied panels flagged either variant, so neither outcome can be credited to the software. In a later reanalysis across the full project, Exomiser ranked candidate variants again, and after manual interpretation of the ranked candidates, diagnoses were found for 463 of 24,015 patients whose cases the earlier analysis had left unsolved. In a separate deployment in Hong Kong, Exomiser ranked 67% of 132 diagnoses first.2

Details

AI system

Application developer

Not named in the cited sources; the pilot's phenotype-based prioritization analysis was led by Damian Smedley's team at Queen Mary University of London and Genomics England.

Inputs and outputs

Exomiser combined candidate genetic variants with structured patient phenotypes to rank possible diagnoses alongside applied gene panels.

Workflow

Genome sequencing with family data across a national pilot. Structured phenotype terms of adequate quality. Exomiser ranking used alongside applied gene panels, with clinical review before reporting.

Human oversight

NHS diagnostic laboratories and clinicians reviewed and confirmed candidate findings before reporting a genetic diagnosis or changing care.

Benefits

Reach

The pilot authors report 490 diagnoses from single-nucleotide variants or indels among 2,183 families. Clinicians reported that 134 of 533 genetic diagnoses were of immediate clinical actionability. In the 2024 reanalysis, the authors report diagnoses for 463 more patients. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Duration

The pilot returned results from May 2016 through April 2019. In December 2024 Exomiser's developers and a Genomics England scientist reported that a fresh run across the full project found diagnoses for 463 of 24,015 unsolved patients. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Genome or exome sequencing with family data where available. Structured HPO phenotype terms of adequate quality. Clinical review of ranked candidates before reporting.
