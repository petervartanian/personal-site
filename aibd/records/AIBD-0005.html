Brain implant decodes a paralyzed man's attempted speech into sentences

A speech neuroprosthesis built at the University of California, San Francisco, used deep-learning models to decode a paralyzed man's attempted speech at a median 15.2 words per minute.

  • 2 a person

    One identifiable person gained the first decoding of his own full words, about three times faster than his pointer typing. The decoding was limited to a 50-word vocabulary in research sessions.

Deep-learning models detected attempted speech in 98% of attempts and classified words. A language model with Viterbi decoding assembled sentences. The output depended entirely on these models. Funding included a research contract under Facebook's Sponsored Academic Research Agreement. The paper states that no study hardware or data were transferred to any sponsor and that author disclosure forms are available at NEJM.org.

What happened

A man had anarthria and severe spastic quadriparesis from an extensive pontine stroke at age 20. He is known in the press by the pseudonym Pancho. Before the study he typed about five words per minute with a head-controlled pointer. In February 2019, at the University of California, San Francisco, the 36-year-old man received a 128-electrode subdural electrocorticography array over sensorimotor cortex in the BRAVO trial (NCT03698149). Data collection began in April 2019. There were 50 sessions over 81 weeks at the participant's residence or a nearby office. Deep-learning models detected his attempted speech and classified words from a 50-word vocabulary. A natural-language model and a Viterbi decoder assembled sentences. On 15 July 2021 Moses and colleagues reported in the New England Journal of Medicine that he produced sentences at a median 15.2 words per minute with a median word error rate of 25.6%. The paper does not date the session in which this sentence decoding was achieved. In a letter to the journal on 13 October 2021, Michael J. Brenner of the University of Michigan Medical School asked whether the participant had anarthria or profound dysarthria, because his words "I am" are intelligible in the study video. On 8 November 2022 the same team reported that the participant spelled sentences by silent speech attempts from a vocabulary of 1,152 words, with stable signals beyond 128 weeks. The authors state that clinical and regulatory guidelines on the implanted connector kept him from using the system on his own.

Details

AI system

Model

Deep-learning models detect speech and classify words. A language model and a Viterbi decoder assemble the output.

Role

The models detected attempted speech, classified words, and assembled sentences.

Configuration

The system used a 128-electrode array with 4 mm spacing and a vocabulary of 50 words.

Inputs and outputs

The implanted cortical array recorded attempted speech. Deep-learning classifiers selected words from a 50-word vocabulary, and a language model assembled them into sentences.

Workflow

Preserved speech-motor cortical pathways more than 15 years after loss of speech. High-density ECoG array over sensorimotor cortex. Deep-learning models trained on 22 hours of attempted-speech data plus language-model decoding.

Human oversight

The system ran only in research sessions at the participant's residence or a nearby office. The authors state that clinical and regulatory guidelines on the implanted connector kept him from using the system on his own. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Benefits

Reach

One person benefited: the study's single participant, a man enrolled in the BRAVO trial at the University of California, San Francisco. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Duration

The 2021 result comes from 50 sessions over 81 weeks. On 8 November 2022 the same team reported that the cortical signals stayed stable beyond 128 weeks after implantation and that the participant spelled sentences from a vocabulary of 1,152 words. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Participant can attempt speech and retains intact cognition. Implant remains stable with adequate signal quality. Accumulated training data maintained over time.

Replication attempts

4 follow-up entries. Benefit recurred: 4.

Availability

The implanted system was available through the BRAVO research study. The participant could not use it independently for daily activities because staff had to handle the connector through his skin.
