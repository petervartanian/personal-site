People with severe paralysis regained control of a computer at home

Two people with ALS regained control of a computer at home using Stentrode and eye tracking.

ⓘ

AI system

System

Stentrode, with a custom decoder and Tobii Dynavox software. [1]

Model

A support-vector machine with a radial-basis kernel for participant 1, and a manually tuned threshold classifier for participant 2. [1]

Release

No software release or trained-weight identifier is reported. NCT03834857 identifies the trial, not the model. [1]

Provider

Synchron supplied Stentrode. The research team developed the decoding method, and Tobii Dynavox supplied eye tracking and computer-access software. [1]

Role

The decoder generated clicks from attempted movements. Eye tracking moved the cursor. [1]

Configuration

The first participant used a trained SVM. The second used a threshold that changed during typing. Both produced short and held clicks. [1]

Inputs and outputs

Electrical signals recorded from a cerebral vein were converted into no click, a short click, or a held click. [1]

Autonomy

Participants controlled selections through attempted movements. The system did not choose messages or purchases for them. [1]

Workflow

A caregiver attached the receiver and opened the software. The participant then used eye movements and brain-generated clicks to operate a computer. [1]

Human oversight

Clinicians selected and implanted participants, monitored safety, and managed antiplatelet treatment. Researchers tuned decoders, and caregivers helped with setup at home. [1]

Relation to AI

Participant 1 used a support-vector machine trained on movement attempts. Participant 2 used a manually tuned threshold classifier. Both converted brain signals into clicks, while an eye tracker handled cursor movement. The measured benefit belonged to the combined system.

AI contribution

The learned decoder supplied the click channel for one of the first two participants. The other used a threshold rule. Eye tracking and commercial software supplied the rest of the interface. [1]

Benefits

Outcome identifiers

AIBD-0052-outcome-01 [1]

Benefit

Participants regained the ability to select items and perform everyday computer tasks at home. [1]

Category

Improved Accessibility

Status

Demonstrated in a small, uncontrolled clinical study. [1]

Beneficiary groups

People.

Beneficiaries

People with severe upper-limb paralysis who retained suitable brain activity and venous anatomy. [1]

Reach

Four unique implanted participants are described across both reports. The initial two are included in the later four. [1] [2]

Distribution and access

The four implanted participants were men of European ancestry. The small, selected group does not establish broad suitability. [1]

Duration

The original report covered 12 months for participant 1 and three months for participant 2. The later paper followed four implanted participants for 12 months each. [1]

Expected?

Yes

Costs and harms

Implantation required anesthesia and antiplatelet treatment. One participant fainted briefly after surgery. The later four-person report recorded eight mild device effects. [1]

Measurement

Studies

NCT03834857 [1]

Study design

A prospective, open-label feasibility study without a control group. The later report includes the first two participants. [1]

Measurement

Researchers measured typing and selection accuracy, observed everyday computer tasks, recorded adverse events, and checked vessel patency and device position. [1]

Setting and sample

Two implanted participants appeared in the first report. The later study enrolled five people, implanted four, and excluded one because of venous anatomy. [1]

Measures

Correct characters per minute, selection accuracy, completion of everyday tasks, serious device-related events, vessel occlusion, and migration. [1]

Comparison

There was no concurrent control. The comparison with the Utrecht interface involved different participants and protocols. [1]

Results

The first two participants typed 13.81 and 20.10 correct characters per minute, with selection accuracy above 92%. [1] [2]

Additional value from AI

The study demonstrates use of the combined implant, decoder, eye tracker, and software. It does not isolate the benefit of machine learning. [1]

Limitations

The cohort was small and selected, and decoders differed between participants. Without a control group, superiority over other assistive systems cannot be established. [1]

Denominators

The first participants began home use without research staff on days 86 and 71 after implantation. Caregiver assistance with setup remained necessary. [1]

Materials for replication

The 2020 supplement gives participant-specific filters, training splits, and click rules. It does not publish exact trained weights or a software release. [1]

Synchron and University of Melbourne researchers developed the interface. The Royal Melbourne Hospital implanted and followed participants, who used it at home. [1]

Follow-up

Conditions for replication

Suitable anatomy, preserved motor signals, training, caregiver setup, and continuing clinical care were required. [1]

Replication attempts

The later four-person report includes the original two participants. [2]

Later findings

The four-person follow-up found no serious device-related events over 12 months. Eight mild device effects occurred, and one participant later demonstrated texting without eye tracking. [2]

Availability

The reports concern an investigational device. They do not establish routine commercial access at the time of the study. [1]

Institutional decision

Australian investigators tested the safety and feasibility of an endovascular brain–computer interface. [1] [2]

Grounds

The study received hospital ethics approvals and authorization under Australia’s Clinical Trial Notification scheme. [1] [2]

Action and result

Investigators selected participants, implanted the device, and monitored home use and safety. [1] [2]

Success criterion

The primary safety outcome was a device-related serious event causing death or permanent disability within 12 months. [1] [2]
