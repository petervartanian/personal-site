What happened

Two people with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and flaccid upper-limb paralysis received the Stentrode at the Royal Melbourne Hospital. The Stentrode is an electrode array delivered via the jugular vein into the superior sagittal sinus. The first-in-human study is in the Journal of NeuroInterventional Surgery (published online 28 October 2020). Participant 1 was implanted in mid-2019 and participant 2 in early 2020. The paper gives no exact dates, so its online publication date is used as the date observed. Machine-learning-assisted training mapped attempted-movement electrocorticography to mouse clicks, combined with an eye-tracker for cursor navigation. Unsupervised home use then began on day 86 for participant 1 and day 71 for participant 2. Click accuracies were 92.63% and 93.18%, and they typed 13.81 and 20.10 correct characters per minute.

They independently did text messaging, online shopping and managing finances. The hospital's statement of 29 October 2020 confirms these activities.

What it was built to do. Restore digital device control for people with severe paralysis without open-brain surgery

The system's part

Per the paper, a machine-learning-assisted decoder (binary SVM or threshold classifier on spectral power) detected attempted movements and generated click commands. Cursor navigation came from an eye-tracker, so the AI contribution is the click selection channel, which the authors report with 92–93% accuracy.

Replication

What produced it. Endovascular electrode array recording motor-cortex ECoG from the superior sagittal sinus. Machine-learning-assisted training of a movement-versus-rest click decoder. Pairing of BCI clicks with eye-tracker cursor navigation on Windows 10.

What must hold again. Preserved motor cortex activity and suitable venous anatomy. Stable signal bandwidth over months without migration or occlusion. Patient able to use an eye-tracker for navigation.
